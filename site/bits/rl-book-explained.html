Sutton & Barto, explained — chapters 1 to 5 in plain words.

rl sutton-barto textbook mdp monte-carlo

Companion notes for Sutton & Barto — Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction (2nd ed., MIT Press), the course textbook (T1). Chapters 1–5 cover the entire mid-sem; here each is summarized in easy language, mapped to the lectures. Read alongside the slides explained.

Chapter 1 — The RL problem

maps to CS1

The opening chapter frames RL as learning what to do — mapping situations to actions — to maximize a reward signal. The learner is not told which actions to take but must discover which yield the most reward by trying them. Two features distinguish RL: trial-and-error search and delayed reward. The chapter introduces the agent, environment, and the four elements — policy, reward signal, value function, model — and stresses the central tension of exploration vs exploitation: to get reward you exploit what you know, but to find better actions you must explore. Tic-tac-toe is the worked example showing how value estimates of positions improve through self-play.

Take-away. RL = goal-directed learning from interaction. Reward defines the goal; value estimates capture the long run; policy is what you learn; exploration is unavoidable.

Chapter 2 — Multi-armed bandits

maps to CS2–CS3

The bandit chapter studies the evaluative feedback at the heart of RL without the complication of states. It defines the action value q*(a) = E[R\|A=a], the sample-average estimate, and the incremental update NewEstimate ← OldEstimate + StepSize·(Target − OldEstimate) — a formula that recurs throughout the book. It develops ε-greedy action selection, shows on the 10-armed testbed that a little exploration beats pure greed, handles non-stationary problems with a constant step size, and introduces optimistic initial values, upper-confidence-bound (UCB) selection, and gradient bandit algorithms. The unifying message: how you balance exploration and exploitation determines performance.

Take-away. Estimate action values by averaging rewards; update incrementally; explore deliberately (ε-greedy / optimistic init / UCB). Constant α for changing worlds.

Chapter 3 — Finite Markov decision processes

maps to CS3–CS5

This is the theoretical core. It formalizes the agent–environment interface, defines the MDP via the dynamics p(s′,r\|s,a), and states the Markov property. It presents the reward hypothesis (goals = maximizing expected cumulative reward) with the warning to reward the what not the how, the recycling-robot example, and the distinction between episodic and continuing tasks with the discounted return G_t = Σγ^k R. It defines policies and the value functions v_π and q_π, derives the Bellman expectation equations, and finally the optimal value functions and the Bellman optimality equations whose solution is the optimal policy.

Take-away. The MDP is the formal model; the Bellman equations relate value now to value next; solving Bellman optimality gives the best policy. Everything later approximates this.

Chapter 4 — Dynamic programming

maps to CS4–CS6

Given a perfect model of the MDP, DP computes optimal policies. The chapter develops iterative policy evaluation (sweep the Bellman expectation backup to get v_π), policy improvement (act greedily w.r.t. v_π; the improvement theorem guarantees no worse), and combines them into policy iteration. It then derives value iteration (one Bellman optimality backup per sweep) and introduces asynchronous DP and the umbrella idea of Generalized Policy Iteration (GPI) — evaluation and improvement interacting until both are consistent with the optimal policy. The chapter is honest about DP's curse of dimensionality and its need for a model — the very limits the rest of the book removes.

Take-away. With a model, iterate Bellman backups: policy iteration and value iteration both reach the optimum. GPI is the pattern; needing the full model + sweeps is the limitation.

Chapter 5 — Monte Carlo methods

maps to CS6–CS7

Monte Carlo methods drop the model and learn from sampled episodes — averaging the actual returns that follow a state (or state–action pair). The chapter covers first-visit and every-visit MC prediction, why control needs action values, Monte Carlo control with exploring starts, and the two practical exploration strategies — on-policy (ε-soft) methods and off-policy methods that learn a target policy from a behaviour policy via importance sampling. It is the first set of methods that learn optimal behaviour purely from experience, with no knowledge of the environment's dynamics.

Take-away. MC = average real returns from episodes; model-free, episodic. Ensure all (s,a) are seen via exploring starts, on-policy ε-soft, or off-policy importance sampling. The doorway to TD learning (Ch 6, post-mid-sem).
