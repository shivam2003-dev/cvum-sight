// what this page is

The picture behind every formula on the cheatsheet — why it is true and when to reach for it — plus a "how to solve" recipe for each GATE question shape with fully worked examples. Read this once with paper and pen; after that the cheatsheet is all you need. Probability & Statistics is the single heaviest-scoring slice of the DA paper, and almost every question reduces to one of the patterns below.

Probability is just careful counting under uncertainty. Statistics is reasoning backwards from a sample to the process that made it. Master the handful of mental pictures here and the formulas stop being things to memorize — they become things you can re-derive in the exam hall when your memory blanks.

01 Counting — the foundation

Every "probability that…" question with equally-likely outcomes is secretly favourable ÷ total. Both numbers are counts, so counting is where the marks start.

key ideaAsk one question: does order matter? Arranging people in chairs → order matters → permutation. Picking a committee → order doesn't → combination. That single decision picks your formula.
  • Permutation nPr = n!/(n−r)! — number of ordered arrangements of r out of n.
  • Combination nCr = n!/(r!(n−r)!) — number of unordered selections. It is nPr with the r! internal orderings divided out.
  • With repetition nr — r slots, each independently chosen from n (PINs, passwords).

AND vs OR. Stages done together (first this and then that) → multiply. Mutually exclusive alternatives (this or that) → add.

How to solve a counting question

  1. Write the total number of outcomes (denominator).
  2. Decide order-matters → permutation, else combination.
  3. Split into AND-stages (multiply) and OR-cases (add).
  4. Count favourable outcomes the same way; divide.
worked5 books, arrange 3 on a shelf → order matters → 5P3 = 60. Choose 3 to donate → order doesn't → 5C3 = 10.

02 Probability axioms & the basic rules

All of probability rests on three rules: every event has probability between 0 and 1; the whole sample space has probability 1; and probabilities of disjoint events add. Everything else is a consequence.

Inclusion–exclusion: adding P(A)+P(B) double-counts the overlap, so subtract it once.

  • P(Ac) = 1 − P(A) — complement. If "at least one" is hard, count "none" and subtract.
  • P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A∩B) — inclusion–exclusion; the overlap is removed because adding counted it twice.
the catchThe "at least one" trap. P(at least one success) is almost always fastest as 1 − P(none). Trying to add up "exactly 1 + exactly 2 + …" wastes minutes and invites arithmetic slips.

03 Conditional probability & independence

think of it likeConditioning on B means shrinking your universe to only the worlds where B happened, then asking what fraction of that smaller world also has A. That is exactly P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B) — you renormalize by the new, smaller total.
  • Chain rule P(A∩B) = P(A|B)·P(B) — the workhorse for "and then" sequences.
  • Independent means knowing B tells you nothing about A: P(A|B)=P(A), equivalently P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B).
  • Mutually exclusive means they can't both happen: P(A∩B)=0.
the #1 trapMutually exclusive is NOT independent. If two events with nonzero probability can't co-occur, then learning one happened tells you the other definitely didn't — that is maximal dependence, not independence. GATE tests this exact confusion almost every year.

04 Bayes & total probability — the most-tested topic

Bayes reverses a conditional. You know P(test positive | disease); you want P(disease | test positive). The bridge is the law of total probability, which builds the denominator by summing over every way the evidence could occur.

  • Total probability P(B) = Σ P(B|Ai)·P(Ai) over a partition A₁…An.
  • Bayes P(Aj|B) = P(B|Aj)P(Aj) / P(B) = posterior = (likelihood × prior) ÷ evidence.

How to solve any Bayes question — the tree method (fewest errors)

  1. Draw a branch for each prior cause Ai; label with P(Ai).
  2. From each, branch to the evidence B; label with P(B|Ai).
  3. Multiply along each path to B → that path's joint probability.
  4. Posterior of one cause = its path ÷ sum of all paths reaching B.
workedDisease in 1% of people. Test: 99% sensitive, 5% false positive. You test positive — chance you're sick?
Sick path: 0.01×0.99 = 0.0099. Healthy-but-positive path: 0.99×0.05 = 0.0495.
P(sick | positive) = 0.0099 / (0.0099+0.0495) = 0.0099/0.0594 ≈ 16.7%. Counter-intuitively low — because healthy people vastly outnumber sick ones, the false positives swamp the true ones. This "base-rate" surprise is the classic exam hook.
the catchForgetting the denominator. The posterior is never just likelihood×prior — you must divide by the total probability of the evidence across all causes.

05 Random variables — PMF, PDF, CDF

A random variable is just a number attached to each outcome (dice → its face). Two flavours:

  • Discrete — countable values. Described by a PMF p(x)=P(X=x), with Σ p(x)=1.
  • Continuous — values on a range. Described by a PDF f(x), with ∫ f(x)dx = 1. Here P(X=x)=0 for any single point — only intervals have probability (it's an area).
the catchFor a continuous variable, f(x) can be greater than 1 — it's a density, not a probability. Only the area under it must total 1. And because single points have zero probability, ≤ vs < never changes a continuous answer (it does matter for discrete).

The CDF F(x)=P(X≤x) works for both; it is non-decreasing from 0 to 1, and for continuous variables f = F′.

How to solve "find the constant k" / "find the median"

  1. Constant: set ∫f(x)dx (or Σp(x)) over the whole support = 1, solve for k.
  2. P(a<X<b): integrate f from a to b (or sum the PMF).
  3. Median m: solve F(m)=0.5.

06 Expectation & variance — the workhorse

think of it likeThe expectation E[X] is the balance point of the distribution — the centre of mass. Variance is the average spread around it: how far, squared, the outcomes typically sit from the mean.
  • E[X] = Σ x·p(x) or ∫ x·f(x)dx.
  • Variance — use this form 90% of the time: Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])². Compute E[X²] with x², then subtract the mean squared.

The linearity rules that save you every time:

  • E[aX+b] = aE[X]+b
  • E[X+Y] = E[X]+E[Y] — always, even if X and Y are dependent. This is the single most useful fact in the subject.
  • Var(aX+b) = a²Var(X) — the shift b drops out; the scale a comes out squared.
  • Var(X+Y) = Var(X)+Var(Y) only if independent; otherwise add 2·Cov(X,Y).
the catchExpectation always adds; variance only adds for independent variables. Mixing these up is a guaranteed lost mark.
worked — linearity beats brute forceRoll two dice, sum S. Instead of the 36-cell table: E[one die]=3.5, so E[S]=3.5+3.5=7 by linearity. Var[one die]=35/12, dice independent, so Var[S]=70/12≈5.83. Seconds, not minutes.

Covariance & correlation. Cov(X,Y)=E[XY]−E[X]E[Y]; correlation ρ = Cov/(σXσY) with −1≤ρ≤1.

the catchCov=0 does NOT imply independent. Correlation only detects linear association. Y=X² with X symmetric about 0 has zero covariance yet is fully dependent. Independence ⇒ Cov=0, but never the reverse.

07 The named discrete distributions

Recognize the story, recall E and Var instantly — these are the cheapest marks in the paper.

  • Bernoulli(p) — one trial, success/failure. E=p, Var=p(1−p).
  • Binomial(n,p) — count of successes in n independent trials. P(X=k)=nCk pk(1−p)n−k, E=np, Var=np(1−p).
  • Poisson(λ) — count of rare events in a fixed interval. P(X=k)=e−λλk/k!, E=Var=λ. It's the limit of Binomial when n→∞, p→0, np=λ.
  • Geometric(p) — trials until the first success. E=1/p; memoryless.

How to pick the distribution

  1. Fixed number of independent yes/no trials, count successes → Binomial.
  2. Events at a constant average rate, count per interval → Poisson.
  3. "How many trials until the first success" → Geometric.
workedCalls arrive at 3/hour (Poisson). P(exactly 5 in an hour)=e−3·35/5! = e−3·243/120 ≈ 0.101.

08 The named continuous distributions

  • Uniform(a,b) — flat density 1/(b−a). E=(a+b)/2, Var=(b−a)²/12.
  • Exponential(λ) — waiting time for the next Poisson event. f(x)=λe−λx, E=1/λ, Var=1/λ². Memoryless: P(X>s+t | X>s)=P(X>t) — the past doesn't age it.
  • Normal(μ,σ²) — the bell curve; the limiting shape of sums (see CLT). The 68–95–99.7 rule: that much mass lies within 1, 2, 3 standard deviations of μ.
key skill — standardizeAny normal question becomes a standard-normal table lookup via Z = (X−μ)/σ. Then use Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z) for the left tail. P(a<X<b)=Φ(zb)−Φ(za).
workedX~N(100,15²). P(X<115)? z=(115−100)/15=1 → Φ(1)≈0.841 → ≈84%. P(85<X<115)=Φ(1)−Φ(−1)=0.841−0.159=0.682 (the 68% rule).

09 Descriptive statistics

Summaries of data you actually hold (a sample), as opposed to the underlying process.

  • Mean — average; sensitive to outliers. Median — middle value; robust. Mode — most frequent.
  • Sample variance uses divide by (n−1), not n — "Bessel's correction" — because the sample mean already used up one degree of freedom, and dividing by n would underestimate the true variance.
the catchRight-skewed data (incomes): mean > median. Left-skewed: mean < median. The mean is pulled toward the long tail. GATE likes "which is larger" one-markers.

10 The Central Limit Theorem — why the bell curve is everywhere

key ideaWhatever the shape of the population, the distribution of the sample mean X̄ approaches Normal as n grows: X̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n). The standard error SE = σ/√n shrinks like 1/√n — quadruple the sample to halve the error.
the catchCLT is about the mean of a sample, not individual data points, and not the sum's raw spread. The √n in the denominator is the most-tested detail: to cut SE in half you need 4× the data, not 2×.

When σ is unknown and n is small, the standardized mean follows a t-distribution (fatter tails) with df=n−1. The χ²-distribution describes sums of squared normals — it powers variance tests and goodness-of-fit.

11 Confidence intervals

A range that, under repeated sampling, would contain the true parameter a stated fraction (e.g. 95%) of the time.

CI = X̄ ± z*·SE, with z*=1.96 for 95%, 2.58 for 99%. Use t* instead of z* when σ is unknown and n is small.

the catchA 95% CI does not mean "95% probability the true mean is in this interval" — the true mean is fixed. It means 95% of intervals built this way would capture it. Conceptual one-marker bait.

12 Hypothesis testing — one recipe for all of it

Every test answers: "is the data far enough from H₀ to reject it?" The machinery is identical; only the test statistic changes.

The universal test recipe

  1. State H₀ (status quo) and H₁ (the claim).
  2. Compute statistic = (estimate − null value) / SE.
  3. Pick z, t, or χ² depending on what's known (below).
  4. Compare |statistic| to the critical value (or p-value to α).
  5. |stat| > critical (or p < α) → reject H₀. Mind one-tail vs two-tail.
  • z-test z=(X̄−μ₀)/(σ/√n) — σ known, or large n.
  • t-test t=(X̄−μ₀)/(s/√n), df=n−1 — σ unknown, small n.
  • χ² χ²=Σ(Oi−Ei)²/Ei — goodness-of-fit, independence, variance.
the catchType I (α) = false alarm (reject a true H₀). Type II (β) = miss (fail to reject a false H₀). You can't shrink both at once for fixed n. Power = 1−β. Critical z*: 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.33 (98%, one-tail), 2.58 (99%).

14 The high-frequency exam traps

memorize these — they repeat every year

  • Mutually exclusive ≠ independent — exclusive events with nonzero prob are maximally dependent.
  • Bayes denominator — always divide by total probability of the evidence.
  • Continuous P(X=x)=0, PDF can exceed 1, ≤ vs < only matters for discrete.
  • Cov=0 ⇏ independent — correlation sees linear relations only.
  • Var adds only when independent; E always adds.
  • CLT is the sample mean; SE=σ/√n shrinks like 1/√n.
  • 95% CI is about the procedure, not "95% chance the mean is here."
  • Type I=false alarm, Type II=miss; can't minimize both.

15 FAQ

When is it Binomial vs Poisson?

Binomial: a fixed, known number of independent trials, you count successes. Poisson: no fixed n, events occur at a constant average rate over an interval, you count how many happened. If n is large and p small with np moderate, Binomial ≈ Poisson(λ=np).

How do I know whether to use z or t?

Population σ known (or n large, say ≥30) → z. σ unknown and n small → t with df=n−1. In GATE, if they hand you a population standard deviation, use z; if only a sample standard deviation s, use t.

I always blank on variance — what's the safest method?

Use Var(X)=E[X²]−(E[X])² every time. Compute E[X] and E[X²] separately (E[X²] uses x²·p(x) or x²·f(x)), then subtract. It avoids the messier Σ(x−μ)² form.

What's the fastest way to handle "at least one"?

Complement: P(at least one)=1−P(none). For n independent trials each with success p, that's 1−(1−p)n.

  • Probability = careful counting; statistics = reasoning backward from a sample.
  • Your two most-used tools: Var=E[X²]−(E[X])² and Bayes by tree.
  • One inference recipe — (estimate−null)/SE vs critical — covers z, t, χ².
  • Memorize the E/Var table and win the conceptual one-markers (exclusive vs independent, Cov vs independence, CI meaning). That alone is a large, reliable score.
