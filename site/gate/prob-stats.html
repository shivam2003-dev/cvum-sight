Probability & Statistics.

This is the one-stop Probability & Statistics page for GATE DA. Every syllabus topic, explained in plain language with the intuition first, then the exact formula, then a "how to solve" recipe and a worked example, plus the traps that cost marks. Read it fully and you can solve any GATE prob/stats question. This is the single heaviest-scoring area in DA — master it and you're already most of the way to a top rank.

Most people fear probability because they try to memorize formulas without the picture. That's backwards. Every formula here comes from one or two simple ideas — counting outcomes, and updating beliefs when you learn something. Get those two pictures and the formulas stop being magic. We build from counting up to hypothesis tests, and at every step the question is the same: what is the recipe to solve the exam question?

00 The exam lens

GATE DA prob/stats questions come in a few predictable shapes: a counting question (how many ways), a conditional/Bayes question (update a probability given info), an expectation/variance calculation, a "which distribution + plug in" question, and a test/CI question (z/t/chi-squared). Almost every problem is one of these. The skill is recognizing which shape in the first 10 seconds, then running its recipe. We'll name the recipe for each.

01 Counting — permutations & combinations

Probability of equally-likely outcomes is just favorable ÷ total, so counting is probability. Two ideas only:

Permutation (order matters)

Arrange r out of n where sequence counts (podium: gold/silver/bronze). nPr = n!/(n−r)!

Combination (order doesn't)

Choose r out of n where only the group matters (a committee). nCr = n!/(r!(n−r)!)

think of it likePermutation = filling labelled seats (seat 1, seat 2…) — swapping people gives a new arrangement. Combination = picking a team — the same people are the same team no matter the order you named them. If you can rename the slots and it's "the same", it's a combination.
how to solve any counting question
  1. Does order matter? Yes → permutation. No → combination. (Test: would swapping two picks create a genuinely different outcome?)
  2. With or without repetition? Same item reusable (digits in a PIN) → use n^r or stars-and-bars; not reusable → nPr / nCr.
  3. Break into stages and use the multiplication rule (AND = multiply) and addition rule (OR of disjoint cases = add).
  4. Watch over-counting: divide by the number of equivalent orderings if you counted ordered but want unordered.
worked exampleFrom 6 people, form a committee of 3 with a designated chair.
→ Choose the chair: 6 ways. Choose 2 more from remaining 5 (order irrelevant): 5C2 = 10. Multiply (AND): 6 × 10 = 60. (Or: choose 3 = 20, then pick chair = 3 → 60. Same answer, sanity-checked.)

02 Axioms, sample space, events

The sample space S is the set of all outcomes; an event is a subset. The three axioms (everything else is derived): probabilities are ≥0, P(S)=1, and for disjoint events probabilities add. Key derived results you'll use constantly:

P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A)
P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B) // inclusion–exclusion
mutually exclusive: P(A ∩ B) = 0 → P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)
watch outMutually exclusive ≠ independent. Mutually exclusive means they can't both happen (P(A∩B)=0). Independent means one tells you nothing about the other (P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)). For events with nonzero probability, being mutually exclusive actually makes them dependent (if A happened, B definitely didn't). GATE loves this trap.

03 Conditional, joint & independence

Conditional probability P(A|B) = "probability of A given B happened" — you've shrunk the world to B.

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B)·P(B) = P(B|A)·P(A) // multiplication / chain rule
independent ⇔ P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B) ⇔ P(A|B)=P(A)
think of it likeConditioning on B is zooming the camera so only B fills the frame. P(A|B) is "what fraction of this zoomed-in world is also A". That's why you divide by P(B) — you're re-normalizing to the smaller world.

04 Bayes theorem — the belief updater

The single most tested idea. Bayes flips a conditional you know into the one you want:

P(A|B) = P(B|A)·P(A) / P(B)
with total probability: P(B) = Σᵢ P(B|Aᵢ)·P(Aᵢ)
how to solve any Bayes question
  1. Identify the partition of causes A₁,A₂,… (e.g. machine 1/2/3, disease/no-disease). Write their priors P(Aᵢ).
  2. Write the likelihoods P(B|Aᵢ) — the probability of the evidence under each cause.
  3. Compute the evidence P(B) = Σ P(B|Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) (total probability — the denominator).
  4. Plug in: posterior = (likelihood × prior) / evidence.
  5. Tree trick: draw a probability tree, multiply along branches, the target branch ÷ sum of all branches that produce B.
worked example (classic medical test)Disease prevalence 1% → P(D)=0.01. Test sensitivity P(+|D)=0.99, false positive P(+|Dᶜ)=0.05. You test positive — actual chance you're sick?
P(+) = 0.99·0.01 + 0.05·0.99 = 0.0099 + 0.0495 = 0.0594.
P(D|+) = 0.0099 / 0.0594 ≈ 0.167 → only ~17%! The low prior dominates. This counter-intuitive answer is a GATE favourite.
the catchThe base rate (prior) is everything, and your gut ignores it. A "99% accurate" test on a rare disease still gives mostly false positives, because there are far more healthy people to misclassify than sick people to catch. Always compute P(B) via total probability — never eyeball it. Skipping the denominator is the #1 Bayes mistake.

05 Random variables: PMF, PDF, CDF

A random variable X maps outcomes to numbers. Discrete X (countable values) → described by a PMF p(x)=P(X=x). Continuous X → described by a PDF f(x), where probability is area: P(a≤X≤b)=∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx. The CDF F(x)=P(X≤x) works for both and is the universal tool.

discrete: Σ p(x) = 1 · P(X=x)=p(x)
continuous: ∫ f(x)dx = 1 · P(X=x)=0 ! · f(x)=F′(x)
CDF: F(x)=P(X≤x), non-decreasing, 0→1
watch outFor continuous X, P(X = exact value) = 0 — probability lives in intervals (area), not points. So ≤ vs < doesn't change a continuous answer, but it absolutely does for discrete. And the PDF f(x) is not a probability — it can exceed 1; only its integral over a range is a probability.
how to solve "find the constant / find P(a<X<b)"
  1. To find an unknown constant in a PDF: set ∫ f(x)dx = 1 over the support and solve.
  2. For P(a<X<b): integrate the PDF from a to b, or compute F(b)−F(a).
  3. For "find the median": solve F(m)=0.5. For a percentile q: solve F(x)=q.

06 Expectation & variance

Expectation E[X] = the long-run average (the balance point of the distribution). Variance Var(X) = average squared distance from the mean (the spread); SD = √Var.

E[X] = Σ x·p(x) or ∫ x·f(x)dx
Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])² // the workhorse formula
E[aX+b]=aE[X]+b · Var(aX+b)=a²Var(X)
linearity: E[X+Y]=E[X]+E[Y] (ALWAYS, even if dependent)
if independent: Var(X+Y)=Var(X)+Var(Y), E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]
key ideaVar(X)=E[X²]−(E[X])² is the formula you'll use 90% of the time — compute E[X] and E[X²], subtract. And linearity of expectation always holds, dependence or not — it's the most powerful shortcut in the subject. Variance only adds for independent variables; otherwise you need the covariance term.

Conditional expectation/variance: E[X|Y] and Var(X|Y) are computed in the conditional (zoomed) world. Two big tools:

law of total expectation: E[X] = E[ E[X|Y] ]
law of total variance: Var(X)=E[Var(X|Y)] + Var(E[X|Y])

watch outIndependent ⇒ Cov=0, but Cov=0 ⇏ independent. Zero correlation only rules out a linear relationship; X and Y can be strongly dependent (e.g. Y=X²) with ρ=0. Correlation measures linear association only. GATE tests this distinction directly.

08 Discrete distributions — recognize & plug in

Bernoulli(p)

One trial, success/fail. E=p, Var=p(1−p).

Binomial(n,p)

Number of successes in n independent trials. P(X=k)=nCk·pᵏ(1−p)ⁿ⁻ᵏ. E=np, Var=np(1−p).

Poisson(λ)

Count of rare events in a fixed interval. P(X=k)=e⁻ᵏλᵏ/k!. E=Var=λ.

Discrete uniform

n equally likely values. E=(a+b)/2.

how to pick the discrete distribution
  1. "Number of successes in n fixed independent yes/no trials" → Binomial.
  2. "Count of events in a time/space interval, rare, average rate λ" → Poisson. (Binomial with large n, small p, np=λ → Poisson.)
  3. "Single yes/no" → Bernoulli. "All outcomes equally likely" → uniform.
  4. Then just plug k into the PMF or use E/Var formulas. Memorize the four E/Var pairs cold.

09 Continuous distributions

Uniform(a,b)

Flat density. f=1/(b−a). E=(a+b)/2, Var=(b−a)²/12.

Exponential(λ)

Waiting time for next event; memoryless. f=λe⁻ᵏˣ. E=1/λ, Var=1/λ².

Normal(μ,σ²)

The bell curve. Symmetric about μ; ~68/95/99.7% within 1/2/3σ.

Standard Normal Z

Normal with μ=0,σ=1. Standardize: Z=(X−μ)/σ, then use the Z-table.

key ideaThe memoryless property of the exponential: P(X>s+t | X>s)=P(X>t) — having waited s minutes tells you nothing about the remaining wait. Only the exponential (continuous) and geometric (discrete) have this. And standardizing Z=(X−μ)/σ turns any normal question into a standard-normal table lookup — that's the whole technique for normal problems.
how to solve a normal-distribution question
  1. Write μ and σ. Standardize the boundary: z=(x−μ)/σ.
  2. Translate to Φ (standard normal CDF): P(X≤x)=Φ(z). Use Φ(−z)=1−Φ(z) for symmetry.
  3. Read Φ(z) from the table; combine for ranges: P(a≤X≤b)=Φ(z_b)−Φ(z_a).

10 CLT & sampling distributions (t, chi-squared)

The Central Limit Theorem: the sample mean of any distribution (finite variance) becomes approximately normal as n grows. This is why the normal is everywhere.

sample mean X̄ ~ Normal(μ, σ²/n) approximately, large n
standard error SE = σ/√n (or s/√n)

Two distributions that appear when σ is unknown or for variance questions:

t-distribution

Like normal but heavier tails; used for means when population σ is unknown and n is small. Parameter: degrees of freedom (df=n−1). → t-test.

Chi-squared (χ²)

Sum of squared standard normals; used for variance and goodness-of-fit / independence tests. Parameter: df.

the catchThe CLT is about the sample mean, not individual data. Individual values keep their original distribution; only the average of many goes normal. Also: SE shrinks like 1/√n, not 1/n — to halve your uncertainty you need four times the data. Both points are routinely misstated and tested.

11 Confidence intervals

A CI is a range that, with stated confidence (say 95%), contains the true parameter. For a mean:

CI = X̄ ± (critical value)·SE
known σ → z*·σ/√n (95% → z*=1.96)
unknown σ, small n → t*·s/√n (df=n−1)
watch out"95% confidence" does not mean "95% probability the true mean is in this interval" — the true mean is fixed, the interval is random. It means 95% of such intervals (over repeated sampling) would capture it. GATE may test this interpretation in a one-mark conceptual question.

12 Hypothesis testing: z, t, chi-squared

The universal recipe — learn it once, applies to every test.

how to solve ANY hypothesis test
  1. State H₀ (null) and H₁ (alternative). H₀ is the "no effect / equals" claim.
  2. Pick the test & statistic: mean, σ known → z; mean, σ unknown/small n → t (df=n−1); variance or categorical fit/independence → χ².
  3. Compute the statistic: (estimate − null value) / SE.
  4. Compare to the critical value at significance α (or use the p-value): |stat| > critical → reject H₀. p < α → reject.
  5. State the conclusion in context. One-tailed vs two-tailed changes the critical value — read the wording.
z = (X̄ − μ₀)/(σ/√n) t = (X̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n)
χ² = Σ (Oᵢ − Eᵢ)²/Eᵢ // observed vs expected
watch outType I error = reject a true H₀ (false alarm), probability α. Type II = fail to reject a false H₀ (miss), probability β. Lowering α raises β — you can't shrink both without more data. A small p-value means "data is surprising under H₀", not "H₀ is false with that probability".

14 Top traps that cost marks

common catches & gotchas

  • Mutually exclusive vs independent — opposite ideas; exclusive events with nonzero prob are dependent.
  • Forgetting the Bayes denominator — always compute P(evidence) by total probability; don't eyeball.
  • Continuous P(X=x)=0 — and the PDF can exceed 1; only its integral is a probability.
  • Cov=0 ≠ independent — correlation catches only linear association.
  • Var adds only for independent — otherwise include 2Cov(X,Y); but E always adds.
  • CLT is about the mean, not individual data; SE shrinks like 1/√n.
  • ≤ vs < matters for discrete, not for continuous.
  • One-tailed vs two-tailed — read the wording; it changes the critical value and the answer.

15 Questions students actually ask

How much of DA does prob/stats cover?

Consistently one of the top-weighted areas (with Linear Algebra and ML). Treat it as non-negotiable: full marks here is the foundation of a top rank.

Do I need to memorize distribution formulas?

Yes — the PMF/PDF and the E/Var of Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson, uniform, exponential, normal should be instant recall. They're cheap marks and you won't have time to re-derive.

z-test or t-test — how do I decide fast?

Population σ known (or large n) → z. σ unknown and small n → t (df=n−1). In practice, "they gave you population σ" → z; "they gave you sample std s" → t.

What's the fastest way to do Bayes in the exam?

Draw the probability tree, multiply along the branch you want, divide by the sum of all branches that produce the evidence. It's the same as the formula but harder to mess up.

Is the normal table provided?

Standard values (like z*=1.96 for 95%) are commonly needed; memorize the key ones (1.645, 1.96, 2.33, 2.58) and the 68-95-99.7 rule so you're never stuck.

17 How to top this subject

  • Recipes over formulas. For each question shape (counting / Bayes / E-Var / distribution / test) you should run the recipe on autopilot. Drill until classification takes seconds.
  • Memorize the E/Var table of all named distributions and the Z key values — instant recall, zero derivation time.
  • Bayes by tree — practice 20 tree problems; it's the highest-frequency, highest-trap topic.
  • Do every PYQ in this subject; prob/stats questions repeat in pattern almost every year.
  • Conceptual one-markers (exclusive vs independent, Cov=0 vs independent, CI meaning, Type I/II) are free marks if you nail the definitions — don't lose them.
  • NAT accuracy: write the formula, plug numbers, double-check the arithmetic — most lost marks here are calculation slips, not concept gaps.
