1. How to use this reference

The earlier parts tell stories. This one is for the day you sit down with a Cost Explorer view and a coffee and need to know: what could I try on this line item, what could go wrong, and who has done it already? Find the service, read the row, and follow the cross-reference to the part that tells the full story.

Two cautions apply to everything below. First, prices quoted are approximate us-east-1 list prices as of September 2026; confirm current numbers before deciding. Second, every technique has a watch-out for a reason: each one is the residue of someone's near-miss or incident.

9. The maths, gathered in one place

Every calculation here was used by a case-study team. Do the arithmetic for your own numbers; the point of each formula is to turn an opinion into a number you can argue about.

9.1 Payback period

payback_months = one_off_cost / monthly_saving

Canva: $1.6M divided by $0.3M a month is about 5.3 months. Rewind: $103K divided by $16K a month is about 6.4 months. 37signals: roughly $700K of hardware against about $160K a month of reduced cloud spend, recouped within 2023. Anything under six months is usually an easy yes; above eighteen months needs a strategic reason.

9.4 NAT gateway versus gateway endpoint

A workload in private subnets pushing 10 TB a month to S3 through a NAT gateway pays about 10,240 GB times $0.045, roughly $461 a month in processing, plus about $33 per NAT gateway per month in hourly charges (times the number of AZs). Through an S3 gateway endpoint the same traffic costs nothing extra. For ECR image pulls, CloudWatch or STS, interface endpoints cost about $0.01 per hour per AZ plus $0.01 per GB, which beats NAT once volume is more than a few hundred GB a month.

9.5 The cost of one log line

A Lambda invoked 50 million times a month that logs a 20 KB event on every call writes roughly 950 GB a month. At around $0.50 per GB ingested, that is about $475 a month before storage, and storage keeps growing if the log group never expires. A 200-byte summary line instead writes about 9.5 GB, roughly $5 a month: the same 99% reduction Levels.fyi saw.

9.6 Cross-AZ chatter

Two services exchanging 50 TB a month across AZs pay about $0.01 per GB in each direction, around $1,000 a month. If zone-aware routing keeps 80% of that traffic local, the charge falls to roughly $200. The saving grows linearly with every new hop in a microservice call chain.

9.7 Sanity-checking a claimed saving

Levels.fyi cut RDS snapshot storage from 15.7 TB to 3 TB and reported about $1,200 a month in savings. At roughly $0.095 per GB-month for RDS backup storage beyond the free allowance, 12.7 TB is about $1,235 a month, which matches. Reproducing a claimed saving from list prices is a quick way to check that you understand where the money went.

9.8 Commitment sizing

  • Finish right-sizing, Graviton moves and scheduling first; commitments freeze the footprint.
  • Look at hourly eligible spend over the last 30-60 days and commit to roughly its trough, not its average.
  • Ladder purchases (for example quarterly) so commitments expire at different times and can track change.
  • Track coverage (discounted share of eligible usage) and utilisation (share of commitment used); aim high on utilisation before pushing coverage.
  • Prefer Compute Savings Plans and Database Savings Plans when your instance families or engines are still changing.

The pitfalls cluster into four families: order (commit before optimising), evidence (trusting graphs and fresh billing data), side effects (event floods, hairpins, churn) and scope (one-instance proofs of concept, lift-and-shift). Most guard rails are a habit rather than a tool: snapshot first, price the one-off, test across every zone, and keep a ticket to undo every emergency scale-up.

Which section of this catalogue should I start with?

Start with observability and storage: log retention, versioning, multipart-upload aborts and lifecycle rules are the lowest-risk, highest-certainty rows, and they appear in nearly every case study. Then networking (endpoints), then compute swaps, and commitments last.

Are these prices still correct?

They are approximate us-east-1 list prices as of September 2026 and change frequently. Use them to understand the shape of each trade-off and always confirm on the current AWS pricing pages.

How do I sanity-check a saving someone claims?

Reproduce it from list prices, as in the RDS backup example: 12.7 TB at about $0.095 per GB-month is roughly $1,235 a month, matching the reported $1,200. If the numbers do not reconcile, the scope or the assumption is different from what you think.

Takeaways

  • Use the catalogue per service. Find the line item, read the watch-out, then read the case.
  • Log retention, versioning, multipart aborts and lifecycle rules are the lowest-risk rows and appear everywhere.
  • The maths fits on one page: payback, S3 break-even, schedules, NAT versus endpoints, log lines, cross-AZ, sanity checks, commitment sizing.
  • Pitfalls cluster into order, evidence, side effects and scope.
  • Confirm prices before relying on any figure; the shape of the trade-off matters more than the third digit.
