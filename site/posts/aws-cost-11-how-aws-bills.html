How Every AWS Component Is Billed: Per Hour, Per GB, Per Request.

devops aws finops billing

Summary: every AWS line item uses one of seven meters: per hour, per GB moved, per GB-month stored, per request, per unit per month, a commitment, or a pass-through tax. Once you know which meters a component uses, you know what drives its cost and which lever to pull. This guide walks through every common component with Mumbai (ap-south-1) list prices, the usage type you will see in Cost Explorer, a formula, the traps, and the fix. The biggest lesson: in a typical networking-heavy account, close to half the bill is charged per hour, whether or not any traffic flows.

About the numbers. Rates are Mumbai (ap-south-1) on-demand list prices in USD as pulled from the AWS Price List API in October 2026, before Savings Plans, credits or private discounts, and before tax. AWS changes prices, so re-check the price list before any purchase decision. All worked examples use a hypothetical team with invented round numbers, chosen to show the arithmetic, not to describe any real company.

1. Why read a bill by meter, not by service

Open Cost Explorer grouped by service and you see names: EC2, VPC, Network Firewall, CloudWatch. That view tells you where money goes, but not why. Two lines of the same size can behave completely differently. One rises with traffic, one with the number of things that exist, one with how long data sits around, one with how often a script calls an API.

Think of AWS as a city utility company. Some things charge rent (you pay every hour the thing exists). Some charge a toll (you pay for every GB that drives through). Some charge storage (you pay for space by the month). Some charge per ticket (you pay per API call). Most real components combine two or three of these. A NAT gateway charges rent and a toll. A load balancer charges rent and a capacity fee. Once you can see the rent and the toll separately, cost work stops being guesswork.

3. Decoding a usage type

Cost Explorer and the Cost and Usage Report (CUR) name every meter with a usage type. Learn the shape and you can decode any line without opening a pricing page. The prefix is a region code, then the meter name.

APS3-Endpoint-Hour                 Mumbai, Network Firewall endpoint, billed in hours
APS3-Traffic-GB-Processed          Mumbai, Network Firewall, billed per GB inspected
APS3-NatGateway-Bytes              Mumbai, NAT gateway data processed
APS3-DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes   Mumbai, traffic between Availability Zones
APS3-EBS:VolumeUsage.gp3           Mumbai, gp3 disk, provisioned GB-month
USE1-APIRequest                    N. Virginia, Cost Explorer API call

APS3 is Mumbai (ap-south-1). Hyderabad (ap-south-2) has its own prefix, which is why a disaster-recovery region shows up as a separate set of lines. Global services such as Cost Explorer are priced in US East and carry a USE1 prefix wherever you call them from.

Four rules used everywhere in this guide

  • A month is 730 hours. AWS uses 730 hours to turn hourly rates into monthly ones. That is where the familiar $3.65 per month for a public IPv4 address comes from ($0.005 × 730).
  • Prices are list, before discounts. Savings Plans, credits and private pricing lower the effective rate. Tax is added on top.
  • Hourly resources bill while idle. A firewall with no traffic, a load balancer with no targets and an unattached disk all keep their meter running.
  • Data in is almost always free; data out and data sideways is not. The path a packet takes decides which per-GB meters it crosses.

5. What a typical bill looks like by meter

Take a hypothetical platform team running Kubernetes on EKS in Mumbai, with a firewall in front of every VPC, a few dozen load balancers and a disaster-recovery copy in Hyderabad. Suppose its monthly bill is $20,000. Grouped by meter instead of by service, a bill like that often looks roughly like this:

Billing modelWhat is in itHypothetical share
Per hour, always onEC2, firewall endpoints, load balancer hours, EKS clustersabout 45%
Per GB movedfirewall processing, cross-AZ transfer, NAT processingabout 15 to 20%
Per GB-month storedEBS volumes, S3about 12%
Logs, IPs, endpoints, misc.CloudWatch, public IPv4, VPC endpoints, capacity unitsabout 10%
Taxpass-throughabout 13 to 15%

The first row is the important one. Nearly half the money is decided by what exists, not by what is used. Traffic can fall to zero overnight and that half barely moves. That is why the fastest savings usually come from deleting or consolidating things, not from tuning them.

Reading the bill backwards: dollars to quantities

A useful trick is to divide each line by its list rate. The result tells you what is actually running, which is often a surprise.

"We have 50 load balancers?" is a common reaction. These are derived numbers, and they assume list price with no discount, but they turn an abstract bill into a list of things you can go and look at.

Part A. Hourly and per-GB networking

Networking is where bills quietly grow. Each component below has a fixed fee for existing plus a variable fee for traffic, and several of them sit on the same path.

AWS Network Firewall

Billed like a rented appliance. You pay for each firewall endpoint for every hour it exists, one endpoint per Availability Zone the firewall covers, and separately for every GB it inspects. Idle or busy, the endpoint fee does not change.

MeteredUnitMumbai rateUsage type
Endpointhour, per endpoint$0.395APS3-Endpoint-Hour
Traffic processedGB$0.065APS3-Traffic-GB-Processed
Traffic via Transit Gateway attachmentGB$0.065APS3-Traffic-Bytes-Processed-Transit-Gateway
Advanced inspection endpoint (TLS)hour$0.380APS3-Advanced-Inspection-Endpoint-Hour

Secondary endpointhour$0.158APS3-Secondary-Endpoint-Hour

Formula: monthly = (endpoints × $0.395 × 730) + (GB inspected × $0.065)

Worked example (hypothetical). A two-AZ firewall with zero traffic costs 2 × $0.395 × 730 = $577 a month before it inspects a single byte. A small dev VPC with such a firewall costs about $19 a day forever. Add 20 TB of inspected traffic and you add 20,480 × $0.065 = $1,331.

Watch for. The firewall is one of three charges on a typical outbound path (NAT hour, NAT GB, firewall GB). Traffic that does not need inspection, such as calls to S3, still pays $0.065/GB if it takes that route. Each extra AZ adds a whole endpoint, and a DR region that mirrors production doubles the fixed floor. Deleting a firewall changes the security model, so it is an architecture decision, not just a cost one.
Lever. Fewer endpoints per environment (one AZ for non-production VPCs where acceptable), a shared inspection VPC behind Transit Gateway instead of a firewall per VPC, and routing AWS-service traffic through free gateway endpoints so it never reaches the firewall.

NAT gateway

A managed NAT is billed for existing and for every GB it processes, in both directions. Because it is one per AZ, an environment with a NAT in each of two zones pays the hourly fee twice.

MeteredUnitMumbai rateUsage type
NAT gatewayhour$0.056APS3-NatGateway-Hours
Data processedGB$0.056APS3-NatGateway-Bytes

Formula: monthly = (NATs × $0.056 × 730) + (GB × $0.056)

Worked example (hypothetical). One NAT is $40.88 a month fixed. Four NATs across environments are about $164 before any data. Push 4 TB through them and add about $230.

Watch for. A NAT in front of a firewall means traffic pays NAT $0.056 plus firewall $0.065, up to $0.121/GB. AWS documents a waiver on part of the NAT charge when both are in the path, so the effective rate lands between $0.065 and $0.121. Public IPv4 on the NAT adds $3.65 a month per address. NAT cost appears in Cost Explorer under "EC2 other", not under VPC, which makes it easy to miss.
Lever. Send S3 and DynamoDB traffic through free gateway endpoints, and remove NAT from VPCs that have no private egress need.

Load balancers (ALB and NLB)

Two meters: a fixed fee for every hour the balancer exists, and a capacity fee measured in LCU (ALB) or NLCU (NLB). You are billed on whichever dimension is highest in that hour: new connections, active connections, bytes processed or rule evaluations.

Lever. Right-size using 14 days of CloudWatch CPU and memory, move to Graviton where the software supports it, then cover steady load with a Savings Plan.

Savings Plans and Reserved Instances

Not a service but a billing contract. You commit to a fixed $/hour for one or three years, and AWS bills matching usage at a lower rate. The commitment is charged every hour whether you use it or not, so it only saves money on usage that is truly steady.

Watch for. Resize first, then commit. Committing on an oversized fleet locks the waste in for years. Savings Plans cover compute (and Compute Savings Plans also cover Fargate and Lambda). They do not cover firewalls, NAT, EBS or data transfer.

Lever. Keep chatty pairs in the same AZ (topology-aware routing in Kubernetes), and watch the cross-AZ usage type every day.

Part B. Storage: pay per GB-month

Storage bills on provisioned or stored size multiplied by time. It does not care whether anything ever reads it.

EBS volumes and snapshots

Part C. Per request, per event and per unit

These cost little per call, so they hide until something automated calls them in a loop, or an agent runs every hour.

AWS Cost Explorer

The Cost Explorer console is free. The API (GetCostAndUsage, GetCostForecast, GetRightsizingRecommendation and the rest) costs a flat fee per request, and a paginated response counts one request per page. A separate opt-in charge applies to hourly and resource-level data.

Resource-level data only reaches back 14 days, so an agent that wants history must save its own snapshots.
Lever. Cache results (Cost Explorer data refreshes at most about once a day), group queries by account in one call, and use the CUR in S3 with Athena for heavy analysis.

7. How to estimate a new component before you build it

  1. List every meter in the component's table above: hours, GB, requests, units.
  2. For each hourly meter, count the resources and multiply by rate × 730. Count per AZ where the table says per AZ.
  3. For each per-GB meter, estimate monthly GB for that path, then multiply. Trace the path: NAT, then firewall, then internet stacks several meters.
  4. For storage, multiply provisioned GB by the GB-month rate, and add snapshot and backup copies.
  5. Add tax (around 15 to 18 percent in India). Then subtract any Savings Plan coverage, on the compute part only.

Worked example: a new two-AZ VPC with NAT and a firewall, 2 TB a month outbound.

8. The levers, ranked by how often they pay

  1. Delete what exists but is not used. Unattached volumes, idle load balancers, empty clusters, unused VPN tunnels, idle public IPs. Pure hourly savings with no performance risk.
  2. Consolidate per-AZ and per-VPC infrastructure. Fewer firewall endpoints, a shared inspection VPC, one ingress per cluster.
  3. Fix the traffic path. Gateway endpoints for S3 and DynamoDB, same-AZ placement for chatty services, no NAT where there is no egress need.
  4. Right-size, then commit. Resize EC2 and RDS on two weeks of real metrics, move to Graviton, then buy Savings Plans for the steady remainder.
  5. Put lifecycles on everything that stores. S3 transitions, noncurrent-version expiry, ECR image limits, snapshot policies, log retention.
  6. Watch the loops. Any automation that calls a per-request API on a schedule: Cost Explorer, KMS, Secrets Manager, Config.

9. Common mistakes

  • Judging cost by service instead of by meter. You cannot tell rent from tolls at the service level.
  • Assuming idle means free. Hourly meters run while idle.
  • Buying a Savings Plan before right-sizing. It locks the waste in for one to three years.
  • Reading member-account bills when the plan sits at the payer. Compute can look free in a member account.
  • Using US prices for Mumbai estimates. Many Mumbai rates are higher, so savings get understated.
  • Reading a daily chart with tax in it. The spike on the 1st is tax for the previous month, not a sudden jump in usage.
  • Counting "calls per day" without multiplying by accounts and pages. Per-request APIs grow fast in automation.

FAQ

Why does my bill not drop when traffic drops?

Because a large share of it is per-hour rent for things that exist: instances, firewall endpoints, NATs, load balancers and clusters. Those meters run whether or not any traffic flows. Only the per-GB part follows traffic.

Where do NAT and cross-AZ charges show up in Cost Explorer?

Mostly under "EC2 other", not under VPC. Filter by usage type, such as NatGateway-Bytes or DataTransfer-Regional-Bytes, to see them clearly.

Is gp3 always cheaper than gp2?

For the same size, yes: in Mumbai gp3 is $0.0912 per GB-month against $0.114 for gp2, and it includes a higher baseline of IOPS and throughput. You only pay extra on gp3 if you provision IOPS or throughput above the baseline.

Do Savings Plans cover the firewall or data transfer?

No. They cover compute only (EC2, and for Compute Savings Plans also Fargate and Lambda). Firewalls, NAT, EBS and data transfer stay at their normal rates.

Is the Cost Explorer API expensive?

Each call is one cent, which is cheap until automation runs it in a loop across many accounts and pages. A design that calls once from the payer account and caches the result stays cheap.

Takeaways

  • Every AWS line item is one of seven meters. Learn the meter and you know the lever.
  • Close to half of a networking-heavy bill can be hourly rent that runs while idle.
  • The path a GB takes decides how many per-GB meters it pays.
  • Storage bills on provisioned size and time, never on use.
  • Per-request services are cheap until automation loops them.
  • Resize first, then commit, and judge commitments at the payer account.
