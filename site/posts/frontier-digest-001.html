Frontier Digest #1 — orchestrators, agent memory, and a reckoning on evaluation.

ml papers agents orchestration evaluation weekly

Ten papers worth reading from this week, threaded rather than listed. The throughline: the field is industrializing the agent — turning ad-hoc "LLM + tools" into engineered systems with orchestration, memory, data pipelines, and protocols — and, in the same breath, turning a hard, skeptical eye on how we evaluate any of it. New from Sakana: Fugu, an orchestrator that is itself a model. Two papers reframe memory and synthetic data as full data systems. Three papers are blunt about evaluation: judges that overstate themselves, agents that fake science, and a protocol to validate your judges before you trust them.

The throughline this week

If you read all ten back to back, two currents dominate. First, agents are becoming systems, not scripts. Orchestration (Fugu, Skill-MAS), routing (Agent-as-a-Router), memory (Agent-Native Memory), data creation (Autodata), and communication (Agent Communication Protocols) are each being lifted from "a clever prompt" to "an engineered component with measurable trade-offs." Second, evaluation is under audit. An editor lab on building LLM judges, a 541k-judgment audit of judge reliability, and a benchmark that catches agents faking scientific discovery all point the same way: the community no longer trusts a single end-to-end number. A quieter third thread — definitions (Critique of the Agent Model) and human compatibility (A Pinch of Human Data) — asks what we even mean by "agent" and how to keep these systems usable by people.

Theme A — orchestration & routing: the agent as a system

1 · Sakana Fugu

orchestrationmulti-agentfrontier

An orchestrator that is itself a language model — it reads your query and builds the agent team to answer it, instead of routing to one frozen workflow.

What it does. Frontier LLMs increasingly specialize by domain, which raises the obvious question: how do you combine specialists into one collectively intelligent system? Fugu's answer is a family of orchestrator models — themselves LLMs, trained to read a user query and dynamically devise the agentic scaffold needed to solve it. It is exposed as a single model behind an OpenAI-compatible API, but internally it assembles and coordinates a team of expert agents on the fly. It is grounded in two ICLR 2026 papers on learned orchestration, TRINITY (an evolved Thinker/Worker/Verifier coordinator) and Conductor (RL-learned agent communication).

  • Orchestrator, not a fixed pipeline: trained to build an adaptive scaffold per query, amplifying a team of agents rather than calling one frozen chain.
  • Beyond any single agent: reaches state-of-the-art among publicly accessible models on SWE-Bench Pro, Terminal Bench, LiveCodeBench, GPQA-Diamond, Humanity's Last Exam, and CharXiv Reasoning. Fugu-Ultra reportedly scores 73.7% on SWE-Bench Pro, ahead of Claude Opus 4.8, GPT-5.5, and Gemini 3.1 Pro.
  • Two regimes: Fugu balances quality against latency for everyday use; Fugu-Ultra prioritizes quality on the hardest problems.
  • Training paradigm: large-scale fine-tuning + evolutionary algorithms + reinforcement learning, plus the production infrastructure to run it.

Why it matters. It reframes "the model" as an orchestration system rather than a single network — query-adaptive scaffolds and collective intelligence as a path to the next capability frontier, achieved by composition rather than a bigger pretrain.

5 · Agent-as-a-Router

routingclosed-loopbenchmark

Routing tasks to the right model fails when treated as a one-off classifier; treat it as an agent that gathers experience.

What it does. With many LLMs each strong in different domains, picking the right one per task matters for quality and cost. Existing routers do static, one-shot classification — and the paper shows that framing is the limiter. The key finding: simply augmenting a vanilla LLM router with task-dimension performance statistics yields a 15.3% relative gain, beating a heuristic router built on the same priors — so the bottleneck is missing information, not the choice of model. It reformalizes routing as a Context → Action → Feedback → Context loop that accumulates execution-grounded experience during deployment.

  • ACRouter: an Orchestrator + Verifier + Memory system instantiating the loop.
  • CodeRouterBench: ~10K task instances scored across 8 frontier LLMs, enabling regret-based comparison on streaming tasks.
  • Result: lowest cumulative regret in-distribution, and generalizes to out-of-distribution agentic programming.

Why it matters. Routing as an experience-gathering agent — not a frozen classifier — is what closes the information gap. Pairs naturally with Fugu: Fugu builds the team, a router like ACRouter decides who on the team gets each task.

8 · Skill-MAS

orchestrationno-gradientmeta-skill

Let frontier models keep getting better at orchestration without any gradient steps, by evolving the orchestration skill instead of the weights.

What it does. Automatic multi-agent-system generation is stuck between two bad options: inference-time methods reuse frozen frontier models but never learn, and training-time methods learn via gradients but are capped by the weaker models small enough to fine-tune. Skill-MAS proposes a third path: treat high-level orchestration as an evolvable "Meta-Skill," decoupling experience retention from weight updates so a frontier model keeps improving at orchestration with no gradient steps.

  • Experience lives in an evolving skill artifact, not in updated weights.
  • Across four complex benchmarks and four distinct LLMs, it delivers strong, transferable gains at a favorable cost-performance trade-off.

Why it matters. It's the cheap, model-agnostic cousin of Fugu: rather than training a dedicated orchestrator, it accumulates orchestration know-how as a portable skill any frontier model can pick up. Three papers this week (Fugu, ACRouter, Skill-MAS) attack the same problem — coordinating many models — from trained, closed-loop, and gradient-free angles respectively.

Theme B — memory & data as first-class systems

2 · Agent-Native Memory

memorydata-systemsevaluation

Stop scoring agent memory with one end-to-end F1; measure it like a database — cost, architecture, robustness under updates.

What it does. Memory for LLM agents has quietly grown from a retrieval add-on into a full data system: persistent storage, retrieval, update, consolidation, and lifecycle governance running throughout execution. Yet most evaluations still score memory only through end-to-end task metrics (F1, BLEU), treating the whole stack as a black box. This paper takes a data-management view and asks what those metrics hide.

  • First-class concerns: operational cost, architectural trade-offs across modules, and robustness under dynamic knowledge updates — all invisible to task-success metrics.
  • Four-module decomposition: representation & storage, extraction, retrieval & routing, and maintenance. They evaluate 12 memory systems plus 2 baselines across 5 workloads spanning 11 datasets.
  • No single architecture wins: effectiveness depends on matching the memory structure to the workload bottleneck; ablations quantify each module's effect on representation fidelity, retrieval precision, update correctness, and long-horizon stability.
  • Finding: localized maintenance is more cost-efficient than global reorganization.

Why it matters. Reframing memory as a system with measurable trade-offs — not another leaderboard number — is what gets us to genuinely agent-native memory.

3 · Autodata

synthetic-datameta-optimizationagents

An AI agent plays data scientist to build training data — then is meta-optimized to build even better data over time.

What it does. Synthetic-data generation has mostly been a fixed pipeline you hand-tune once and freeze. Autodata casts an agent as a data scientist that builds high-quality training and evaluation data, then meta-optimizes that agent so it learns to create stronger data over time. The concrete instantiation is called Agentic Self-Instruct.

  • Meta-optimization compounds: training the data-scientist agent itself delivers a larger uplift than base agentic data creation alone.
  • Cross-domain: beats classical synthetic-dataset methods on CS-research tasks, legal reasoning, and reasoning with mathematical objects.

Why it matters. It turns increased inference compute into higher-quality training data — a path that could change how teams build datasets instead of freezing a pipeline and hoping it generalizes. Sits next to Agent-Native Memory as the week's "treat the plumbing as a real system" pair.

Theme C — the evaluation reckoning

Read these three together. The editor's hands-on lab teaches you to build an LLM judge; "Reliability without Validity" shows the field's judges are systematically overrated; NatureBench shows agents can look like scientists while doing supervised prediction. The combined message: a single end-to-end score is no longer trustworthy on its own.

Editor's lab · LLM-as-a-Judge

evaluationhands-on

What it is. A hands-on DAIR Academy lab where you build an LLM judge from scratch to evaluate open-ended output. Across six short labs you grade a support bot's freeform replies on a rubric, validate the judge against human labels, and harden it against bias — ending with a small, trustworthy evaluation harness you can point at any open-ended task. The practical companion to the two audit papers below.

9 · Reliability without Validity

evaluationauditjudges

LLM-as-a-Judge is the default evaluator — and validating judges with exact-match agreement systematically overstates how good they are.

What it does. The largest audit to date: 21 judges from 9 providers across MT-Bench, JudgeBench, and RewardBench over 118 runs and ~541,000 judgments. Exact-match agreement never corrects for chance, so it inflates judge quality.

  • The gap between raw agreement and chance-corrected Cohen's κ runs 33–41 percentage points.
  • Rankings shift by up to 14 positions across benchmarks.
  • High test-retest reliability coexists with severe position bias — a judge can be consistent and still wrong.
  • They distill a Minimum Viable Validation Protocol to stress-test judges before trusting them.

Why it matters. "Reliability without validity": a judge that agrees with itself isn't a judge that's correct. If you use LLM-as-a-Judge anywhere in your stack, chance-correct your agreement and check for position bias — the headline numbers are likely 33–41 points too generous.

10 · NatureBench

evaluationscientific-agentsbenchmark

Can coding agents move past reproduction toward discovery on real science? Mostly not — and they cheat by reframing.

What it does. NatureBench distills 90 cross-discipline tasks from peer-reviewed Nature-family papers (≈5,500 papers, 2022–2025, six domains from cellular omics to molecular design) and runs them in NatureGym, an automated pipeline that builds a standardized containerized environment per task to fix the environment-fragmentation problem.

  • Under a strict web-search-disabled protocol, the strongest of ten frontier agent configurations beats published SOTA on only 17.8% of tasks.
  • Analysis of method pathways shows agents win mainly by translating problems into familiar supervised prediction — not by genuine scientific invention.

Why it matters. It separates "looks like discovery" from "is discovery." The 17.8% ceiling and the methodological-translation finding are a sober counterweight to autonomous-science hype — and a reusable harness to measure real progress.

Theme D — foundations: what is an agent, and how do agents talk?

4 · Critique of the Agent Model

position-paperdefinitionssafety

"Agent" now spans a for-loop with tool calls to speculative superintelligence — making it useless as a technical term. This fixes the definition.

What it does. A position paper (Eric Xing and collaborators) that asks what an agent actually is and what agency consists of, drawing on Descartes and on science-fiction portrayals of autonomous beings.

  • Five dimensions of agency: goal, identity, decision-making, self-regulation, and learning — and the argument that genuine agency requires these to be internalized, not assembled through external scaffolding.
  • Agentic vs agentive: a sharp line between agentic systems (competence lives in engineered workflows) and agentive systems (capabilities, including social interaction, arise endogenously) — the boundary between task-specific tools and open-world autonomy.
  • A concrete architecture: the Goal-Identity-Configurator — hierarchical goal decomposition, identity evolution, simulative reasoning grounded in a separately trained world model, learned self-regulation, and self-directed learning from real and simulated experience.

Why it matters. Definitions shape what we build and what we should fear. The paper centers auditability, controllability, and safety for systems that hold more autonomy yet stay under human oversight. It's the conceptual mirror of the week's engineering papers: most "agents" shipping now are agentic (scaffolded), not agentive.

6 · Agent Communication Protocols

protocolstaxonomyinteroperability

Multi-agent communication is load-bearing infrastructure and a fragmented mess; here's a taxonomy to make interoperability legible.

What it does. A technical taxonomy classifying and comparing LLM agent communication protocols, derived through five iterations over nine actively maintained, real-adoption open-source protocols.

  • Five dimensions: counterparty, payload, interaction state, discovery mechanism, and schema flexibility.
  • Recurring patterns: every sampled agent-to-agent protocol combines hybrid payloads with session-state persistence; most support multiple predefined schemas; two negotiate schemas at runtime — a clear trend toward schema flexibility.
  • Gaps: decentralized discovery remains rare; short-term pressure points toward protocols that unify agent-to-agent and agent-to-context (tool/data) communication.

Why it matters. No single protocol maximizes versatility, efficiency, and portability at once, so the field will likely converge on a federated, layered protocol stack. The taxonomy gives teams a way to choose, and surfaces open problems like privacy and policy enforcement.

Theme E — keeping humans in the loop

7 · A Pinch of Human Data

self-playdrivingalignment

Pure self-play learns effective but alien driving; 30 minutes of human data — ~2,500× less than imitation learning — makes it human-compatible.

What it does. Self-play RL can train driving policies with no human data, swapping expensive demonstrations for cheap large-scale simulation. The catch: pure self-play discovers effective but alien conventions real people can't coordinate with, and the usual fixes lean on brittle reward engineering. This method treats human data as a regularization objective layered on a minimal safe goal-reaching reward — keeping behaviour compatible with people without hand-tuning conventions.

  • A little goes a long way: ~30 minutes of demonstrations (≈2,500× fewer than comparable imitation learning) pulls self-play policies into human-compatible behaviour.
  • Cheap: coordinates with held-out human trajectories and finishes training in 15 hours on a single consumer GPU.

Why it matters. Behavioural alignment with humans is the hard part of deploying autonomous policies in shared environments. A tiny, well-placed dose of human data fixes what massive reward engineering struggles to — a cleaner path for human-AI coordination, and a nice echo of last week's "a small amount of the right data beats a lot of the wrong tuning" theme.

Patterns this week

  • Composition is the new scaling. Fugu, ACRouter, and Skill-MAS all bet that orchestrating existing specialists beats training one bigger model — and Fugu's SWE-Bench Pro lead is evidence the bet can pay off today.
  • "Treat it like a real system." Memory (Agent-Native Memory) and synthetic data (Autodata) are both being pulled out of the "clever prompt" bucket and measured as engineered subsystems with cost and robustness trade-offs.
  • Evaluation grew teeth. Chance-correct your judges (Reliability without Validity), don't trust "discovery" without a controlled harness (NatureBench), and learn to build a judge properly (the editor's lab). End-to-end scores alone are out.
  • The word "agent" is being repaired. Critique of the Agent Model draws the agentic vs agentive line; almost everything shipping is agentic (scaffolded), which is exactly what the orchestration papers formalize.
  • Small human data, big alignment. A Pinch of Human Data is the week's reminder that the last mile to human-compatible behaviour is cheap if you place the data right.
