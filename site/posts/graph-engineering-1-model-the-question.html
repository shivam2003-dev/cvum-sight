Graph Engineering Starts With Questions, Not Nodes.

How to recognize relationship-shaped decisions, choose a graph contract, and model only facts a decision needs.

graph-engineeringknowledge-graphsdata-systems

A graph is not a prettier ER diagram and it is not a licence to turn every noun into a node. Graph engineering is the work of designing, validating, operating, and evolving connected data so people and programs can answer relationship-shaped questions reliably.

Start with a question that loses meaning when flattened into one record: “Which suppliers sit downstream of this component?”, “Which policy applies through ownership, geography, and product?”, or “Which identities can reach a database through a chain of roles?” The entities matter, but the decision lives in the paths between them.

Start from a decision

Record who acts on the answer, what evidence they require, how fresh it must be, and the cost of being wrong. Then express the smallest useful path: (component)-[:SUPPLIED_BY]->(supplier)-[:OWNED_BY*1..3]->(company). If the work is mostly filtering independent records, a relational model may be the right primary store. If it repeatedly discovers dependencies, neighborhoods, variable paths, or authorization chains, a graph makes the relationship explicit and reusable.

Choose the semantic contract deliberately

RDF represents subject–predicate–object triples, uses global identifiers, and supports datasets with named graphs. It is useful for shared vocabularies, interoperable data, and source partitions. A labeled property graph represents entities as nodes and connections as relationships, often with labels and properties on both. It is natural for application traversals such as REPORTS_TO { since, source, confidence }. Neither is “more graph”; choose the identity, constraints, query behavior, and interoperability contract that your consumers need.

Model claims, not vague links

An ACQUIRED link may be a verified legal fact, an announcement, a vendor import, or a model extraction. When provenance, time, confidence, or disagreement matters, give the claim an identity. An assertion can link subject, predicate, object, source document, extraction run, observation time, and review status. Serving queries can return accepted claims; audits can explain why.

Identity is load-bearing

Names are labels for people, not durable keys. Define a canonical key per type, name the source that owns it, preserve aliases and source IDs, and set merge rules before ingestion. Entity resolution should yield candidates with evidence and thresholds. A bad merge joins entire neighborhoods and creates persuasive but fictional paths.

A graph earns its complexity when it makes a relationship-dependent decision faster, clearer, or safer—not when it contains more dots.

Start with one hard decision, two or three entity types, real source data, and the three hardest traversals. If a returned path cannot be explained to a domain expert in plain language, it is not ready to scale.
