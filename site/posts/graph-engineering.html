Graph Engineering — Connected Knowledge You Can Trust.

A practical systems guide to relationship-shaped decisions, provenance, GraphRAG, agent boundaries, and production operations.

graph-engineeringknowledge-graphsgraphragdata-systems

Most teams do not decide to build a graph because they are enchanted by nodes and arrows. They arrive there after a more practical frustration: the answer they need is scattered across records that make sense alone but become useful only when connected. A service is healthy, a dependency is vulnerable, an owner exists somewhere in a directory, a change ticket explains an exception, and a policy says who may act. The difficult question is never “show me this row.” It is “what is connected to this thing, why does that connection matter, and can I trust the path that led me here?”

That is the moment graph engineering begins. Not with a database purchase, a visual canvas, or an LLM demo. It begins with a decision that depends on relationships. The work is to make those relationships explicit enough to query, cautious enough to audit, and durable enough to survive a changing organisation. A graph is simply the medium. The engineering is the discipline around it.

The question hiding inside the diagram

Imagine an incident at 2 a.m. A package vulnerability has landed in the security queue. An engineer wants to know which production services can be affected, who owns them, whether a compensating control exists, and which customers sit downstream. A relational system can answer pieces of that question with joins. But the shape of the investigation is a walk: package to image, image to deployment, deployment to service, service to team, service to customer, exception to evidence. The number of hops is not known in advance, and neither is the important branch.

A graph makes that walk a first-class object. It says a Service runs an Image; an Image contains a Package; a Team owns a Service; a Document supports an Exception. That is more than a prettier schema. It gives an engineer a vocabulary for asking questions about paths rather than reconstructing them from application code every time.

The important restraint is this: not every system needs a graph. A ledger, a checkout flow, or an append-only event stream often has a clearer primary model. Graphs earn their cost when the relationship itself is part of the answer: impact analysis, authorization chains, fraud rings, supply networks, entity resolution, lineage, or connected retrieval. If the business question is mostly filtering and aggregation over independent records, a conventional table may remain the more honest design.

Every edge should be able to answer “according to whom?”

The most seductive graph is also the most dangerous: a clean picture full of confident lines whose origin nobody can explain. Take the statement “Company A owns Company B.” Is that a legal filing, a current vendor record, a news report, an analyst inference, or a sentence an LLM extracted from a PDF? Those are not the same claim. They may disagree, and they may change at different times.

Good graph engineering treats a relationship as a claim with a life. It records the source system or document, the source version, the observation time, the transformation that produced it, and the review or confidence state. Sometimes the relationship can carry that metadata directly. When several sources, competing interpretations, or detailed evidence matter, an assertion deserves its own node: the assertion points to subject and object, carries its predicate, and links to the evidence that supports it.

This feels heavier than drawing a line, but it changes the experience of using the system. An operations engineer can ask not only “who owns this service?” but “why does the system believe that, and when was it last verified?” A correction can replace one assertion without erasing the history that led to it. A serving graph can contain only approved, current claims while a canonical graph retains the evidence needed to rebuild that view. The difference is the difference between a useful answer and a defensible one.

Identity is where the real work starts

Relationships are only as good as their endpoints. Names are poor identifiers: teams rename themselves, companies have subsidiaries and aliases, people change email addresses, and a service can acquire a friendly name, a repository name, and an infrastructure ID. If an ingestion job accidentally merges two entities because their labels look similar, it does not create one small error. It joins their neighborhoods and manufactures a whole family of false paths.

That is why entity resolution is a product decision, not a fuzzy-matching checkbox. Define a canonical key for each important entity, name the authority responsible for that key, preserve aliases and external IDs, and keep uncertain matches visible. A candidate match with evidence is much safer than a silent merge. In a graph, identity is the load-bearing beam: every traversal trusts it.

Schema is not the enemy of exploration

“Graph databases are schema-flexible” is true in the narrow sense that a new node or property does not always require a destructive migration. It should not mean that every producer can invent meanings without consequence. A schema is the agreement that lets a graph become a shared system: which types exist, which relationships are legal, which fields are required, which identifiers are unique, and which temporal or provenance fields are mandatory.

RDF systems can express much of this with SHACL shapes. Property-graph systems often combine database constraints with application and pipeline validation. The mechanism matters less than the habit. Validate the paths people rely on, not only individual fields. If a production service must have one current owner, catch the ingestion run that creates two. If an access path crosses an expired role grant, make validity time part of the traversal. If a relationship is inferred, do not let it answer a question that requires verified legal evidence.

Those rules also give evolution a safe shape. A vague RELATED_TO edge might eventually become DEPENDS_ON, RUNS_ON, and OWNED_BY. The right move is not a silent rename. Introduce precise predicates, migrate or dual-write historical claims, compare the critical answers, update consumers, and retire the old meaning when the transition is complete. A graph changes constantly; unmanaged semantic drift is what makes it untrustworthy.

Where GraphRAG helps—and where it does not

GraphRAG has made graphs feel newly urgent because it promises an AI that can reason across a private corpus. The useful interpretation is less magical. A conventional retrieval system finds passages that look relevant to a question. A graph can add entity identity, typed relationships, provenance, and a way to expand from one fact to its connected context. That can be invaluable for questions such as “which policy applies to this system through its region and data classification?”

But the graph is not a replacement for text. The phrase in a source document still carries caveats, negation, scope, and exact language that an extracted edge may lose. A strong GraphRAG system retrieves both: a compact, permission-filtered evidence path and the passages that support it. The model should know which claims are verified, which are inferred, and when to abstain. An answer that cites a graph edge without showing its source has merely moved the hallucination problem one layer down.

Microsoft’s GraphRAG documentation usefully separates local search from global search. Local retrieval combines graph data with source chunks for specific entities; global retrieval works from generated community reports to reason about themes across a collection. Those are different tools for different questions. Treat them as routes in a retrieval system, measure them against a plain keyword/vector baseline, and keep the budget visible. Indexing, extraction, embedding, and broad graph expansion all cost money and introduce their own failure modes.

Three systems that are often confused

Graph engineering also sits beside two adjacent disciplines that are easy to collapse into one. Harness engineering builds the environment around an agent: tools, permissions, workspace state, memory, logging, and approval gates. Loop engineering designs the control cycle: observe, plan, act, inspect feedback, verify, stop, or escalate. Graph engineering builds the connected knowledge layer: identity, claims, evidence, time, and paths.

An incident agent makes the distinction concrete. The graph can return the services upstream of a vulnerable library and the documents that support each ownership claim. The loop decides whether the evidence is sufficient to page someone or ask a follow-up question. The harness controls whether the agent may send that page, open a ticket, or change infrastructure. More graph edges do not repair a missing approval gate. A cleverer agent loop does not repair ambiguous identity. Each layer has a different job, and a reliable system keeps that boundary visible.

The production test is a suspicious answer

Graphs are often demonstrated with a friendly query and a beautiful path. Production starts with the uncomfortable query: “Why did the system say this?” The answer should lead to the normalized entities, the exact edges, the source evidence, the graph and schema version, the authorization context, and the retrieval configuration. If any of those are missing, correction becomes storytelling rather than engineering.

Operate the graph like a data product. Track freshness from each source, validation pass rate, unresolved entities, orphan nodes, unexpected degree changes, query latency, traversal fan-out, permission denials, and the rate at which humans correct outputs. Version transformations and schemas. Make writes replayable. Test restore and rebuild paths. Give variable-length traversals limits on relationship type, hops, result count, and time. A graph query that can expand without a budget is not insight; it is an outage waiting for a user to type the wrong question.

The appeal of a graph is that it turns relationships into something a system can execute. The responsibility is to ensure it executes relationships that are meaningful, current, permitted, and supported by evidence. Start from one decision that genuinely needs a path. Model the claims behind that path. Keep text with structure when building retrieval. Measure what the system returns when it is wrong as carefully as when it is fast. That is what makes graph engineering less like a wall of notes—and more like a dependable way to understand a complicated world.

What to build first

If this still sounds abstract, resist the urge to model the whole organisation. Pick the decision with the most painful manual join. It might be answering which production services inherit a policy exception, identifying the blast radius of a supplier change, or finding the owner and evidence behind a failing control. Put a small number of authoritative entities and relationships behind that decision into the graph. Write the query that an expert runs today. Then sit with the result and ask the expert to challenge every edge.

That review reveals the work that diagrams hide. Someone will say the ownership record is stale. Someone else will point out that “uses” means runtime dependency in one system but a build-time import in another. A third person will ask whether an offboarded employee’s access should count historically. Those objections are not reasons to abandon the graph. They are the domain knowledge the graph needs in order to deserve trust.

Only after that loop works should you add automation, more sources, or an LLM interface. The successful graph is usually not the most complete one. It is the one whose most important path has clear semantics, durable evidence, known limits, and an owner willing to fix it. Build that path first. The rest of the graph can grow around a decision that already matters.
