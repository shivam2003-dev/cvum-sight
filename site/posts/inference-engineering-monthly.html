September 2026 the runner becomes the only path, correctness bugs in LoRA and cache sizing, decision models in Ollama, operational hardening in llm-d

Summary: September closed the year's arc with consolidation and with correctness work.

The year ends with a default that is also a test

Across the year, a steady number of features became defaults: asynchronous scheduling in January, speculative decoding version 2 in May, the cache manager version 2 in August, and now the runner for every model in September.

A default is also a test. The engine's users are the test suite for the default, and the bugs they find are reported in the notes that follow.

The lesson for a team at the end of the year is to treat the default as a moving target.

Model Runner V2 for every model

vLLM v0.29.0 on September 9 brought 594 commits from 277 contributors and completed the rollout of Model Runner V2 to all models.source ↗

Batch-sharded sampling deserves a closer look, because it is an example of the memory budget from January applied to a part of the pipeline most people do not think about.

The CUDA graph memory profiling is the other half of the same story.

vLLM v0.30.0 on September 22 brought 762 commits from 315 contributors, the largest count of the year.

Correctness bugs in the release candidates

TensorRT-LLM's September candidates disclosed bugs that are worth a close read, because they are the kind that do not fail a test suite.source ↗

A silent adapter bug is a serious one. A team that serves a customer-specific adapter, and gets the base model's answer instead, may not notice for days.

The memory estimate bug is the opposite kind of problem. The server refuses to start while memory sits unused.

The rc26 notes also mentioned GPT-OSS with an Eagle draft model among the known issues.

TensorRT-LLM removes AutoDeploy

TensorRT-LLM v1.3.0rc29 on September 29 removed the AutoDeploy integration and its public entry points, labeled as a breaking change.source ↗

A removal that was announced in July and carried out in September is the pattern to expect from a mature project.

The same candidate added a long list of model items, including Qwen3.5 checkpoints with global FP8 scales, a vision rotary embedding change for Gemma 4 that avoids a matrix multiply, a CuTeDSL MoE backend for Kimi K3 NVFP4, and extended piecewise CUDA graphs for MiniMax-M3. The breadth of the list shows how much of the runtime is now shared across model families.

SGLang: Qwen3.8 and the 2.4-trillion-parameter model

SGLang v0.5.19 on September 5 brought 786 pull requests from 214 contributors, and its headline model was Qwen3.8 at 2.4 trillion total parameters with 95 billion active, listed in the notes as an autoregressive model with a dense 27 billion parameter sibling.source ↗

SGLang v0.5.20 on September 18 brought 713 pull requests and added GLM-5.3-Flash, Tencent's Hy4-Preview, and Qwen3.8-Flash-Next.

A large model with a small active set raises a specific question for serving: where do the weights live?

Ollama adds decision models

Ollama v0.35.0 on September 28 introduced decision models through a new endpoint called systemone.source ↗

This is a different kind of model from the ones earlier in the series.

The interface matters as much as the model. Returning probabilities and scores instead of text means the caller can apply thresholds, log calibration, and compare decisions over time.

A note of caution applies. Any scored decision needs calibration testing, meaning a check that a score of 0.8 is right about 80 percent of the time on your data.

Ollama v0.34.x ran from September 5 to September 23, with several patch releases, and v0.33.3 on September 2 added image and audio input for Gemma 4 on the MLX engine, reported cached prompt tokens, and honored default parameters defined in GGUF files.

llm-d v0.10.0: operational hardening

llm-d v0.10.0 on September 29 set its themes in plain language: operational hardening, to make the production path safe and boring, with rollouts, high availability, and failure behavior that operators can trust, and production readiness, to move the flagship paths from working to default.source ↗

The word boring deserves attention. In infrastructure, boring is a goal.

The move of changes upstream is a sign of consolidation across the ecosystem.

The llm-d themes also match what a fleet team should watch for in production.

LMDeploy: DeepEP v2 and a Mooncake connector

LMDeploy v0.17.0 on September 1 integrated DeepEP v2, a communication library for mixture-of-experts dispatch, and added Kimi K2.6 support on its PyTorch path.source ↗

LMDeploy v0.18.0 on September 28 followed. The release notes for that version were not part of this issue's detailed reading, so this section does not describe its contents.

llama.cpp and the MoE reduction

llama.cpp published a build on September 1 with a CUDA change that fuses the weighted reduction of mixture-of-experts outputs.source ↗

Memory traffic is the constraint that matters most for decode, because each generated token must move the weights and the intermediate results.

The release cadence continued at a high pace in September, with a batch of builds on September 30 alone.

FlashAttention 4 reaches beta 33

The FlashAttention repository published fa4 betas 29 through 33 in September, with the latest on September 30.source ↗ Beta 29 on September 2 was a compilation speedup for scalar mask generation on SM100 hardware.

That long beta period is itself a lesson about kernels. A kernel that serves many shapes, hardware generations, and precision modes takes a long time to harden, and the team is right to keep the beta label until the coverage is broad.

Concepts this month adds

September adds three concepts. The first is the calibrated decision.

The second concept is the silent failure. A silently ignored adapter or a silently wrong cache estimate does not raise an error and does not move a latency metric.

The third concept is the active parameter count. For mixture-of-experts models, the total parameter count sets the memory needed for weights, and the active count sets per-token compute.

What to check this month

  1. Test LoRA adapters end to end. For any adapter that targets experts in a mixture-of-experts model, compare the adapted output to a known-good reference on every deployment, not only the first.
  2. Check cache sizing after startup. If the engine refuses to start on a conservative estimate, measure free memory and set an explicit cache size if the estimate is consistently off.
  3. Plan the TensorRT-LLM AutoDeploy migration. If you used the AutoDeploy path, the removal has landed in rc29. Verify your replacement before the next stable release.
  4. Calibrate decision scores before setting thresholds. For any decision model, measure whether the scores match outcomes on your data before using them to route traffic.
  5. Use reported cached tokens for cost tracking. If your runner reports cached prompt tokens, log them per request so the prefix cache's benefit is visible.
  6. Review llm-d upgrades as a pair with the engine. Version the fleet layer and the engine together, following the coupling from the earlier months.
  7. Run a year-end default review. List every default that flipped this year for your engines, and confirm each setting you rely on still does what you expect.

Frequently asked questions

Is a runner that handles every model the same as a faster runner?

Not necessarily. It is a more uniform runner, which means fewer special cases and fewer silent fallbacks. Speed depends on the model and hardware. Test the speed on your models, and test the correctness on your adapters.

Should I trust a silent-failure fix without a test?

No. A fix for a silent failure should be verified with an end-to-end check that would have caught the original bug. Otherwise the fix is a claim, not a result.

Does a decision model replace a classifier?

It can, for some tasks. It returns structured scores that a classifier also returns. The choice depends on whether the model's training matches your labels, which you must test on your own data.

Why is a quiet month worth noting?

Because it is a window to validate the previous release. Use the quiet time to test the current baseline, so the next release can be compared against something verified.

August 2026 Kimi K3 on day zero, KV-aware routing in Ray Serve, restore points for cancelled prefills, KV cache manager V2 by default

Summary: August was the month the cache became something the router, the runtime, and the client all reason about together.

The cache becomes shared state

Earlier in this series, the cache was an engine detail. A single process owned its blocks, and the router only knew which worker to pick.

That shift changes who is responsible for correctness. When a cache lives in one process, only that process can get it wrong.

There is also a cost dimension. Every extra agreement is a message, and messages take time.

Read against the earlier months, August is the point where the stack's pieces start to close their loops.

Ray Serve LLM: routing on KV state

Ray 2.58.0 on August 23 completed a feature that Ray 2.57 had previewed: KV cache and token aware request routing for Ray Serve LLM.source ↗

Why does a single extra tokenization matter? Tokenization is cheap per request, but at high request rates it adds up, and it adds latency at the moment a request enters the system.

Broadcasting cache lifecycle events to every ingress replica is the more interesting part.

The January section described cache-aware routing with a radix tree and the risk of a stale view.

Ray 2.57.0 on August 11 had its own data-side change: DataSourceV2 became the default, so read calls use the new scan and listing infrastructure with row-group aware chunking, and Hash Shuffle V2 removed a pool of aggregator actors that had to be sized from an estimate of input size.

Restore points for cancelled prefills

Ollama v0.33.0 on August 21 included a set of caching changes that are worth reading slowly.source ↗

The notes give a concrete example. On models with recurrent layers, a resumed prefill recorded restore points that did not cover what they claimed.

The lesson is about what a restore point means. A restore point is a promise that the cache holds state up to a given token.

For agent workloads, which cancel and resume often, this matters a great deal.

Ollama v0.33.0 also added Claude Desktop configuration for Ollama as a third-party gateway provider.

Kimi K3 and the hybrid attention pattern

SGLang v0.5.17 on August 8 described Kimi K3 day-zero support.source ↗

The layer mix is the detail to study. Linear attention layers have a state that stays fixed in size as the context grows, instead of a cache that grows with every token.

The number of layers of each kind is a design choice that trades memory against recall.

This is why the hybrid cache work from May and July matters.

vLLM v0.27.0 on August 10 brought Kimi K3 support as a full stack in one release: core model files, kernels, Python and Rust frontends, and compressed-tensors quantized checkpoints.

The adaptive speculative token budget is a connection to the July section.

A note on the one-million-token context: a long window is valuable only if the model uses it, and the way a team checks that is a recall test at multiple depths.

TensorRT-LLM: KV cache manager V2 by default

TensorRT-LLM v1.3.0rc25 on August 31 announced that its KV cache manager V2 is now the default for a long list of model families, including DeepSeek V3, R1, and V3 Lite, DeepSeek V3.2 and V4, GLM-5, GPT-OSS, Mistral Large 3, the Kimi K2 line including K3, MiniMax M2 and M3, the Nemotron H family, and Qwen3-Next, Qwen3.5, and Qwen3.8.source ↗ The candidate that preceded it had listed a KVCacheManagerV2 teardown failure among its known issues, which is a reminder that a default flip follows a period of known problems.

A default change in the cache manager reaches the most sensitive layer of the engine.

The breadth of the list is informative. The model families that now default to the V2 manager cover nearly the whole recent lineup, which suggests the team considers the manager stable across dense, mixture-of-experts, and hybrid models.

Mooncake and LMCache: the storage layer keeps moving

Mooncake v0.3.13 on August 26 and v0.3.13.post1 on August 31 added several fixes in its transfer engine and CI.source ↗

LMCache v0.5.5 release candidates arrived on August 28 and August 31, with builds for CUDA 12.9, ROCm, and Intel XPU alongside the standard wheel.

LMCache's ROCm wheel in early August, for AMD MI300X and MI350 series hardware, was built to match the upstream vLLM ROCm container's torch version.

The storage work in August also reflects a broader lesson about where failures hide.

llama.cpp reaches a release every few days

The llama.cpp project is the engine underneath much of local inference, and its build cadence matters more each month.source ↗

For a team, a fast cadence means that pinning a version is a choice, not a default.

The llama.cpp build cadence also explains why Ollama's engine changes track it.

xDiT and parallel diffusion

xDiT released version 0.5.0 on August 31.source ↗ The project is a parallel inference framework for diffusion transformers, which splits the work of generating an image or video across several GPUs.

The general point is that the engines in this series now serve several kinds of model, and the lessons carry across.

Reading August against the year so far

If you read this series from January, August looks different from the months before it.

Agreement is also where teams get hurt in ways that dashboards do not show.

A second pattern is that the pieces increasingly come from different projects.

Finally, August shows that the frontier is moving toward very large models with very long contexts.

Concepts this month adds

August adds three concepts. The first is the restore point, a promise that a stored state is complete up to a given token.

The second concept is the mixed-state cache. Hybrid models, such as the Kimi K3 layer mix, hold two kinds of state: fixed recurrent state and growing cache.

The third concept is the default flip for critical components.

What to check this month

  1. Measure KV-aware routing on your churn. In Ray Serve LLM, record the realized cache hit rate and the broadcast traffic at peak churn before and after enabling the routing.
  2. Test cancellation and resume on agent traffic. Cancel long prefills on purpose and confirm the retry resumes from its restore point, not from zero.
  3. Compare decode does not recompute attention.">KV cache manager V2 against the previous default. Use a replay and long-output tests, and compare hit rates and output drift before promoting.
  4. Test long-context recall for Kimi K3 class models. Serving a one-million-token window quickly is not the same as using it correctly. Run recall at several depths.
  5. Pin llama.cpp and Ollama versions in local products. A fast build cadence means unpinned output can drift between runs.
  6. Watch transfer leaks in long-running processes. Track memory over multi-day runs after upgrading Mooncake, since leaks do not fail tests.
  7. Re-run the cancellation test after every Ollama upgrade. Restore-point behavior is a recent change, so keep a test that cancels a long prefill and checks the retry cost.

Frequently asked questions

Is KV-aware routing worth it for a small fleet?

Usually not until the fleet has several replicas and repeated prefixes. With one or two replicas, the router has little to choose between. Measure the hit rate gain against the broadcast cost before enabling it.

Does a one-million-token context mean the model remembers everything?

No. It means the model can accept that many tokens. Recall depends on training and on the attention layout, and it must be tested at several depths in the context.

Should I adopt a new cache manager default right away?

Not without a replay. A default flip changes correctness-sensitive behavior for every deployment that did not set the flag, so test it first and keep the previous manager available for rollback.

Why do local runner releases matter for server teams?

Because local tools and agent clients are often where new behaviors first appear, such as cancellation patterns and restore points. The fixes often carry over to server engines.

July 2026 the legacy attention path is deleted, DSpark confidence-driven drafting, a runner default for dense models, agents inside the CLI

Summary: July was the month the stack removed its old paths and made its new ones the default.

Deleting code as a feature

Most of the features in this series were additions. A new kernel, a new cache policy, a new routing method.

Deleting a path is expensive to do well. The old implementation has users, edge cases, and tests that depend on its behavior.

For operators, the lesson of a deletion is to read the removal notice as a forced migration.

July also shows that the engines have started to converge on a common set of paths.

Model Runner V2 as the default

vLLM v0.25.0 on July 11 brought 558 commits from 232 contributors.source ↗

The same release made the Transformers modeling backend as fast as native vLLM, and added FP8 mixture-of-experts support to it, along with migrations for several older model families.

vLLM v0.25.1 on July 14 was a two-commit patch that stopped a missing system FFmpeg from blocking model startup when torchcodec was installed.

vLLM v0.26.0 on July 27 brought 411 commits from 212 contributors.

DSpark: sizing the verify window by confidence

SGLang v0.5.16 on July 25 introduced DSpark, which the notes describe as confidence-driven speculative decoding.source ↗

DSpark drafts in blocks, semi-autoregressively, and then sizes each verification window from the draft's own confidence.

The idea of tying the window to confidence is a general one.

The release notes also mention an environment setting that changes how verification is batched in compact mode.

SGLang v0.5.15 on July 10 made Spec V2 the default, with zero-overhead scheduling through CUDA-graphable draft extension, removed device-to-host and host-to-device synchronizations, and fused metadata operations.

CacheBlend under the hybrid manager

LMCache v0.5.2 on July 22 carried an important compatibility requirement.source ↗

CacheBlend is a method for reusing cached text that was computed in a different context.

Position is the subtle part. A rotary position embedding encodes where a token sits in the sequence, so a block cached at one offset carries the wrong position if it is reused at another.

The compatibility line is a practical one. A team running vLLM 0.25 cannot take LMCache 0.5.2 without checking the version pairing.

TensorRT-LLM: the AutoDeploy deprecation

TensorRT-LLM v1.3.0rc21 on July 15 included a deprecation notice for its AutoDeploy backend.source ↗

That reasoning is worth thinking about, because it describes a real tension.

The rc23 release on July 31 listed a long set of known issues, including hangs on GB300 disaggregated setups for DeepSeek V4 Pro and a multi-GPU crash for DeepSeek R1 with pipeline and multi-token prediction.

Ollama v0.32.0 on July 11 changed what the command does.source ↗ Running plain ollama now launches an interactive agent that can help with coding and delegated tasks, using a cloud model by default in the example.

For the series, the shift matters because it changes what a local runner is for.

The release also added guided decoding support for speculative decoding.

Mooncake v0.3.12 on July 23 and v0.3.12.post1 on July 25 added transport-level work.source ↗

These changes are invisible to most users and critical to cache-heavy clusters.

Early detection is the important part. Out-of-memory failures are expensive because they kill work in progress.

The FlashAttention repository published fa4 betas 20 through 24 in July, on a steady weekly cadence.source ↗

Reading July against the earlier months

The months since February have a clear arc. February asked whether the pieces could run together.

That shift has a practical consequence for benchmarking. When a feature is optional, a benchmark compares it against the default.

The arc also changes the risk. A feature in beta risks a bug in one configuration.

Concepts this month adds

July adds three concepts. The first is removal as maintenance.

The second concept is confidence as a control signal. DSpark ties the verification window to the proposer's confidence, rather than to a fixed number.

The third concept is position in cache reuse. Reusing a cached block at a different offset requires correcting its positional encoding, and getting that wrong degrades answers quietly.

What to check this month

  1. Find configurations that depended on the removed attention path. Before upgrading vLLM past v0.25.0, check your settings for anything that assumed the legacy implementation, and test the new default on your traffic.
  2. Check optional dependencies at build time. The FFmpeg fix shows that an optional library can block startup. Verify the container starts with every model you serve.
  3. Pair LMCache with vLLM by version. LMCache 0.5.2 requires vLLM 0.26 or later for its supported path. Pin the pair as one unit.
  4. Test DSpark against fixed-length speculation. Compare both on your batch sizes and content, not on the batch-one headline number.
  5. Test CacheBlend answers against a fresh computation. For reused context, check that answers match a no-cache reference on the documents you serve.
  6. Check schema validity under speculation. For structured output, test that constrained decoding still produces valid output with speculation on.
  7. Review the Ollama telemetry default if you use its launch path. The Claude Code launch path now disables telemetry by default, and you should confirm that matches your policy.

Frequently asked questions

Did vLLM remove PagedAttention entirely?

The notes say the legacy attention implementation was removed, and that the newer backends are the standard path. The block-based idea remains in those backends. What is gone is the older code path that implemented it.

Is DSpark faster than a fixed draft length?

The notes show a strong result at batch size one on one model and hardware. Whether it wins on your workload depends on batch size, content, and calibration. Measure both methods on your traffic.

Should I trust a release candidate with known hangs?

Not for production. A candidate with open hang reports needs more time. Wait for a stable release, or test the candidate against your exact configuration first.

Does the Ollama agent change how I should benchmark it?

Yes. An agent sends repeated, growing-context requests, so benchmark with multi-turn sessions and tool calls, not single prompts.

June 2026 peer-to-peer KV transfer, TensorRT backend sunset, MoE load balancing, FP8 cache in LMDeploy, multi-token drafting on Apple silicon

Summary: June was about moving cache between machines and retiring old paths.

The month cache stopped being local

For most of this series, the KV cache lived in one process on one machine.

That changes the shape of the system. A central store is simple to reason about.

The same month also brought the opposite move in the engine world.

Taken together, these changes show that the system is being rebuilt around fewer, better-understood paths, with cache and routing treated as first-class concerns.

Peer-to-peer KV transfer in LMCache

LMCache v0.5.0 introduced full peer-to-peer support for multiprocess mode.source ↗

Why would a team want this? Consider a fleet of replicas serving the same system prompt and the same retrieved documents.

A useful way to frame the decision is as a cost comparison per request.

The break-even point is the important number. Recomputing a prefix costs compute time that scales with prefix length.

Peer transfer also raises a trust and correctness question. A block fetched from a peer must match the model, the tokenizer, and the cache layout of the requesting instance.

The TensorRT backend sunset

TensorRT-LLM's v1.3.0rc20 release candidate, dated June 30, begins with a notice in bold: this release candidate will be the last one supporting the TensorRT backend, and the backend will be removed in the next version.source ↗

Backends in inference engines often exist for historical reasons.

The practical response is to inventory which backend each deployment uses, and to set a migration deadline before the removal lands.

Read the notice alongside the candidate's other changes. The same release candidate changed the request chat template to be opt-in, a breaking API change in the notes.

MoE load balancing

SGLang v0.5.14 highlighted two MoE load-balancing methods, Waterfill and LPLB.source ↗

Load balancing can happen in two places. Static placement decides which experts live on which GPUs, based on expected load.

The measurement that matters is the spread of work across GPUs at each step, not just the average.

The same logic applies to vLLM v0.23.0's work on DeepSeek V4. The release added expert parallel load balancing for its Mega-MoE path, along with a TRTLLM-gen attention kernel and sparse multi-head latent attention metadata that no longer depends on the DeepSeek V3.2 path.

The v0.23.0 notes contain one change that matters for long-context teams: selective prefix-cache retention for sliding-window KV cache.

The DeepSeek V4 entries across v0.22.0, v0.23.0, and v0.24.0 show a steady hardening pattern: first support, then patch releases, then backend-specific kernels and load balancing.

The v0.24.0 MiniMax-M3 entry also mentions a fix for a MiniMax-M2 performance regression.

Why January matters more than it looks

Most months in inference engineering produce a long list of model support announcements.

It also helps to know who is building these changes. Most of the core work in this list comes from open-source maintainers who work across companies, with large model labs and inference-focused startups contributing kernels, model support, and benchmarks.

So this section spends most of its length on the mechanisms.

Theme 1: stop the GPU from waiting for the CPU

To see why asynchronous scheduling matters, you need the basic loop of an LLM server.

Asynchronous scheduling overlaps the two. While the GPU runs step N, the CPU prepares step N plus one.

Overlapping is hard when the next step depends on the result of the current one.

Check it on your own system: profile one decode step and measure the gap between the end of one GPU kernel batch and the start of the next. If the gap is a meaningful fraction of the step, asynchronous scheduling is directly relevant. If the gap is tiny, the feature will barely change your numbers.

Theme 2: send each request where its cache already lives

The SGLang gateway release is a clean example of routing treated as a cache problem.source ↗

Cache-aware routing fixes this by tracking which prefixes each worker holds and sending requests accordingly.

Concepts you need for this series

Every month leans on the same small set of ideas. January touches nearly all of them, so it is a good place to pin them down.

Prefill and decode

An LLM request has two phases. Prefill processes the whole input prompt in one pass and produces the KV cache for every input token.

The metrics follow the phases. Time to first token is mostly prefill cost plus queueing.

KV cache and PagedAttention

The KV cache stores the keys and values for every token in the context, so decode does not recompute attention over the whole history at each step.

PagedAttention, introduced by Woosuk Kwon and colleagues in "Efficient Memory Management for Large Language Model Serving with PagedAttention" (arXiv 2309.06180), splits the cache into fixed-size blocks managed like virtual memory pages.

Continuous batching

Static batching waits for a full batch to finish before starting another, so short requests sit idle behind long ones.

Prefix caching

When many requests share a prefix, such as a long system prompt or a retrieved document, the KV cache for that prefix can be computed once and reused.

Agent workloads fit well. Each turn resends a growing conversation, so most of each prompt repeats the previous turn.

Speculative decoding

Decode is sequential, so each output token normally costs one full pass through the large model.

Disaggregation

Disaggregated serving runs prefill and decode, or other stages such as the vision encoder, on separate workers.

KV cache tiers and offload

GPU memory is the scarcest resource, so the cache does not have to stay there.

A worked memory budget

Concepts stick better with numbers, so take a simple budget.

That arithmetic explains almost every design choice in this series.

Two details change the arithmetic. Models with grouped-query or multi-query attention store far fewer KV heads than attention heads, which is why modern open models are cheaper to serve per token than their parameter count suggests.

How to read a release note

Release notes are written for maintainers and early adopters, and they mix three kinds of information.

A useful habit is to read the breaking changes section before the highlights, and the exclusion list before the feature.

Benchmarks in notes need the same care. A router microbenchmark measures operations per second on a synthetic tree.

Finally, watch for the word "default." Defaults decide what most users run, and they change without much fanfare.

What to check this month

  1. Measure your CPU gap. Profile a decode step and check how much time the GPU is idle between kernel batches. If the gap is large, compare throughput with and without async scheduling.
  2. Check your exact combination. If you run speculative decoding with pipeline parallelism, confirm the release notes list your combination as supported. Before v0.15.0 it was on the exclusion list.
  3. Test the strict sampling change. vLLM v0.14.0 makes unsupported sampling parameters fail under speculative decoding. Run your integration tests before upgrading production.
  4. Replay routing on real traffic. Send a day of prompts through round robin and a prefix-aware policy. Compare realized cache hit rates and time to first token, not the router's own counters.
  5. Measure prefix repetition in your agents. Count how much of each turn's prompt repeats the previous turn. That number tells you how much prefix caching and offload can save.
  6. Pin engine versions. Track latest in staging, not production. TensorRT-LLM candidates in particular are still changing.

Frequently asked questions

Is asynchronous scheduling always faster?

No. The gain is largest when CPU scheduling time is a real fraction of each step, which happens at moderate batch sizes and fast models. With very large batches where the GPU is saturated, there is little idle time to recover. Measure on your own workload, and keep --no-async-scheduling in mind if something misbehaves.

Do I need the SGLang gateway for cache-aware routing?

You need some router that tracks where prefixes are cached. The SGLang gateway is one implementation. A simpler version uses consistent hashing on a stable prompt prefix. That needs no tracking state, but it cannot adapt when one prefix becomes very popular.

Should a small team care about any of this?

Yes, mainly for the concepts. A team running one engine on one GPU already gets continuous batching and prefix caching from the engine. Routing and pipeline changes matter once you scale to several workers or very long contexts. The vocabulary also makes the release notes faster to read.

Why does an inference series include Ollama?

Many developers run models locally through it, and its direction shows where local inference is heading. The agent loop in v0.14.0 and ollama launch in v0.15.0 change the workload profile. Agent traffic is many short sequential requests with growing context, which stresses prefix caching more than single-shot chat does.

Common mistakes in 2025

The first mistake of the year was assuming a rewrite would be fast. The V1 work went from a testable alpha in January to a default in March to a full removal of V0 in October. Teams that planned a short migration found that the old and new engines coexisted for much longer than they expected. Plan migrations in quarters, and keep the old path available until the new one has been tested on your workload. A migration plan that names an owner, a test, and a rollback step for each stage is the difference between a smooth year and an emergency every quarter.

The second mistake was treating a release-candidate or alpha label as a signal to stop checking. Pre-release builds carry the most change per week, and a team that adopted one without a test matrix had to absorb every change as it arrived. A pre-release is a place to test, not a place to depend on.

The third mistake was confusing a gateway with a cache. The SGLang gateway shipped in late 2025 as a routing layer, and teams that expected it to manage cache state directly were disappointed until the later routing work arrived. Know which component owns which decision before you write the architecture down.

The fourth mistake was ignoring the storage layer when upgrading the engine. LMCache and Mooncake changed their interfaces during the year, and an engine upgrade that did not also upgrade the store could break the cache path silently. Version the storage layer with the engine, and run a cache correctness test after every change to either.

The fifth mistake was running local models without pinning. Ollama's pace of 59 releases in 2025 meant that a model that answered one way in January could answer slightly differently in September. Reproducible local results require a pinned runtime and a recorded model digest.

Concepts 2025 made standard

Four concepts moved from research papers into release notes in 2025. The first is the V1 scheduler and runner: a rewrite that separates the scheduling loop from the model execution, which later enabled the asynchronous and overlap work of 2026. The second is cache offload, the movement of cold blocks to host memory and disk, which appeared in LMDeploy and was built into the LMCache and Mooncake layers.

Each of these is a concept the earlier sections of this page assumed.

What 2025 teaches an operator

  1. A default flip is the largest change an operator can receive. The V1 default in March 2025 touched every user who had not pinned a setting. Keep a record of the settings you rely on, and review it at every major release.
  2. Staged removal is the norm for mature projects. The V0 removal ran from July to October. Plan migrations around the removal notices, not the deletion dates.
  3. Stable interfaces are worth more than fast ones. TensorRT-LLM's 1.0 label is a contract. Pin to the stable line when the interface matters to your application.
  4. Pin local runners as carefully as servers. Fifty-nine Ollama releases in a year means output can shift between builds.
  5. Treat storage as part of the engine. LMCache and Mooncake changed how cache moves between machines. Test cache correctness whenever you change the storage layer.

Three themes of 2024, in depth

Three themes run through the 2024 releases, and each one set up something in 2025 and 2026. The first is scheduling overhead.

The second theme is the arrival of very large open models. DeepSeek V3 pushed engines to handle mixture-of-experts layouts, multi-head latent attention, and cache layouts that differed from standard attention.

The third theme is routing and load balancing across replicas.

The three themes connect. Lower scheduler overhead makes each replica faster.

A deeper look at the 2024 concepts

Multi-step scheduling trades latency granularity for overhead.

A zero-overhead scheduler is a different design. Rather than running less often, it runs the scheduling work in parallel with the GPU work for the current step, so the time spent scheduling is hidden.

Multi-head latent attention reduces the size of the cache by projecting keys and values into a smaller latent space, and reconstructing them when needed.

Cache-aware load balancing is the simplest of the three to state and the hardest to get right.

Concepts 2024 made practical

Three concepts moved from papers into engines in 2024. The first is multi-step scheduling, which amortizes the scheduler's cost across several decode steps.

A fourth concept appeared in the DeepSeek releases: a new attention variant, multi-head latent attention, with a cache layout different from standard attention.

What 2024 teaches an operator

  1. Test batching changes against your own load. A 2x throughput claim measured against an earlier version is a relative figure. Measure the change on your traffic before rolling it out.
  2. Wait for the first patch on a major release. The patch cadence of 2024 shows that first versions often carry bugs that users find quickly.
  3. Read licence changes as operational changes. TGI's licence reversal in April 2024 changed what companies could do with the project, and the notes stated it in the first highlight.
  4. Pin local runners. Sixty-eight Ollama releases in a year is a fast pace, and results depend on the build.
  5. Check day-one model support against the model's real architecture. A new attention variant needs engine support before the model runs well, and the notes will say whether it is there.

Was 2024 a year of big architectural rewrites?

Less than 2025. Most engines added features and kept their structure, and the rewrites came in 2025 with V1 in vLLM and the PyTorch architecture in TensorRT-LLM. Multi-step scheduling, cache-aware load balancing, and the first multi-head latent attention support are all still in use. The 2026 months describe their refinements.

Common mistakes in 2024

The first mistake was adopting a model on day one without checking the engine's support for its architecture. A new attention variant can run on an engine that handles it poorly, and the throughput numbers will reflect that. Check the release notes for the specific model before committing capacity.

The second mistake was comparing throughput numbers across setups. A 2x figure against v0.5.3 and a 7x figure for an attention variant are not comparable. Each needs its own configuration, and a team should reproduce the one that matches its traffic.

The third mistake was treating a fast-moving local runner as stable. Sixty-eight Ollama releases in one year means that a result from March may not reproduce in July without pinning the build.

The fourth mistake was ignoring the difference between a feature and a default. Multi-step scheduling in vLLM v0.6.0 was recommended rather than turned on for every user, and teams that did not try it left the gain on the table.

What 2023 teaches an operator

  1. Read first-release notes as scope, not performance. A first release says what the project supports first. Check that scope against your models before you plan around it.
  2. Profile the non-GPU work. vLLM's 2023 v0.2.0 gain came from de-tokenization and sampling. Measure where your loop spends time before assuming the GPU is the limit.
  3. Check quantized formats for speed separately from support. A format that loads may not be fast until kernels arrive. Measure both.

Frequently asked questions

Why does 2023 have so little performance data?

Because the projects were still establishing their interfaces and scope. Performance became a headline in 2024, when the engines had enough users to compare. The 2023 notes are better read for scope and design choices.

Is the 60 percent claim in vLLM v0.2.0 useful for planning?

It is useful as a sign of where the 2023 cost was, and as a relative gain against the earlier version. It does not describe a setup you can reproduce without the configuration, which the release does not fully list.

Why does TGI's licence change matter a decade later?

Because the licence decides what teams can do with the software, and it changed twice in this data: restricted in v1.0 and reverted to Apache 2.0 in v2.0. A team that tracked the history knew which versions it could use under which terms.

Should an early Ollama build be used in production?

Not in the sense of this series, where production means a pinned, tested build with a rollback plan. The 2023 Ollama builds were built for developers and were changing quickly.

Common mistakes in 2023

The first mistake was reading an early release as a performance guarantee. A first release shows scope, and the performance work often came in later releases. Checking the date and the scope prevents a lot of disappointment.

The second mistake was assuming a supported format was a fast format. AWQ support in vLLM v0.2.0 meant the models could load, not that they ran at full speed. Teams that expected speed from day one found the gap.

The third mistake was upgrading across a licence change without review. TGI v1.0 changed the licence, and an upgrade that did not check it changed the terms of use without anyone noticing.

The fourth mistake was leaving a cluster dashboard exposed. The 2023 Ray dashboard fixes are a reminder that a management interface needs the same access control as any other sensitive service.

The 2023 year also shows how much the vocabulary has changed. Terms that now appear in every engine's documentation, such as prefill, decode, continuous batching, and speculative decoding, were either absent from the 2023 notes or used in a narrower sense.
