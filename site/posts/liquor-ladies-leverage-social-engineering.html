Only 3 Ways a Smart Person Gets Hacked: Liquor, Ladies and Leverage.

security social-engineering human-factor counterintelligence insider-risk

Summary: an old investing line says a smart person can only go broke three ways: liquor, ladies and leverage. A post quoting it went around this week next to the Nvidia spy story, and it turns out to be a surprisingly good model of social engineering. Read as security advice, the three words mean your state of mind, your attachments, and the hold someone has over you. This article spends very little time on the news and most of its time on the human side: why intelligent people are the easiest targets, which mental shortcuts attackers press on, how a slow recruitment actually works step by step, what the warning signs look like, and what individuals and companies can do. Historical cases are collapsed at the end as extra reading.

1. The quote, and why it is a security model

The line "there are only three ways a smart person can go broke: liquor, ladies and leverage" is usually credited to the investor Charlie Munger, passed along by Warren Buffett. Buffett has joked that the first two were only added because they start with L, and that the real killer is leverage, meaning borrowed money.

A social media post repeating the line spread this week because of the Nvidia story (covered briefly in the next section). The joke lands because everyone can see the pattern. But if you work in security, the line is more than a joke. Change one word and you have a threat model:

There are only three ways a smart person gets compromised: liquor, ladies and leverage.

Here is the translation this article uses throughout.

The wordWhat it really means in securityPlain example
LiquorState. Anything that lowers your judgment in the moment: alcohol, tiredness, jet lag, stress, hurry, excitement, flattery.You approve a payment at 11 pm because the message said "urgent".
LadiesAttachment. Any relationship that makes you want to please someone. Romance is one form. Friendship, mentorship and admiration work the same way. It applies to every gender.You share a roadmap with a "recruiter" who has been kind to you for months.
LeverageHold. Something the other side can use to push you: a secret, a debt, a favour you owe, a job you want, a career you could lose.You keep helping because stopping would mean admitting what you already did.
A note on the word "ladies". The quote is from another era. In real cases the person doing the approaching is a man about as often as a woman, and the bait is as likely to be a job offer or a friendship as a romance. East Germany famously sent men, nicknamed Romeo spies, to charm women working as secretaries in West German ministries. Read the word as "attachment" and the model works for everyone.

Whether or not the allegation turns out to be true, the story is useful for one reason. It shows the shape of a threat that most security training ignores. No firewall was bypassed. No password was phished. The claimed attack surface was a person's private life, and the claimed prize was not a file but closeness to someone who shapes policy.

That is social engineering at its oldest and slowest. The rest of this article is about how it works on ordinary people, because the same playbook is used every day against engineers, finance clerks, help desk staff and system administrators.

3. What social engineering actually is

Social engineering is getting a person to do something that helps an attacker, by using psychology instead of technical exploits. The person might hand over a password, click a link, approve a payment, hold a door open, introduce the attacker to someone, or simply talk too much.

Three ideas are worth fixing in your head early.

  • It attacks trust, not software. Every organisation runs on people trusting each other. You cannot patch that away, because without trust nothing gets done. The attacker borrows the trust you give colleagues, friends and partners.
  • It runs at two speeds. The fast kind takes minutes: a phishing email, a fake support call. The slow kind takes months or years: a friendship, a romance, a business relationship. The fast kind is common. The slow kind is rare, patient and aimed at people who matter.
  • The victim usually cooperates willingly. This is the uncomfortable part. Most victims are not forced. They are helpful, polite, curious, flattered or in love. They do what a good person would do, for someone who turned out to be lying.

Recent editions of Verizon's Data Breach Investigations Report put the share of breaches involving a human element at roughly 60 to 70 percent. The exact number changes each year and depends on definitions, but the message is steady. Most break-ins start with a person, not a zero-day.

4. Why smart people are the best targets

The quote says smart person on purpose. It is tempting to think that only careless or foolish people fall for manipulation. The opposite is closer to the truth, for five reasons.

4.1 Smart people have what attackers want

Attackers go where the access is. The senior engineer has production credentials. The finance head can release money. The founder knows the roadmap. The executive can change a decision with one phone call. Nobody spends six months building a fake friendship with someone who has nothing to give.

4.2 Confidence is a blind spot

People who are good at one thing tend to assume they are good at spotting lies. Research on lie detection is humbling here: most people, including trained professionals, do only a little better than a coin flip. The belief "I would notice" is exactly what stops you from checking.

4.3 Intelligence is good at building excuses

When something feels slightly off, a clever mind is very good at explaining it away. "She asks a lot about my work because she is in the same industry." "He wants to move to another chat app because the first one is buggy." Psychologists call this motivated reasoning. The smarter you are, the better your excuses.

4.4 Success brings isolation

The higher someone rises, the fewer people tell them no, and the fewer people they can talk to honestly. Lonely, busy, admired people are easier to approach than they look. Someone who offers warmth without asking for anything is rare in their life, and that makes them stand out.

4.5 Rules feel like they are for other people

Security policies, disclosure rules and conflict-of-interest forms are often designed for staff and quietly skipped by leaders. An attacker loves an organisation where the person with the most access has the least oversight.

The key idea: being a target is not an insult. It is a description of your access. If you can touch money, code, data, or decisions, someone has a reason to work on you. Accepting that calmly is the first real defence.

5. The human operating system and its known bugs

Human brains use shortcuts. We could not get through a day otherwise. We trust people in uniform, return favours, follow the crowd and hurry when told something is running out. These shortcuts work well most of the time. A social engineer simply triggers them on purpose.

The psychologist Robert Cialdini described the main shortcuts in his book Influence. Almost every con ever run is a mix of these seven.

PrincipleThe shortcut in your headHow an attacker uses itCounter-move
Reciprocity"They did something for me, I owe them."Gifts, dinners, free advice, an introduction, a small favour first.Notice when a favour arrives before a request. You may thank someone without repaying in access.
Commitment"I already said yes once, I should stay consistent."Starts with a tiny harmless ask, then grows it step by step.Judge each request on its own. Past yeses do not bind you.
Social proof"Others trust this person, so it must be fine."Mutual contacts, photos with well-known people, many connections.Mutual contacts are not vetting. Ask the mutual contact directly.
Authority"This person is senior or official, I should comply."Pretends to be the CEO, IT, a bank, the police, a regulator.Verify through a channel you chose, not one they gave you.
Liking"I like this person, they are like me."Shared hometown, same hobbies, compliments, charm, attraction.Liking someone is fine. Let it change your mood, not your access rules.
Scarcity"This will be gone soon, act now."Deadlines, "last chance", "only you can help", "do it before the audit".Treat pressure to hurry as a reason to slow down.
Unity"This person is one of us."Same school, same country, same faith, same company, "us against them".Shared identity is not shared loyalty.

On top of these sit a few more weaknesses that matter a lot in long operations.

  • Truth bias. We assume people are telling the truth unless given a strong reason not to. Society needs this. Liars depend on it.
  • Ego and the wish to be seen. Experts love talking about their work. Ask a proud engineer a slightly wrong question and they will correct you with the right answer. Intelligence officers call the gentle art of getting information through ordinary conversation elicitation.
  • Loneliness. Attention is a powerful reward. A person who feels unseen at home or at work will give a great deal to someone who listens.
  • Shame. Once someone has done something embarrassing, they hide it. Hiding it is what gives the other side power.
  • Sunk cost. After months invested in a relationship, admitting it was fake is painful. Many victims defend the attacker even after being shown evidence.

Fig 1. The three L model. Each one alone is manageable. The danger is when all three line up on the same person at the same time.

6. Liquor: attacks on your state of mind

The first L is not really about drinking. It is about the fact that your judgment is not constant. The careful person who reviews every pull request at 10 am is a different decision-maker at midnight in a hotel bar after a 14-hour flight.

Attackers do not need you to be foolish. They need you to be foolish for five minutes. So they pick the moment.

6.1 States that lower your guard

StateWhy it worksWhere it shows up
AlcoholLowers inhibition, loosens talk, weakens memory of what was said.Conference parties, client dinners, banquets, golf trips.
Tiredness and jet lagThe careful, slow part of thinking is the first to switch off.Business travel, on-call nights, release weeks.
HurryNo time to check means no checking."Wire it before 5 pm", "the CEO is boarding a plane".
FearPanic narrows attention to the threat in front of you."Your account is locked", "legal action today".
ExcitementGood news switches off suspicion as well as bad news does.Dream job offers, big deals, sudden attention from someone impressive.
Being away from homeNormal routines and the people who would notice are absent.Trade shows, offsites, long postings abroad.

6.2 Why conferences and dinners are the classic hunting ground

Industry events stack every state in that table at once. You are travelling, tired, slightly drunk, wearing a badge with your name and employer, surrounded by friendly strangers who share your interests, and in a mood to talk about your work. Meeting new people is the whole point of being there, so an approach does not look like an approach.

This is why government travel briefings spend so much time on hotels, bars and hospitality. It is not prudishness. It is that the environment is built for lowered guard.

6.3 The same trick at keyboard speed

A phishing email is the "liquor" attack done in thirty seconds. It manufactures a state, usually hurry or fear, and then asks for an action before the state passes. That is why so many of them arrive on Friday afternoon, at month end, or during an outage.

Defence in one line: make important decisions only in a good state. A simple check used in other fields is HALT: if you are Hungry, Angry, Lonely or Tired, do not decide now. Add "drunk" and "rushed" and you have a complete rule for approvals, access and oversharing.

7. Ladies: attacks through attachment

The second L is the one in the headlines, and the one people least like to discuss honestly. It covers any case where a relationship is built, or used, to get something from you.

7.1 The forms it takes

  • The honeytrap. A romantic or sexual relationship started for a purpose. The purpose might be information, access, influence, or simply material for later pressure.
  • The cultivated friend. No romance at all. Someone who shares your interests, remembers your children's names, and is always glad to hear about your week. Far more common than the honeytrap and far harder to notice.
  • The admirer. A junior person, a journalist, a student or a fan who makes you feel important and asks you to explain things.
  • The helpful contact. A recruiter, consultant, investor or customer whose business reason for talking to you is perfectly normal.
  • The online romance. The mass-market version. Dating app matches that move to private chat and, weeks later, to investment advice or a request for money. Often called pig butchering.

7.2 Why attachment works so well

Affection changes whose side you think you are on. Once you care about someone, their questions stop feeling like questions from an outsider. Telling them about your day is not "disclosing information". It is just talking. Helping them is not "granting access". It is just being kind.

Attachment also bundles several Cialdini principles together. There is liking, reciprocity (they have been good to you), commitment (you have invested months), and unity (the two of you against the world). Very few controls survive that combination.

7.3 The hardest case: mixed relationships

People imagine a honeytrap as a stranger in a bar. The harder case is when the personal relationship sits on top of a real business one. A customer, a supplier, a partner company's executive. Now there is a legitimate reason to meet, to travel together, to talk about products and plans. Every individual meeting can be explained.

This is the pattern alleged in the Nvidia story: a person who was reportedly a senior figure at a customer company. Whatever the facts turn out to be, the structure is worth learning. A relationship that is both commercial and personal has two cover stories, each hiding the other.

7.4 It does not have to be fake to be dangerous

One more uncomfortable point. The feelings may be real on both sides. A person can be genuinely fond of someone and still be passing things along, sometimes under pressure themselves. A real relationship with a person who is controlled or pressured by someone else carries the same risk as a staged one. So the question to ask is never "is this love real?" It is "what does this relationship give access to, and who else might benefit?"

Not paranoia. None of this means you should distrust everyone who is nice to you. Nearly everyone who befriends you is exactly who they seem. The skill is narrower: notice when a relationship keeps bending toward the things you are trusted to protect.

8. Leverage: attacks through a hold over you

Buffett's joke was that leverage is the one that really ruins people, and the same is true here. Liquor and attachment open the door. Leverage keeps it open.

In finance, leverage means borrowed money: it multiplies what you can do and also what you can lose. In social engineering, leverage means anything that makes it costly for you to say no.

8.1 Kinds of leverage

KindWhat it looks likeHow it is used
SecretAn affair, an addiction, a past mistake, a hidden side business.Open blackmail is rare. More often the secret is never mentioned and simply makes the victim avoid reporting anything.
MoneyDebt, gambling losses, a lifestyle above income, a consulting fee.Paid "reports" or advisory work that slowly drift into confidential material.
FavourA gift, a job for a relative, help with a visa, an introduction.Creates a feeling of debt that is repaid in information or decisions.
AmbitionA promised job, board seat, investment or deal.The target behaves as if already on the other side's team.
ComplicityThe victim has already broken a rule once."You already sent the first file. Stopping now helps nobody."
ReputationThe harm from exposure exceeds the harm from continuing.The more senior the person, the larger this lever.

8.2 MICE: why people cross the line

Intelligence services have a simple old checklist for why people agree to help the other side. It is called MICE.

  • Money. Need or greed.
  • Ideology. Belief in a cause, a country, or a grievance against an employer.
  • Compromise (or coercion). A secret or a threat.
  • Ego. The wish to feel important, clever, appreciated.

Experienced officers say ego is the most underrated. A person who feels overlooked at work and is treated as brilliant by an outsider will often give more than someone who is paid. A later model, sometimes called RASCLS, replaces MICE with Cialdini's principles (reciprocation, authority, scarcity, commitment, liking, social proof), on the view that people are recruited through ordinary persuasion more often than through weakness.

8.3 The leverage you build yourself

The most important thing to understand about leverage is that the victim usually creates it. Nobody blackmails you with an affair you did not have or a relationship you disclosed. The moment something is hidden, it becomes a handle. The moment it is known to the people who need to know, the handle is gone.

This is the single most practical lesson in the article: secrecy is the attack surface. A reported mistake is an incident. A hidden mistake is a lever. Every good insider-risk programme is built around making it safe to report early.

9. How a slow approach actually works, step by step

Long social engineering operations follow a pattern so regular that intelligence services teach it as a cycle. Knowing the stages is useful because each has its own warning signs.

Fig 2. The recruitment cycle. The same six stages describe a spy recruiting a source, a scammer grooming a victim, and an attacker working a help desk over several calls.

9.1 Spotting

The attacker looks for people with access. Today this takes an afternoon. LinkedIn lists your employer, title, team and projects. Conference programmes list speakers. GitHub shows what you commit to. Company blogs name the people who own each system.

9.2 Assessing

Next comes the question: what does this person want, and what do they lack? Are they proud, bored, in debt, recently divorced, passed over for promotion, new to the country, looking for a job? Social media answers most of this. People publish their hobbies, travel, family, complaints and ambitions for free.

9.3 Developing

Contact is made in a way that looks like chance or normal business: a conference chat, a connection request, a shared friend, a sales meeting. Then follows a long period in which the attacker asks for nothing. They are simply good company. This is the stage victims remember fondly, and the stage where suspicion would feel rude.

9.4 The first small ask

The first request is designed to be easy to grant. An opinion on the industry. A public document. An introduction. A short paid "consulting" note. It matters less for what it delivers than for what it establishes: you are now a person who helps this person.

9.5 Escalation

Requests grow slowly. Each is only a little past the last one. At some point a line is crossed, usually without drama, and the victim realises they have done something they could not comfortably explain to their employer. From here, the hold exists.

9.6 Handling

The relationship is maintained. Pressure is rarely needed and rarely spoken. The victim's own wish to avoid exposure does the work.

Why it is so hard to see from inside: no single step looks wrong. The danger is only visible in the direction of travel. That is why an outside view, a colleague or a security contact you can talk to, catches what you cannot.

10. What the attacker actually wants (it is rarely "the secret file")

When a story like the Nvidia one breaks, the first question people ask is "were any secrets stolen?" It is a reasonable question, and a narrow one. A person close to a decision-maker can be valuable in at least five ways, and only one of them involves taking documents.

GoalWhat it meansWhy it is hard to detect
InformationDocuments, designs, credentials, plans.The easiest to detect. Leaves logs.
InsightWhat the person thinks, worries about, plans to do next, who they trust.Passed in conversation. No log exists.
InfluenceNudging the target's views or decisions in a useful direction.Looks identical to an honest opinion from a friend.
AccessIntroductions to others, invitations to rooms, a place in the network.Networking is normal and encouraged.
Leverage for laterNothing is used now. The material is kept.Nothing happens, so there is nothing to see.

This is why "we have no evidence that secrets were compromised" is a true and incomplete sentence. An influence operation does not steal anything. It changes what a powerful person believes or does, and from the outside that is nearly impossible to separate from the person simply holding that view.

It also explains why security people say that nobody at the top has a private life in the old sense. A leader's opinions are an asset. Whoever has their ear has a piece of that asset.

11. The same playbook at everyday scale

Most readers will never be the target of a multi-year operation. All of us are targets of the fast version, which uses the same three Ls compressed into minutes.

Two changes make this worse than it was five years ago. First, AI tools can now write a perfect, personal message in any language and hold a patient conversation for months at almost no cost. The slow, expensive kind of approach is becoming cheap. Second, voice and video can be faked well enough that "I heard them say it" is no longer proof.

12. Warning signs

No single sign proves anything. Three or four together deserve a quiet conversation with someone you trust.

12.1 In a relationship or new contact

  • The meeting was a lucky coincidence, and they turned out to share an unusual number of your interests.
  • They are more interested in your work than most people are, and their questions get more specific over time.
  • They ask opinions about your employer's plans, people, or problems, not just about you.
  • Their own background is thin, hard to check, or changes in small ways.
  • They prefer private channels, disappearing messages, or meeting away from people you know.
  • They are generous early: gifts, travel, introductions, paid "advisory" work for little effort.
  • They encourage a feeling that the two of you understand each other better than your colleagues or family do.
  • You notice you have not mentioned them to people who would normally know.

The last one is the best single test. If you find yourself hiding a contact, ask what you already know that makes you hide it.

12.2 In a message or call

  • Pressure to act now, combined with a reason you cannot check right now.
  • A request to skip the normal process "just this once".
  • A request for secrecy: "do not tell the team yet".
  • A change of channel: "I am on my personal number today".
  • Anything involving codes, passwords, gift cards, crypto, or new bank details.
  • A voice or face you know, on a call you did not expect, asking for something unusual.

13. Defences for individuals

You cannot stop being human, and you should not try. The aim is to keep being warm and helpful while making a few things non-negotiable.

  1. Accept that you are a target. If you have access, someone has a reason. This is the opposite of paranoia. It lets you stay relaxed because you are not surprised.
  2. Separate liking from access. You can enjoy someone's company completely and still never discuss unreleased work, credentials, internal problems or colleagues' details with them. Decide the line in advance, when nobody is asking.
  3. Slow down when pushed. Urgency from someone else is not your emergency. A real request survives a ten-minute check.
  4. Verify on your own channel. Call back on a number you already had. Open the site by typing the address. Ask a question only the real person could answer.
  5. Agree a family and team safe word. A simple shared word defeats most cloned-voice calls.
  6. Reduce what the internet says about you. Trim job details, team names, travel plans and family information. Every item is an opening line for someone.
  7. Watch your state. No approvals, no access grants and no shop talk when drunk, exhausted or rushed. At events, decide your limits before the first drink.
  8. Keep no secrets that could be used against you. Where you cannot avoid having one, tell the person or team who needs to know before someone else does.
  9. Use phishing-resistant sign-in. Passkeys or hardware security keys mean that even if you are fooled, the stolen login is useless. Good controls forgive human error.
  10. Report early and report small. "Someone odd asked me about X at the conference" is a useful report. You do not need proof.

14. Defences for organisations

Awareness posters do not stop a patient human attacker. Design does.

14.1 Design so that one fooled person is not enough

  • Two-person rules for payments, bank detail changes, production access, key ceremonies and bulk data exports.
  • Least privilege and short-lived access, so that the person who gets fooled does not hold everything.
  • Call-back verification written into finance and help desk procedures, with no exception for seniority.
  • Phishing-resistant MFA everywhere, and strict identity proofing before any help desk reset.

14.2 Apply the rules at the top first

The people with the most access and influence are usually the least monitored, the most public, and the most likely to be excused from policy. That is backwards. Executives and board members need:

  • Personal security briefings, especially before travel and major events.
  • A clear rule on disclosing close personal relationships with customers, suppliers, investors, regulators and foreign officials.
  • A board-level owner for counterintelligence risk, so that the question does not depend on the CEO raising it about themselves.
  • Independent review of unusually large or fast-growing customers and partners, separate from the people who own those relationships.

14.3 Make reporting safe

This matters more than every tool on the market. If admitting "I think I was approached" or "I made a mistake" ends careers, people will hide it, and hiding it is what gives the attacker power. Practical steps:

  • A confidential channel to a named person, not a generic inbox.
  • A stated policy that early self-reporting is treated as a security contribution, not a confession.
  • Managers who thank the person who reports a clicked link.
  • Separate handling of personal matters from security matters, so that one does not automatically become a disciplinary case about the other.

14.4 Train for the real thing

  • Teach elicitation and the recruitment cycle, not only "hover over the link".
  • Brief staff before conferences and overseas travel, and debrief afterwards.
  • Run realistic exercises on the help desk and finance teams, including voice calls.
  • Screen remote hires properly: live video, identity checks, device shipping address checks.
  • Watch for the human signals of insider risk (sudden wealth, unexplained travel, unusual interest in systems outside one's role) with care and privacy, not suspicion of everyone.

15. If you think you are being worked

Suppose something in this article sounded familiar. Here is the order to do things in.

  1. Do not confront the person. It warns them and gains you nothing.
  2. Do not suddenly vanish either. A sharp change in behaviour also warns them. Simply stop sharing anything new.
  3. Write down what you remember. How you met, dates, what they asked, what you told them, how you communicate.
  4. Tell your security team or a trusted senior person. Early and honestly, including the embarrassing parts. Those parts are exactly what they need.
  5. Keep the messages. Do not delete chats or emails. They are evidence and they help measure what was exposed.
  6. If you are being threatened, do not pay and do not comply. Go to your security team and, where appropriate, the police. Paying confirms the lever works.
  7. Change what can be changed. Passwords, access, plans that were discussed.

The cost of reporting feels enormous in the moment and is almost always smaller than the cost of waiting. Every month of silence makes the hold stronger and the eventual story worse.

16. Common myths

MythReality
"Only careless people fall for it."Targets are chosen for access. Careful, senior, intelligent people are picked on purpose.
"I would know if someone was lying."Almost nobody can tell reliably. Rely on verification, not instinct.
"Honeytraps are always women targeting men."Any gender, either direction, and friendship or job offers are used more often than romance.
"It is always blackmail."Open threats are rare. Affection, flattery and the victim's own shame do most of the work.

"My private life is nobody's business."True, until it overlaps with what you are trusted to protect. Then the overlap is a work matter.

17. FAQ

Is the Nvidia case proven to be a spy operation?

No. As of 10 November 2026 it rests on reporting about US intelligence assessments. China denies it, no charges have been filed, and Nvidia's board has said it has no reason to believe secrets were compromised while its own investigation runs. This article uses the story only as an example of a pattern.

What is the difference between a honeytrap and ordinary social engineering?

A honeytrap is one type of social engineering in which the relationship used is romantic or sexual. The mechanics are the same as with a fake friend or fake recruiter: build trust, make a small ask, escalate, and rely on the victim's reluctance to admit what happened.

What is elicitation?

Getting useful information through normal-seeming conversation, without the other person feeling questioned. Common tricks are saying something slightly wrong so the expert corrects you, sharing a small "secret" so they share one back, or praising their work so they explain it.

Are junior employees really at risk?

Yes. Junior staff often have more direct system access than executives, are easier to flatter, and are more likely to be job hunting. Help desk, finance operations, DevOps and executive assistants are favourite targets.

How does AI change this?

It lowers the cost. A convincing persona with photos, a work history and flawless messages can be produced in minutes, and an AI system can keep up hundreds of patient conversations at once. Voice and video cloning also removes "I recognised their voice" as a check.

Should companies ban relationships with customers or partners?

Bans mostly produce secrecy, which is the thing you are trying to avoid. A disclosure rule works better: the relationship is reported in confidence, and the person steps back from decisions involving the other party.

Does this mean I should distrust friendly strangers at conferences?

No. Be friendly. Simply keep a fixed list of things you never discuss outside the company, and stick to it however good the evening is.

What is the single most effective technical control?

Phishing-resistant sign-in, meaning passkeys or hardware security keys, plus a two-person rule on anything irreversible. Both make a fooled human much less damaging.

18. Takeaways

  • The old line about liquor, ladies and leverage is a compact threat model: state, attachment and hold.
  • Social engineering attacks trust, and trust cannot be patched. It has to be designed around.
  • Smart, senior people are the preferred targets because of what they can reach and because confidence dulls checking.
  • Slow approaches follow a cycle: spot, assess, develop, first ask, escalate, handle. Each step is small. Only the direction gives it away.
  • The prize is often insight or influence, not documents. "Nothing was stolen" does not close the question.
  • The victim usually builds the lever themselves by hiding something. Disclosure removes it.
  • For organisations: two-person rules, phishing-resistant sign-in, the same rules for the top as for everyone else, and a culture where reporting early is safe.
  • For individuals: decide your lines in advance, slow down under pressure, verify on your own channel, and notice when you start hiding a contact.

19. Additional reads: case files

Short summaries of well-known cases, collapsed to keep the main article focused. Each is tagged with the L it shows best. Details come from public reporting and court records; where a person was never charged, that is stated.

The Romeo spies of East Germany (attachment)

During the Cold War, East Germany's foreign intelligence service under Markus Wolf sent male officers to form relationships with women working as secretaries and assistants in West German ministries and NATO offices. Many of the women passed documents for years out of affection, and some only learned the truth after arrest. The lesson: the target is often not the powerful person but the person next to them who handles the paper.

Katrina Leung, "Parlor Maid" (attachment and leverage)

A Los Angeles businesswoman who worked as a paid FBI informant on China for about two decades while having long affairs with two FBI agents, one of them her handler. Prosecutors alleged she took classified material from his briefcase and passed information to China. The main case collapsed on procedural grounds and she pleaded guilty in 2005 to lesser charges. The lesson: the relationship compromised the very people whose job was to judge her reliability.

Christine Fang and local politicians (attachment and access)

Axios reported in 2020 that a Chinese national known as Fang Fang built ties with up-and-coming politicians in California between about 2011 and 2015 through fundraising, networking and, in some cases, romantic relationships. US officials believed she was working for Chinese intelligence. She left the country in 2015 and was not charged, and officials did not believe she obtained classified information. The lesson: the aim was long-term access to people before they became important.

Robin Sage, the analyst who did not exist (liking and social proof)

In 2009 and 2010 security researcher Thomas Ryan created a fake profile of an attractive young "cyber threat analyst" and connected with hundreds of people in defence, intelligence and security firms. The persona received job offers, dinner invitations and sensitive details, including from people whose work was security. The lesson: each new connection made the next person more willing to accept.

Mia Ash (attachment, then malware)

Researchers at SecureWorks described in 2017 a fake persona, a London photographer, used by a group linked to Iran. Over weeks of friendly chat on LinkedIn and Facebook with employees at targeted companies, "Mia" eventually asked a victim to open a survey document on his work computer. It carried a remote access tool. The lesson: a slow personal approach was used to deliver an ordinary technical payload.

LinkedIn recruitment: Kevin Mallory and Dickson Yeo (money and ego)

Former CIA officer Kevin Mallory, in financial difficulty, was contacted on LinkedIn in 2017 by someone posing as a think tank recruiter, travelled to Shanghai and sold classified documents. He was convicted in 2018. Separately, Singaporean Dickson Yeo pleaded guilty in 2020 to working for Chinese intelligence by using a fake consulting firm and LinkedIn to find Americans with security clearances and pay them to write reports. Britain's MI5 said in 2021 that more than 10,000 UK nationals had been approached by fake profiles over five years. The lesson: "paid consulting" is the most common first ask.

Xu Yanjun and the invited lecture (ego and reciprocity)

A Chinese Ministry of State Security officer cultivated engineers at Western aviation companies, in one case inviting a GE Aviation engineer to give a talk at a university in China with travel paid. Follow-up requests then asked for technical material. The FBI ran the contact back against him, and he was arrested in Belgium in 2018, extradited, and convicted in the US in 2021. The lesson: an invitation to speak is flattering, feels academic, and creates a small debt.

Fake recruiters and the Ronin bridge theft (ambition)

In 2022 roughly 600 million dollars in cryptocurrency was stolen from the Ronin network behind the game Axie Infinity. Reporting attributed the entry point to a fake job offer sent to a senior engineer, delivered as a PDF, in a campaign linked to North Korea's Lazarus group. The lesson: a job hunt is a state in which people open files from strangers and tell nobody at work.

The XZ Utils backdoor (attachment at open source scale)

Disclosed in March 2024. A contributor using the name "Jia Tan" spent about two years being helpful on a widely used compression library, while other accounts pressured the tired sole maintainer to share control. Once trusted, the contributor slipped in a backdoor aimed at SSH on Linux systems. An engineer noticed a half-second slowdown and caught it before wide release. The lesson: patience, helpfulness and an exhausted target, with no romance and no money involved.

Help desk calls: Twitter 2020 and MGM 2023 (state and authority)

In 2020 attackers phoned Twitter staff posing as internal IT and obtained credentials that let them take over famous accounts. In 2023 attackers reportedly called the IT help desk of MGM Resorts posing as an employee, got a reset, and caused days of disruption across hotels and casinos. The lesson: a confident voice and a plausible story still beat expensive defences when the reset process trusts the caller.

The deepfake finance meeting (authority)

In early 2024 Hong Kong police described a case, later confirmed to involve the engineering firm Arup, in which a finance employee joined a video call where the chief financial officer and colleagues were all AI-generated fakes, and then transferred about 25 million dollars. The lesson: seeing and hearing someone is no longer verification. Process is.

Fake profiles aimed at soldiers' phones (attachment)

Israel's military has said several times since 2017 that Hamas operators used fake social media profiles of young women to chat with soldiers and persuade them to install apps that were actually spyware. The lesson: the romance was only a delivery method for access to a device.
