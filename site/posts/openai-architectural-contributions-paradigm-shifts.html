From GPT to recurrent depth: OpenAI's architectural contributions and the paradigm shifts that shaped modern AI.

ml openai llm-architecture reasoning survey

This is a research survey, not a company history. Its purpose is to answer one narrow question precisely: for each major idea associated with OpenAI, did OpenAI invent it, co-invent it, meaningfully advance it, scale it to frontier size, popularize an existing academic idea, or merely report — via industry journalism — to be using it? The Transformer is Google's. Diffusion models are academic (Sohl-Dickstein, Ho et al. at Google/Berkeley). Chain-of-thought prompting is Google's (Wei et al.). Mixture-of-Experts, RAG, and the Universal Transformer are not OpenAI ideas. What OpenAI did — repeatedly, across a decade — was take an underexplored axis of scale (RL environment interaction, parameter count, in-context adaptation, human preference data, tool use, test-time compute) and demonstrate what happens when you push it further than anyone had before. That pattern is the article's actual subject.

Executive thesis. Modern AI capability is not the product of one architectural insight. It is better modeled as a function over several largely independent scaling axes that different labs, at different times, discovered could each be pushed: \[ \text{Capability} = f(\text{architecture}, \text{parameters}, \text{data}, \text{training compute}, \text{post-training}, \text{inference compute}, \text{tools}, \text{memory}, \text{environment interaction}) \] OpenAI's research history reads, almost in order, as a sequence of experiments pushing one axis of this function at a time — RL environment interaction (2016-2019), parameters and in-context adaptation (2018-2020), human preference data (2017, 2022), modality breadth (2020-2021), tool use (2021), and, most recently, inference-time compute (2024-2026). This article walks that sequence, is explicit about credit at every step, and ends with the least-settled part of the story: Astra's reported use of recurrent depth, and what that would mean if confirmed.
A note on sourcing and dates. This article uses information available as of September 2, 2026. Confirmed facts are drawn from peer-reviewed papers, arXiv preprints, and OpenAI's own technical reports. Where architecture is undisclosed (GPT-4, GPT-4o, and — critically — Astra), this article says so explicitly rather than repeating unverified numbers as fact. Astra-specific claims about recurrent depth are attributed to The Information's industry reporting, not to an OpenAI technical paper, because no such paper exists publicly as of this writing.

Part I — Before GPT: reinforcement learning

1. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), 2017 Invented by OpenAI

Before language models, OpenAI's most consequential contribution was an RL optimization algorithm, and it's worth understanding why it mattered enough to still be load-bearing infrastructure a decade later. Policy-gradient methods update a policy \(\pi_\theta\) in the direction that increases the probability of actions that led to high advantage \(A_t\). The problem: a single large gradient step can move the policy far enough that the data collected under the old policy no longer represents what the new policy would actually do — importance-sampling corrections become unreliable, and training can collapse irrecoverably. Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO, Schulman et al., 2015) fixed this with a hard KL-divergence constraint enforced via constrained optimization, which works but is expensive (second-order information) and awkward to implement.

PPO[1] (Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford, Klimov — arXiv:1707.06347) replaces TRPO's hard constraint with a simple clipped surrogate objective:

\[ L^{CLIP}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)A_t,\ \text{clip}(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon,\,1+\epsilon)\,A_t\big)\Big] \]

where \(r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}\) is the probability ratio between the new and old policy for the action actually taken. The intuition: when the advantage is positive (the action was good), the objective wants to increase \(r_t\), but the clip term caps the reward for doing so beyond \(1+\epsilon\) — there's no benefit to pushing the ratio further once it's already clipped, so the optimizer has no incentive to move the policy arbitrarily far from where the data was collected. When the advantage is negative, the same logic applies symmetrically in the other direction. The min operator means the objective always takes the more pessimistic (lower) of the clipped and unclipped estimate, which is what actually enforces the trust-region-like behavior without ever computing a KL constraint explicitly. This gets nearly all of TRPO's stability with only first-order gradient information — dramatically simpler to implement, and (critically for what came later) dramatically simpler to scale to distributed training and to novel domains that don't look like classic robotics benchmarks.

PPO's importance to modern AI has almost nothing to do with robotics. The chain that actually matters: PPO → preference-based reward optimization → RLHF → InstructGPT → early reasoning-model RL. PPO became the default optimizer for RLHF specifically because it's stable enough to fine-tune a pretrained language model against a learned, noisy reward signal without catastrophic policy collapse — a property that turned out to matter far more for aligning an 175B-parameter language model than for training a MuJoCo humanoid to walk. It is worth being explicit that PPO should not be assumed as the RL algorithm behind any specific undisclosed modern reasoning model — GRPO (DeepSeek), and other PPO variants that drop the value-function critic, have become common alternatives specifically because they remove PPO's need for a separately-trained value model, and no public source confirms which algorithm trains OpenAI's current reasoning models internally.

2. Deep reinforcement learning from human preferences, 2017 Co-developed (OpenAI + Google DeepMind)

This paper[2] is explicitly a joint effort and should never be credited to OpenAI alone: authors Paul Christiano, Jan Leike, Tom B. Brown, Miljan Martic, Shane Legg, and Dario Amodei spanned both OpenAI and DeepMind (arXiv:1706.03741). Its architecture is the direct conceptual ancestor of every modern RLHF pipeline:

human comparisons  →  preference dataset  →  reward model  →  RL optimization

Rather than requiring a hand-specified reward function — which is often either impossible to write down (what does "be helpful" mean, numerically?) or trivially exploitable once an agent optimizes hard against it — the approach asks humans to compare pairs of trajectory segments and says which one they prefer. A reward model is trained to predict these preferences, and the RL policy is then optimized against the learned reward model instead of a hand-coded one. The paper demonstrated this on Atari and simulated locomotion tasks using less than 1% of the agent's total environment interactions for feedback — a genuinely important result, because it showed reward learning could be sample-efficient enough to be practical, not just theoretically appealing.

The conceptual leap this made possible: alignment could be treated as a learned, differentiable component of the training stack, rather than an afterthought bolted onto a fixed objective. Five years later, this exact three-stage pipeline (preferences → reward model → RL) is what InstructGPT (§16) runs, just with a pretrained language model's outputs standing in for trajectory segments.

3. OpenAI Five and scaling reinforcement learning, 2018-2019 Scaled / popularized by OpenAI

OpenAI Five — a team of five neural networks that learned to play Dota 2 at a professional level — introduced no new architecture. It is included here precisely because its lesson is architectural in a different sense: it demonstrated that scaling existing algorithms along the axis of environment interaction could produce qualitatively new capability, not just quantitatively better performance on the same task. The system used PPO (§1) at a scale of roughly 180 years of self-play experience per day across thousands of CPU cores and hundreds of GPUs, with no architectural innovation beyond an LSTM policy network. What emerged — long-horizon strategic planning, team coordination, item-build curricula that tracked patch changes — was not designed in; it was a product of scale and self-play alone.

The broader philosophy this established, which is worth stating explicitly because it recurs at every later stage of this article: sufficiently scaling compute, environment interaction, and optimization can produce qualitatively new capabilities that were not explicitly engineered. This is the same philosophy that later underwrote the bet on GPT-3's scale (§8) and, arguably, the entire test-time-compute research program (§23).

Part II — The GPT paradigm

4. GPT-1 — generative pretraining, 2018 Existing idea (Transformer, Vaswani et al.) adapted by OpenAI

The Transformer architecture itself belongs entirely to Vaswani et al., "Attention Is All You Need" (Google, 2017)[3] — this is not in dispute and should never be attributed to OpenAI. What GPT-1[4] (Radford, Narasimhan, Salimans, Sutskever, 2018) contributed was a training recipe: unsupervised generative pretraining of a 12-layer decoder-only Transformer on unlabeled text, followed by lightweight discriminative fine-tuning per downstream task. The objective is standard autoregressive next-token prediction:

\[ P(x) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(x_t \mid x_{The significance is the shift this represented in NLP methodology: from task-specific architectures trained from scratch on labeled data, to one general pretrained model adapted cheaply to many tasks. GPT-1 improved the state of the art on 9 of 12 evaluated tasks with a single pretrained backbone, at a time when the dominant paradigm was still bespoke architectures per task (bidirectional LSTMs with attention for QA, different again for entailment, different again for sentiment). Why next-token prediction turned out to be such a general learning objective is itself worth pausing on: predicting the next token in arbitrary internet text requires implicitly modeling grammar, factual knowledge, reasoning patterns, and even rudimentary world models, because all of these are needed to predict what comes next in sufficiently diverse text. The objective is trivial to state and infinitely scalable in supervision (every token in every document is a training example), which is precisely what made it a viable substrate for the scaling story that follows.

5. GPT-2 — scaling generative models, 2019 Scaled / popularized by OpenAI

GPT-2[5] (Radford et al., 1.5B parameters, arXiv via OpenAI CDN) is architecturally almost identical to GPT-1 — the contribution is scale (roughly 10× the parameters, and a far larger, higher-quality 40GB web-text training corpus) and what scale revealed. The paper's central claim, "Language Models are Unsupervised Multitask Learners," is precisely descriptive: a model trained with no task-specific supervision at all began to perform summarization, translation, reading comprehension, and question answering in a zero-shot setting, purely as a byproduct of having been trained to predict next tokens over a sufficiently broad and large corpus. This was the first strong empirical signal that scale alone — no architecture change, no new objective — could unlock capabilities the model was never explicitly trained to have. GPT-2 is the bridge between "representation learning for downstream fine-tuning" (GPT-1's framing) and "foundation model" (the framing that GPT-3 and everything after operates under) — it is the paper where the field's center of gravity visibly starts to shift from architecture design toward scale as the primary lever.

Part III — Architectural efficiency

6. Sparse Transformer, 2019 Invented by OpenAI

This is a genuine, direct OpenAI architecture contribution — Child, Gray, Radford, Sutskever (arXiv:1904.10509)[6] — and deserves to be treated as one rather than folded into the "scaling" narrative. Standard self-attention computes a similarity score between every pair of positions in a sequence of length \(N\), so both its compute and memory cost scale as \(O(N^2)\). For sequences of a few thousand tokens this is manageable; for the tens-of-thousands-of-timestep sequences the paper targets (raw audio, high-resolution images, long text), quadratic cost becomes prohibitive on both time and memory.

The Sparse Transformer's fix: replace full \(N \times N\) attention with a factorization into a small number of sparse attention patterns — in the paper's main variants, a strided pattern (each position attends to a fixed stride of recent positions, capturing local structure) combined with a fixed pattern (each position also attends to a small set of summary positions that aggregate global context). Composed across layers, this reduces the attention cost to approximately \(O(N\sqrt{N})\) while still letting information propagate across the full sequence within a bounded number of layers — the same "different receptive field per layer, but propagate across depth" logic that appears again, in a completely different form, in recurrent depth (§25) and in modern sliding-window/hybrid-attention LLM stacks. The paper also introduced practical training tricks (recomputation of attention weights during the backward pass to save memory, and modified initialization for training much deeper networks) that were as important to making the idea usable as the sparsity pattern itself. Using the same architecture across text, images, and raw audio bytes, the paper set new state-of-the-art density-modeling results on Enwik8, CIFAR-10, and ImageNet-64, and explicitly demonstrated that attention over million-length sequences was possible in principle.

It's worth being precise about how this relates to later long-context work, because they solve related but distinct bottlenecks: Transformer-XL (Dai et al., 2019) tackles context-length limits via segment-level recurrence and caching, not sparsity. Longformer and BigBird (2020) generalize the sparse local+global attention pattern idea with task-specific patterns and give it a more thorough theoretical treatment (BigBird proves its sparse pattern is a universal approximator under certain conditions). FlashAttention (Dao et al., 2022) solves a different problem entirely — it doesn't change what gets attended to, it changes how the (still dense) attention computation is scheduled to minimize slow HBM reads/writes, achieving exact (non-approximate) attention faster through IO-awareness rather than through sparsity. The Sparse Transformer's contribution was being early, and being the first to show a concrete sparsity pattern that worked across modalities at meaningful scale.

Part IV — Scaling as an engineering science

7. Neural scaling laws, 2020 Invented by OpenAI later superseded in its prescriptive conclusion

Kaplan, McCandlish, Henighan, Brown, Chess, Child, Gray, Radford, Wu, Amodei — "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models," arXiv:2001.08361[7] — is one of the most consequential empirical papers in the field's history, not because any single number in it survived unrevised, but because of the methodological shift it triggered. The paper showed that cross-entropy loss \(L\) follows clean power-law relationships with parameter count \(N\), dataset size \(D\), and training compute \(C\), holding across more than seven orders of magnitude:

\[ L(N) \propto N^{-\alpha_N}, \qquad L(D) \propto D^{-\alpha_D}, \qquad L(C) \propto C^{-\alpha_C} \]

with the paper's fitted exponents implying that, for a fixed compute budget, allocating disproportionately more of that budget to parameters than to data was compute-optimal (\(N_{\text{optimal}} \propto C^{0.73}\), \(D_{\text{optimal}} \propto C^{0.27}\)) — and, notably, that architectural details like width and depth had comparatively minor effects within a broad range once total parameter count was fixed. The shift this triggered in AI research strategy is difficult to overstate: before this paper, the dominant strategy for improving a model was invent a smarter architecture. After it, a credible and empirically-grounded alternative strategy became hold architecture roughly fixed and scale parameters, data, and compute according to a predictable curve. This is the direct intellectual justification for GPT-3's 175B-parameter bet (§8).

It is essential not to present this as the final word — the paper's own prescriptive conclusion was significantly revised two years later. Hoffmann et al.'s Chinchilla paper (DeepMind, 2022)[8] showed that Kaplan et al.'s training runs were generally stopped before fully converging at each model size, which biased the fitted optimum toward oversized, undertrained models; running training to convergence at each scale instead produced a roughly 1:1 (later refined to about 20 tokens per parameter) balance between parameter and data scaling, rather than Kaplan's parameter-heavy prescription. Chinchilla itself (70B parameters, trained on 1.4T tokens) outperformed the much larger Gopher (280B) and GPT-3 (175B) at equal training compute, precisely by following this corrected allocation. The scaling-laws story is thus a genuine two-lab correction cycle — OpenAI established the empirical phenomenon and the methodology of studying it; DeepMind corrected its most consequential practical conclusion two years later. Neither should be presented as having had the final answer.

Part V — In-context learning

8. GPT-3 — few-shot and in-context learning, 2020 Scaled / popularized by OpenAI the ICL phenomenon: existing idea significantly advanced

"Language Models are Few-Shot Learners" (Brown et al., 175B parameters, arXiv:2005.14165)[9] is more important for what it revealed about model behavior than for its parameter count in isolation. GPT-3 demonstrated in-context learning: a frozen model, with no gradient update whatsoever, could be shown a handful of input-output examples in its prompt and then correctly perform a new instance of that task — an ability that scaled smoothly and, at GPT-3's scale, sometimes matched task-specific fine-tuned models. This is conceptually distinct from — and in some sense stranger than — the fine-tuning paradigm GPT-1 and GPT-2 still relied on:

\[ \underbrace{\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta \nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}}_{\text{parameter learning (fine-tuning)}} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \underbrace{P(y \mid x, \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^{k})}_{\text{in-context adaptation (no weight update)}} \]

Why a model with entirely fixed weights can "learn" a new task from a handful of examples in its context is still, as of this writing, not a fully settled question, and it's worth being precise about which explanations are established versus debated:

  • Induction heads — Olsson et al. (Anthropic, 2022)[10] provided the most mechanistically concrete and empirically supported explanation for a large fraction of in-context learning: a specific two-head circuit (one head that finds a prior occurrence of the current token, a second "induction head" that copies whatever followed it) that completes pattern [A][B]...[A] → [B]. Causal intervention experiments (ablating these heads) support a genuine causal role in in-context learning for the patterns this circuit covers. Established, with strong causal evidence, for the specific pattern-completion behaviors it targets.
  • Implicit Bayesian inference — the theoretical framing (Xie et al., 2022, and related work) that pretraining implicitly teaches the model a distribution over "concepts" or tasks, and that in-context examples act as evidence the model uses to perform approximate posterior inference over which task is being asked. Theoretically motivated and empirically supported on synthetic/controlled setups; less clearly established for open-domain natural-language ICL.
  • Meta-learning interpretation — the view that the outer pretraining loop implicitly trains the model to be good at an inner "few-shot learning" loop, by analogy to explicit meta-learning algorithms like MAML. Suggestive framing rather than a mechanistically verified claim.
  • Internal-optimization hypotheses — the more speculative claim (von Oswald et al. and others) that a Transformer's forward pass can implement something functionally analogous to a gradient-descent step on the in-context examples, particularly demonstrated in simplified linear-attention settings. Demonstrated in restricted, simplified architectures; whether this generalizes to full-scale pretrained Transformers on natural language remains actively debated, not established.

None of these theories is OpenAI's contribution — the theoretical and mechanistic explanations came almost entirely from academia and other labs (notably Anthropic) after GPT-3 made the phenomenon impossible to ignore. GPT-3's contribution was empirical and at scale: demonstrating that the phenomenon was real, robust, and improved smoothly with model size, which is precisely what made it worth explaining in the first place.

Part VI — One model family across modalities

9. ImageGPT, 2020 Invented by OpenAI (as a demonstration)

Chen, Radford, Child, Wu, Jun, Luan, Sutskever[11] asked a deliberately narrow question: if you flatten an image into a 1D sequence of pixel values and apply exactly the same autoregressive Transformer recipe used for text — with zero built-in knowledge of 2D spatial structure — does it learn anything useful?

text tokens  →  autoregressive Transformer  →  next-token prediction
pixel tokens →  autoregressive Transformer  →  next-pixel prediction

The answer was yes, more than expected: a GPT-2-scale model trained this way on low-resolution, unlabeled ImageNet learned image representations strong enough to be competitive on linear-probe and low-data classification benchmarks. ImageGPT never became the dominant image-generation architecture — treating an image as a raw 1D pixel sequence is a poor inductive bias compared to convolutional or patch-based approaches, and the compute cost of full autoregressive pixel generation is severe. Its actual significance is conceptual: it demonstrated that the same architecture and objective that worked for language was not language-specific at all — a striking generality result that foreshadowed the "any modality can be tokenized" framing that later shaped DALL·E (§11) and, eventually, native multimodal systems.

10. Jukebox, 2020 Invented by OpenAI

Dhariwal et al.[12] (arXiv:2005.00341) tackled a much harder version of the long-sequence problem than text ever presents: raw audio at CD quality is on the order of tens of thousands of samples per second, so a few minutes of song is a sequence of several million raw timesteps — far beyond what any direct autoregressive model over raw samples could handle. Jukebox's solution is a hierarchical VQ-VAE: a multi-scale vector-quantized autoencoder compresses raw audio into discrete codes at several levels of temporal resolution, and separate autoregressive Transformers then model the sequences of discrete codes at each level (coarsest first, then progressively finer levels conditioned on the coarser ones), rather than ever modeling raw audio samples directly. The result could generate multi-minute songs, with singing, conditioned on artist, genre, and (unaligned) lyrics — coherent over a timescale that direct raw-sample modeling could not plausibly reach. The broader lesson, which recurs across several OpenAI multimodal projects: when a modality's native sequence length is too long for direct modeling, compress it into a shorter, discrete latent sequence first, and apply the same autoregressive machinery to the latent codes instead.

11. DALL·E, 2021 Invented by OpenAI

Ramesh, Pavlov, Goh, Gray, Voss, Radford, Chen, Sutskever, "Zero-Shot Text-to-Image Generation," arXiv:2102.12092[13], extends the same "compress to discrete tokens, then autoregress" pattern from Jukebox to a joint text-image setting. A discrete VAE compresses images into a grid of discrete image tokens; text is tokenized with a standard BPE tokenizer; and a single Transformer models the concatenation of text tokens followed by image tokens as one undifferentiated autoregressive sequence — the model has no architectural distinction between "this is a text token" and "this is an image token" beyond their position in the sequence. This crystallizes into a genuinely important paradigm, worth naming explicitly:

Modality as tokens. If any modality can be reduced to a sequence of discrete tokens (via a learned tokenizer/quantizer specific to that modality), then a single sequence-modeling architecture and objective can, in principle, jointly model — and jointly generate across — arbitrarily many modalities. This idea, not any specific detail of DALL·E's dVAE, is what later echoes through GPT-4o's native multimodality (§21) and the broader industry move toward unified multimodal token streams.

Part VII — Representation learning across vision and language

12. CLIP, 2021 Invented by OpenAI

Radford, Kim, Hallacy, Ramesh, Goh, Agarwal, Sastry, Askell, Mishkin, Clark, et al., "Learning Transferable Visual Models From Natural Language Supervision," arXiv:2103.00020[14] — trained on 400 million image-text pairs — is architecturally simple and empirically enormous in consequence. CLIP uses a two-tower design: an image encoder (a ResNet or Vision Transformer) and a text encoder (a Transformer), trained jointly with a contrastive objective rather than a classification or generation loss.

image  →  Vision Encoder  →  image embedding z_i
                                       ↘
                                    similarity (cosine)
                                       ↗
text   →  Text Encoder    →  text  embedding z_t

Both towers project into a shared embedding space, and training maximizes cosine similarity between the embeddings of matched (image, caption) pairs while minimizing it for the \(N-1\) mismatched pairs in the same training batch — an InfoNCE-style symmetric contrastive loss:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left[\log\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_i,z_t^{(i)})/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\text{sim}(z_i,z_t^{(j)})/\tau)} + \log\frac{\exp(\text{sim}(z_t^{(i)},z_i)/\tau)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\exp(\text{sim}(z_t^{(i)},z_j)/\tau)}\right] \]

where \(\text{sim}(\cdot,\cdot)\) is cosine similarity and \(\tau\) is a learned temperature. Once trained, CLIP performs zero-shot classification on an entirely new label set with no fine-tuning: embed each candidate class name as a prompt ("a photo of a {class}"), embed the query image, and pick the class whose text embedding is closest. Its influence has been genuinely broad — CLIP embeddings became a standard building block for multimodal retrieval systems, provided the guidance signal for early text-to-image diffusion pipelines (DALL·E 2, and classifier guidance-adjacent applications), and became a common vision-encoder choice inside later vision-language models. Contrastive representation learning across modalities did not originate with CLIP — it has a broad research history (earlier joint image-text embedding work, and contrastive learning more generally going back well before CLIP) — but CLIP is the result that demonstrated the approach at a scale and simplicity that made it the default reference point for the field. Its documented limitations are also worth stating plainly: dataset bias inherited from web-scraped image-caption pairs, susceptibility to shortcut learning (relying on spurious correlations rather than genuine visual understanding), known robustness gaps under distribution shift, and weak compositional reasoning (CLIP-style models are known to struggle distinguishing "a red cube on a blue sphere" from "a blue cube on a red sphere" in ways that suggest something closer to bag-of-concepts matching than compositional understanding).

Part VIII — The diffusion revolution

13. Guided diffusion (ADM), 2021 Existing idea (diffusion models), significantly advanced by OpenAI

State this plainly: OpenAI did not invent diffusion probabilistic models. The lineage runs from Sohl-Dickstein et al. (Stanford, 2015, the original nonequilibrium-thermodynamics formulation) through Ho, Jain, and Abbeel's Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (Google/UC Berkeley, 2020, DDPM), which is the paper that made diffusion models practically competitive as generative models. What OpenAI contributed, in Dhariwal and Nichol's "Diffusion Models Beat GANs on Image Synthesis," arXiv:2105.05233[15], was an architecture search that improved the diffusion U-Net substantially through careful ablations, plus classifier guidance, a genuinely new and influential technique.

The diffusion process itself: a forward process progressively adds Gaussian noise to a data sample over \(T\) steps until it is indistinguishable from pure noise, and a learned reverse process is trained to undo this one step at a time:

\[ x_0 \rightarrow x_1 \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow x_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0, I) \qquad \text{(forward)} \qquad\qquad x_T \rightarrow x_{T-1} \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow x_0 \qquad \text{(learned reverse)} \]

Classifier guidance modifies the reverse process to condition on a class label \(y\) by adding the gradient of a separately-trained classifier's log-probability to the diffusion model's score estimate at each denoising step:

\[ \nabla_x \log p(x \mid y) \approx \nabla_x \log p(x) + s \cdot \nabla_x \log p(y \mid x) \]

where \(s\) is a guidance scale controlling the tradeoff — higher \(s\) pushes samples more strongly toward being classifiable as \(y\), trading sample diversity for fidelity to the condition. The empirical result — a diffusion model outperforming the strongest contemporary GANs (BigGAN-deep) on ImageNet FID, including matching quality with as few as 25 sampling steps — was historically significant precisely because GANs had dominated high-fidelity image synthesis for years, and diffusion models had until this point been seen as a theoretically elegant but practically inferior alternative. This paper is one of the clearest turning points in the field-wide shift from GAN-dominated to diffusion-dominated generative image modeling.

14. Classifier-free guidance, 2022 Not invented by OpenAI

This is an important correction to a common misattribution: classifier-free guidance was introduced by Jonathan Ho and Tim Salimans[16] (arXiv:2207.12598), both at Google Research / Google Brain at the time of publication — not OpenAI. (Salimans had previously been affiliated with OpenAI years earlier, but not at the time of this paper; the paper's own author affiliation is Google.) It is included in this article because of how directly it built on and displaced the OpenAI-originated classifier guidance technique above, and because it is foundational to essentially every modern text-to-image and text-to-video diffusion system, so getting the attribution right matters.

The idea: rather than training a separate classifier to provide a guidance gradient, jointly train one diffusion model to make both conditional predictions \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, y)\) and unconditional predictions \(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)\) (achieved simply by randomly dropping the conditioning signal during training), and combine them at sampling time:

\[ \epsilon_{\text{guided}} = \epsilon_{\text{uncond}} + w\,(\epsilon_{\text{cond}} - \epsilon_{\text{uncond}}) \]

with guidance weight \(w > 1\) amplifying the direction the conditioning pushes the prediction, without needing any separate classifier at all. This eliminated classifier guidance's two practical weaknesses — the need to train and maintain a separate noise-robust classifier, and the fact that classifier gradients tend to be adversarial-example-like rather than genuinely informative at high guidance strengths — and it works for arbitrary conditioning signals (full text captions, not just class labels), which classifier guidance could not easily do. This is why virtually every modern text-to-image and text-to-video diffusion model (Stable Diffusion, Imagen, Midjourney's underlying pipeline, Sora) uses classifier-free guidance rather than classifier guidance today.

Part IX — Tool-using language models

15. WebGPT, 2021 Invented by OpenAI

Nakano, Hilton, Balaji, Wu, Ouyang, Kim, Hesse, Jain, Kosaraju, Saunders, et al., arXiv:2112.09332[17], deserves to be read as a direct precursor of modern LLM agents, not merely an early retrieval system. WebGPT represents the shift:

\[ \text{LM} \rightarrow \text{text} \qquad\qquad \text{becomes} \qquad\qquad \text{LM} \leftrightarrow \text{environment} \]

GPT-3 was fine-tuned to operate a text-based web-browsing environment via a small fixed action set — issue a search query, click a link, scroll, find text on a page, quote a passage as evidence — and trained via a combination of imitation learning on human browsing demonstrations and RLHF (preference comparisons between candidate answers, judged partly on whether cited evidence actually supports the claims made). This introduces a genuine action-observation loop: the model's own actions change what it observes next, and its final answer must be grounded in evidence it collected through that loop, which makes human evaluation of factual accuracy considerably easier than evaluating an ungrounded free-text answer. It's worth being precise about a distinction the field still sometimes blurs: WebGPT is not the same as retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). RAG (Lewis et al., Facebook AI Research, 2020) retrieves relevant documents in a single, typically non-interactive step and conditions generation on them. WebGPT's loop is genuinely agentic — the model chooses which actions to take, observes their results, and can revise its plan (search again, follow a different link) based on what it finds, which is the structural template that later tool-calling, browser agents, coding agents, and computer-use agents all share.

Part X — Alignment becomes part of the model stack

16. InstructGPT and RLHF, 2022 Existing pipeline (preference learning, §2), significantly advanced and industrialized by OpenAI

Ouyang, Wu, Jiang, Almeida, Wainwright, Mishkin, Zhang, Agarwal, Slama, Ray, et al., arXiv:2203.02155[18], is the paper that turned §2's preference-learning architecture into the standard post-training recipe for language models, and it is important to be precise that this is an alignment/post-training paradigm, not a base Transformer architecture change — InstructGPT's underlying network is architecturally the same GPT-3 family model.

pretrained model  →  supervised fine-tuning (SFT)  →  human preference collection
                                                              ↓
              aligned model  ←  RL optimization (PPO)  ←  reward model

The reward model is trained on pairwise human comparisons using a Bradley-Terry-style preference model: for two candidate responses \(y_w\) (preferred, "won") and \(y_l\) (rejected, "lost") to the same prompt, the reward model \(r_\theta\) is trained so that

\[ P(y_w \succ y_l) = \sigma\big(r_\theta(y_w) - r_\theta(y_l)\big) \]

where \(\sigma\) is the logistic sigmoid — the model learns a scalar reward such that higher reward corresponds to higher probability of being the human-preferred response. The policy (the language model itself) is then optimized with PPO against this learned reward, with a KL-divergence penalty against the original SFT model added to the reward to discourage the policy from drifting so far from natural language that it starts exploiting quirks of the reward model rather than genuinely improving — a direct, practical instance of reward hacking, and the KL term is the standard mitigation. The headline empirical result: human evaluators preferred outputs from a 1.3B-parameter InstructGPT model over outputs from the 175B-parameter base GPT-3, despite having roughly 100× fewer parameters — a striking demonstration that alignment/post-training quality can matter more for a model's perceived usefulness than raw parameter count, and a result that directly established RLHF as a required stage in the modern LLM training stack rather than an optional add-on. The paper also documents, honestly, the tradeoffs this pipeline surfaces: gains in helpfulness and instruction-following can trade off against harmlessness if not carefully balanced, reward-model imperfections create exploitable gaps the policy can learn to walk into, and the whole pipeline is sensitive to distribution shift between what the reward model was trained on and what the RL policy later generates.

Part XI — Speech at scale

17. Whisper, 2022 Scaled / popularized by OpenAI

Radford, Kim, Xu, Brockman, McLeavey, Sutskever, arXiv:2212.04356[19] — Whisper's significance is primarily about data scale, multilingual and multitask supervision, and resulting robustness, not architectural novelty: it is a fairly standard encoder-decoder Transformer, architecturally unremarkable relative to prior speech models. What is unusual is the training recipe: 680,000 hours of multilingual, multitask audio scraped from the web with only weak (uncurated, naturally noisy) supervision, spanning transcription, translation, language identification, and timestamp prediction — all cast as a single sequence-to-sequence task via a multitask tokenization scheme where special tokens in the decoder's input sequence specify which task and language is being performed. The payoff of weak supervision at this scale was strong zero-shot transfer: Whisper's out-of-the-box performance on standard benchmarks was often competitive with prior models that required full supervised fine-tuning on each specific benchmark's training distribution — the same "scale plus weak/broad supervision beats narrow strong supervision" pattern that GPT-2 first demonstrated for text, now shown for speech. Whisper matters to this article's broader argument specifically because it's a clean, small-scale preview of the multitask, multimodal-token framing that later shows up at much larger scale in native multimodal foundation models.

Part XII — Reasoning supervision

18. Process supervision / process reward models, 2023 Invented by OpenAI

Lightman, Kosaraju, Burda, Cobbe, et al., "Let's Verify Step by Step," arXiv:2305.20050[20], draws a distinction that turned out to matter enormously for later reasoning-model training. An Outcome Reward Model (ORM) scores only the final answer:

\[ R(\text{final answer}) \]

while a Process Reward Model (PRM) scores each intermediate reasoning step \(s_i\) in a chain-of-thought individually:

\[ R(s_1),\ R(s_2),\ \dots,\ R(s_n) \]

Training on the more challenging MATH dataset with a GPT-4-based model and substantially more human step-level feedback than prior process-supervision attempts, the paper found process supervision measurably outperformed outcome supervision at producing solutions that were correct for the right reasons, not just correct by coincidence — and, importantly, that this held even when both approaches were given the same total amount of human-labeling budget. The reasons this matters go beyond raw accuracy: process supervision gives much better credit assignment (a wrong final answer might stem from one bad step in an otherwise sound ten-step derivation — outcome supervision penalizes the whole trajectory equally, while process supervision can localize exactly where reasoning went wrong), it produces a natural interpretability signal (a human or automated system can inspect which specific step a PRM flags as low-quality), and it enables step-level search — a PRM can score partial reasoning trajectories, which is exactly what's needed to guide tree search or best-of-N reasoning at inference time rather than only evaluating complete outputs. The clear limitation, which the paper itself is candid about, is annotation cost: step-level human labels are far more expensive to collect than final-answer correctness labels, which is a large part of why later reasoning-model work has explored automated or self-generated process supervision signals rather than relying purely on human step labels at scale.

Part XIII — Consistency models

19. Consistency models, 2023 Invented by OpenAI

Song, Dhariwal, Chen, Sutskever, arXiv:2303.01469[21], addresses diffusion models' single biggest practical weakness: sample generation requires many sequential denoising steps (commonly 50-100+), each a full forward pass through the network, making generation slow relative to single-pass generative models like GANs.

Diffusion:          noise  →  [step] → [step] → ... (50-100+ steps) → image
Consistency model:  noise  →  [one or few model evaluations]         → image

A consistency model is trained so that its output, applied to any point along a noise trajectory back to a clean data point, maps directly to that same clean data point — enforcing self-consistency along the trajectory (hence the name). Two training regimes are given: consistency distillation, which trains the consistency model to mimic the multi-step trajectory of an already-trained diffusion model, and consistency training, which trains a consistency model from scratch without requiring a pretrained diffusion teacher at all. At inference, sampling is a single forward pass (or, optionally, a small handful of passes for a quality/speed tradeoff — the model supports both one-step and multi-step sampling by design, letting a user trade compute for quality without retraining). Reported results at the time — state-of-the-art one-step generation FID of 3.55 on CIFAR-10 and 6.20 on ImageNet 64×64 — beat prior diffusion-distillation approaches specifically in the one-and-few-step regime. Follow-up work, including continuous-time consistency models and later simplified/stabilized/scaled variants (sCM) from OpenAI, has continued to close the quality gap with full multi-step diffusion sampling while preserving the speed advantage. This line of work is best understood as OpenAI's direct research contribution to inference-efficiency research for generative models — the generative-model analogue of the test-time-compute tradeoff (§23) explored later on the language-model side, just running in the opposite direction: trading iterative computation away rather than adding more of it.

Part XIV — Multimodal foundation models

20. GPT-4, 2023 Undisclosed architecture — no verified claim possible

The GPT-4 Technical Report (arXiv:2303.08774)[22] is unusual among the papers in this article precisely because of what it withholds: OpenAI explicitly states the report contains no further details about the architecture (including model size), hardware, training compute, dataset construction, or training method, citing competitive and safety considerations. This marked a clear departure from GPT-1 through GPT-3's papers, which disclosed architecture and scale in detail. This article will not repeat unverified figures about GPT-4's parameter count, layer count, dataset composition, or whether it uses a Mixture-of-Experts architecture as if they were confirmed facts — such figures circulate widely (including detailed claims from industry-analysis outlets like SemiAnalysis) but originate from leaks and inference, not from OpenAI's own disclosure, and should be labeled explicitly as industry speculation, not confirmed architecture, whenever discussed at all. What is confirmed is that GPT-4 is multimodal (accepting image and text input) and shows markedly improved performance across a broad range of professional and academic benchmarks relative to GPT-3.5, per OpenAI's own reported evaluation results.

21. GPT-4o — omni models, 2024 Invented by OpenAI (as a product/system integration); underlying architecture undisclosed

GPT-4o, announced May 13, 2024, represents a genuine conceptual shift in how voice interaction with a language model is built, even though its internal architecture is not publicly documented in comparable detail to GPT-1 through GPT-3. The prior standard pipeline for voice assistants built on LLMs was strictly modular:

\[ \text{speech-to-text} \rightarrow \text{text LLM} \rightarrow \text{text-to-speech} \]

a three-model chain with reported end-to-end latency around 2.8 seconds on average in OpenAI's prior voice mode, and — critically — one that structurally discards paralinguistic information (tone, emotion, emphasis, interruption timing) at the speech-to-text step, since the LLM in the middle only ever sees flattened text. GPT-4o is described by OpenAI as natively processing and generating combinations of text, audio, image, and video within one model, reporting audio response latencies as low as 232ms (average 320ms) — comparable to typical human conversational response time — and OpenAI's own materials describe this as end-to-end training across modalities rather than a pipeline of separately-trained single-modality models. It is important to distinguish the product capability (genuinely novel and well-documented via OpenAI's own demos and latency figures) from the disclosed architectural novelty (minimal) — OpenAI has not published a technical report detailing GPT-4o's internal architecture comparable to, say, the CLIP or Whisper papers, so claims about its internal mechanism beyond "natively multimodal" and the reported latency/capability figures should be treated as unconfirmed.

Part XV — Test-time compute becomes a scaling axis

22. Chain-of-thought and reasoning, 2022 Not invented by OpenAI

Chain-of-thought prompting belongs to Wei, Wang, Schuurmans, Bosma, Ichter, Xia, Chi, Le, Zhou — all Google researchers, arXiv:2201.11903[23] — and this attribution should never be blurred. The idea: prompting a model to produce intermediate reasoning steps \(r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_n\) before its final answer \(y\), rather than jumping directly from question \(x\) to answer,

\[ x \rightarrow r_1 \rightarrow r_2 \rightarrow \dots \rightarrow r_n \rightarrow y \]

substantially improves performance on tasks requiring multi-step arithmetic, commonsense, or symbolic reasoning — apparently because it gives the model additional forward-pass computation, spread across additional token positions, to work through a problem incrementally, rather than forcing the entire reasoning process to happen implicitly within a single token's worth of computation. Follow-on academic work extended this substantially before OpenAI's reasoning-model line existed at all: self-consistency (Wang et al., Google, 2022)[24] samples multiple independent reasoning paths and takes a majority vote over their final answers, meaningfully improving accuracy over single-greedy-path CoT. Best-of-N and rejection sampling, verifier-guided search, and tree-structured search over reasoning paths are further generalizations, explored across many academic and industry papers, of the same core idea: reasoning quality can be improved by spending more inference-time compute generating and evaluating candidate reasoning trajectories, rather than only by making the model bigger or better-trained. This body of work — CoT itself, self-consistency, and search-based reasoning — is the direct intellectual predecessor to o1 (§23), and none of its foundational ideas originated at OpenAI.

23. OpenAI o1 and test-time compute scaling, 2024 Existing idea (CoT + RL + search), scaled to a new training/inference paradigm by OpenAI

o1[25] represents a genuine paradigm shift, and it's worth being precise about exactly what the shift was, since "reasoning model" alone under-describes it. Prior LLM scaling had focused almost entirely on one term:

\[ \text{Capability} = f(C_{\text{train}}) \]

o1's reported finding — that performance improves both with more reinforcement-learning compute spent during training and, independently, with more compute spent generating a longer chain of thought at inference time before answering — established a second, largely orthogonal scaling axis:

\[ \text{Capability} = f(C_{\text{train}},\ C_{\text{post-train}},\ C_{\text{test}}) \]

The training approach, per OpenAI's own o1 System Card[25], uses large-scale reinforcement learning to train the model to produce a long internal chain of thought before responding — this internal reasoning is described as being trained via RL rather than simply elicited via prompting, and is reported to include mechanisms like searching against dense process-based verifier reward models and adaptively revising the model's response distribution at test time. Concretely reported capability results: 89th percentile on Codeforces-style competitive programming, performance among the top 500 US students on AIME, and accuracy exceeding human PhD-level baselines on curated physics, biology, and chemistry benchmark subsets, per OpenAI's own reporting. The paper also introduces deliberative alignment — reported as having the model explicitly reason about safety policies within its own chain of thought before responding to a potentially unsafe prompt, which OpenAI reports produces improved robustness to jailbreaks relative to prior alignment approaches on internal evaluations.

The economic consequence deserves its own sentence: this genuinely changed the cost model of running frontier AI. Before test-time-compute scaling, inference cost per query was roughly fixed for a given model. After it, a model can be made to think longer at inference time in exchange for better answers on hard problems, meaning the same trained model now has a tunable cost-quality tradeoff at serving time, with real consequences for inference-serving infrastructure, latency budgets, and per-query pricing — a theme this article returns to directly in Part XXI.

Part XVI — From language models to agents

24. Tool calling and agentic models Trajectory scaled and industrialized by OpenAI; individual components have mixed origins

The lineage is direct and traceable through this article's own sections: GPT-3 prompting → WebGPT (§15) → function/tool calling → code execution → retrieval → computer use → autonomous multi-step agents. The general structure that all of these share is a policy interacting with an environment over multiple steps, observing the result of each action before choosing the next:

\[ s_t \rightarrow \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) \rightarrow a_t \rightarrow \text{environment} \rightarrow o_{t+1} \rightarrow s_{t+1} \] 
           LLM
            ↓
        decision
     ┌──────┼──────┬────────┬─────────┐
   search  code  browser   API     computer
     └──────┴──────┴────────┴─────────┘

            ↓
           LLM  (loop continues)

What differs from step to step across this lineage is primarily the action space (browsing actions in WebGPT; arbitrary structured function calls in tool-calling models; a full code interpreter; raw screen/mouse/keyboard control in computer-use systems) rather than the fundamental loop structure, which was already present in WebGPT. The genuinely open engineering questions in agent design — planning over long horizons, maintaining useful memory across many steps, choosing which tool to invoke when several could plausibly apply, recovering gracefully from a failed or misinterpreted action, and knowing when to stop — are largely orthogonal to base-model architecture, and there is a reasonable case that agent scaffold and loop design now matters as much for real-world task success as base-model capability does, particularly for long-horizon tasks where a single bad decision several steps into a trajectory can derail an otherwise capable model.

Part XVII — Recurrent depth and latent reasoning

25. Recurrent depth Academic origin (Universal Transformer, Google, 2018); actively developed in academia through 2025-2026; reportedly adopted by OpenAI in Astra

This is the deepest technical section of the article, and it deserves careful unpacking because the underlying mechanism is genuinely different from everything else described so far. A standard Transformer's depth is a stack of distinct layers, each with its own independent parameters:

\[ h^{(l+1)} = F_l(h^{(l)}), \qquad l = 1, \dots, L \quad \text{(each } F_l \text{ has separate parameters)} \]

Recurrent depth replaces some or all of this stack with a single block \(F_\theta\) whose parameters are shared and reapplied repeatedly:

\[ h^{(k+1)} = F_\theta(h^{(k)}), \qquad k = 1, \dots, K \quad \text{(the same } F_\theta \text{, applied } K \text{ times)} \]

The architectural template used by recent recurrent-depth models (following Geiping et al., described below) separates the network into three parts: a non-recurrent prelude that processes the input into an initial hidden state, a recurrent block that gets applied repeatedly (\(K\) times, where \(K\) can vary — including at inference time, after training), and a non-recurrent coda that maps the final recurrent state to output logits:

          Input
            ↓
         Prelude
            ↓
    ┌─────────────────┐
    │ Recurrent Block  │  ←──┐
    └────────┬─────────┘     │
             │                │  repeat K times
             └────────────────┘
             ↓
           Coda
            ↓
          Output

The academic lineage runs unmistakably through Google, not OpenAI. The Universal Transformer (Dehghani, Gouws, Vinyals, Uszkoreit, Kaiser — Google, arXiv:1807.03819, ICLR 2019)[26] first introduced weight sharing across depth combined with Adaptive Computation Time (Graves, 2016)[27] — a mechanism that lets the model learn, per position, how many recurrent steps to apply before halting, rather than using a fixed depth for every input. The Universal Transformer showed improved generalization on algorithmic tasks and machine translation relative to both a standard Transformer and an LSTM of comparable size, establishing the core empirical claim that depth-recurrence with adaptive halting can be a genuinely useful inductive bias, not merely a parameter-saving trick.

The idea saw a substantial 2025 resurgence directly relevant to current frontier-model research: Geiping, McLeish, Jain, Kirchenbauer, Singh, Bartoldson, Kailkhura, Bhatele, et al., "Scaling up Test-Time Compute with Latent Reasoning: A Recurrent Depth Approach," arXiv:2502.05171[28], trained a proof-of-concept 3.5B-parameter model (released as Huginn-3.5B) on 800B tokens, weighted toward code and mathematical reasoning, on the Frontier supercomputer. The paper's central empirical claim is that unrolling the recurrent block to greater depth at test time — with no additional training — improved performance on reasoning benchmarks, in some cases matching the effective capability of a model with roughly 50B parameters despite the underlying network having only 3.5B trained parameters. Because the recurrence happens in hidden-state space rather than by emitting additional tokens, the paper explicitly notes it requires no specialized chain-of-thought training data, works within small context windows (since no extra tokens are generated), and — its most consequential claim for the rest of this article — may capture forms of reasoning that are not easily represented in words at all, precisely because the computation never has to be projected down into a discrete token vocabulary at each intermediate step the way explicit chain-of-thought does.

Why this increases effective computational depth without proportionally increasing parameter count follows directly from the architecture: total FLOPs scale with \(K\) (the number of recurrent passes), while the parameter count of \(F_\theta\) itself stays fixed regardless of \(K\) — the model trades additional sequential compute at inference time for additional effective depth, decoupling "how much computation is applied" from "how many distinct weight matrices exist," which is a fundamentally different lever than either making the model wider/deeper (more parameters) or making it generate a longer chain of thought (more tokens).

26. Recurrent depth vs. explicit chain-of-thought

These two approaches to spending extra test-time compute look superficially similar (both "think more before answering") but differ in almost every practical respect, and the comparison is worth doing in full detail because it directly sets up the Astra discussion in Part XVIII.

Explicit chain-of-thoughtRecurrent depth

A point the source material for this article is explicit about, and which deserves equal emphasis here: latent computation inside a recurrent block should not be casually described as a "hidden chain of thought" in the literal, human-cognition sense. It is iterative refinement of a hidden vector representation under gradient-trained dynamics — there is no guarantee this refinement corresponds to anything resembling discrete propositions, steps, or human-legible reasoning stages, even though it may be functionally solving similar problems to what explicit CoT solves. Treating "the model is doing latent reasoning" and "the model has an internal monologue analogous to human thought" as equivalent claims goes well beyond what the recurrent-depth literature actually demonstrates.

Part XIX — The monitorability problem

The tension this section analyzes can be stated as two contrasting information flows:

\[ \text{reasoning} \rightarrow \text{text} \rightarrow \text{monitor} \qquad \text{(explicit CoT)} \qquad\qquad \text{reasoning} \rightarrow \text{hidden states} \qquad \text{(latent/recurrent)} \]

Chain-of-thought monitoring — having a separate system (automated or human) read a model's generated reasoning trace to catch misaligned intentions, planning around a safety guardrail, or reward-hacking behavior before it manifests in the final output — has emerged as a genuinely useful, if imperfect, safety tool precisely because current frontier reasoning models externalize enough of their intermediate computation as text for this to work at all. A body of interpretability research exists to partially substitute for this when text isn't available or isn't trustworthy: activation probing (training small classifiers on a model's internal activations to detect specific properties or behaviors), the logit lens (projecting intermediate-layer hidden states through the model's own unembedding matrix to see what token distribution they would predict if output at that layer, as a rough window into what the model "believes" partway through computation), and broader mechanistic interpretability approaches (like the induction-head circuit analysis in §8) that attempt to reverse-engineer what a specific learned computation is actually doing.

The core, honestly-stated tension: a model that performs a meaningful fraction of its "thinking" through repeated latent recurrent passes rather than through generated text has, by construction, less of its reasoning process available to a text-only monitor — not necessarily because it is deceptively hiding anything, but simply because the computation was never in token form to begin with. This is a structural reduction in one specific safety tool's coverage, not evidence of intent. It is important to hold two things in mind simultaneously and not conflate them: this is a demonstrated, structural property of how recurrent-depth architectures allocate computation (established by the architecture itself, independent of any specific model's behavior) — but claims that this property has already caused or will inevitably cause deceptive or misaligned behavior in any specific deployed system are, as of this writing, theoretical concerns being actively raised by researchers and reporters, not demonstrated incidents. The correct posture for this article, and arguably for the field, is to treat reduced monitorability as a genuine, worked-out engineering tradeoff that recurrent-depth architectures introduce — one that argues for investing more, not less, in activation-level interpretability tooling as latent-computation architectures become more prevalent — rather than to treat it as proof of any specific hidden failure mode.

Part XX — Compute becomes dynamic

This article's central thesis, stated once more directly: earlier neural network paradigms operated, to a rough first approximation, with fixed compute per input:

\[ \text{compute per token} \approx \text{constant, regardless of input difficulty} \]

Modern AI research has been progressively exploring the alternative:

\[ \text{compute} = f(\text{problem difficulty}) \]

Every major theme of this article is a different mechanism for achieving some version of this, and it's clarifying to see them side by side as instances of the same underlying idea — conditional computation:

  • Bigger static model — more parameters, applied uniformly to every input regardless of difficulty. Not conditional at all; the blunt instrument this whole trend moves away from.
  • Mixture-of-Experts — activate only a selected subset of parameters per input (a much older research lineage than any single lab; not covered as an OpenAI contribution in this article, and explicitly listed among ideas OpenAI did not originate in Part XXIII). Conditional over which parameters fire, not over how much total compute is spent.
  • Chain-of-thought (§22, Google-originated) — generate additional reasoning tokens for harder problems; compute scales with output length, which can itself be made conditional on difficulty via learned stopping behavior.
  • Search / best-of-N — evaluate multiple candidate reasoning paths and select or aggregate over them; compute scales with the search budget allocated, which can be set per-problem.
  • Recurrent depth (§25) — repeat internal computation a variable number of times; compute scales with the number of recurrent passes, potentially decided per-input via a learned halting mechanism.
  • Tool use (§15, §24) — offload part of the computation to an external system (a calculator, a code interpreter, a search engine) entirely outside the model's own forward pass; compute for that sub-task is whatever the external tool costs, not a fixed model-internal amount.

All six are answers to the same underlying question — where should extra computation go, and how much of it does this specific input deserve — and no single one of them is architecturally "the future" in isolation; the field's current trajectory (visible across o1's search-plus-RL approach, MoE's parameter conditionality, and Astra's reported recurrent depth) looks like convergence toward systems that combine several of these mechanisms simultaneously, letting different axes of conditional computation handle different kinds of difficulty.

This section is written specifically for the infrastructure, MLOps, and inference-engineering audience this article targets. Compare three inference regimes directly on the dimensions that actually determine serving cost and latency:

The sharpest way to frame the central engineering question this raises: could recurrent depth exchange token-level sequential decoding for layer-level sequential computation?

\[ \text{token-level sequential decoding} \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad \text{layer-level sequential computation} \]

Both are fundamentally sequential — you cannot compute recurrent pass \(k+1\) before pass \(k\) completes, just as you cannot generate token \(t+1\) before token \(t\) is decoded — so recurrent depth does not straightforwardly escape the sequential-dependency latency problem that makes autoregressive decoding slow relative to, say, a single forward pass through a fixed-depth network. Where it plausibly helps: if a recurrent pass is cheaper or more parallelizable per unit of "effective reasoning" than generating and caching a reasoning token (no KV-cache write, no sampling/detokenization step, no risk of a malformed or off-distribution reasoning token derailing the trace), then trading some chain-of-thought tokens for recurrent passes could reduce KV-cache memory pressure and total token count at equivalent reasoning quality. Where it plausibly does not help, or introduces new costs: inference schedulers, continuous-batching logic, and speculative-decoding pipelines built around the token-generation paradigm assume the dominant cost structure of an LLM forward pass — a recurrent-depth model's dominant cost structure (repeated full-network passes with no new tokens) does not obviously fit the same scheduling assumptions, and serving infrastructure would plausibly need real changes (batching strategies that account for variable, possibly adaptive, per-request recurrence depth; different profiling and autoscaling heuristics; different approaches to guaranteeing latency SLOs when a request's total compute isn't knowable in advance from its prompt alone) to serve such models efficiently at scale. This is squarely an open systems-engineering problem, not a solved one, as of this writing.

Part XXIII — Ideas OpenAI did not invent

This section exists specifically to prevent the kind of historical revisionism that happens by omission — where a lab's genuine contributions get inflated, in casual retelling, into having invented the whole surrounding field. None of the following originated at OpenAI, and each deserves its real attribution stated plainly:

  • The Transformer architecture — Vaswani et al., Google, 2017[3]. Every model discussed in this entire article is built on this foundation.
  • Attention mechanisms — predate the Transformer itself; Bahdanau et al.'s additive attention for neural machine translation (2014) is the direct academic ancestor.
  • Chain-of-thought prompting — Wei et al., Google, 2022[23].
  • Diffusion probabilistic models — Sohl-Dickstein et al. (Stanford, 2015) originated the framework; Ho, Jain, Abbeel (Google/UC Berkeley, 2020, DDPM) made it practically competitive.
  • Mixture-of-Experts — a much older research lineage, with foundational work going back to Jacobs, Jordan, Nowlan, Hinton (1991) and substantially revived for deep learning by Shazeer et al. (Google, 2017).
  • Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) — Lewis et al., Facebook AI Research (Meta), 2020.
  • The Universal Transformer — Dehghani et al., Google, 2018[26] — the direct architectural ancestor of recurrent depth.
  • Recurrent neural networks — a much earlier research tradition; Elman networks (1990), LSTMs (Hochreiter & Schmidhuber, 1997).
  • Reinforcement learning as a field — decades older than any lab discussed here; Sutton and Barto's foundational textbook synthesis long predates modern deep RL.
  • Self-play — a much earlier idea in game-playing AI, well-established before OpenAI Five (TD-Gammon, Tesauro, 1992, is a classic early instance; AlphaGo's self-play training, DeepMind, 2016, is a direct and closer predecessor).
  • Contrastive learning — a broad research history predating CLIP by years, including SimCLR and MoCo (2020) in vision, and contrastive objectives in representation learning more generally reaching back further still.

What OpenAI's actual contribution was, in nearly every one of these cases, is visible in this article's earlier sections: taking an existing idea and demonstrating it at a scale, or in a combination, that had not been shown before — CLIP's contrastive learning at 400M-pair scale; InstructGPT's preference-learning pipeline applied to a 175B-parameter language model; WebGPT's agentic loop applied to open-domain web research. The contribution is real. It is a different kind of contribution than invention, and conflating the two is exactly the error this section exists to prevent.

Part XXV — Five eras of AI compute

Stepping back, the trajectory this article has traced organizes cleanly into five eras, each defined by which axis of the capability function (from the introduction) was the primary lever being pulled:

EraPrimary leverRepresentative systems
Era 1 — Architecture scalingBetter inductive biases: CNNs, RNNs, then the TransformerAlexNet, LSTM seq2seq, Vaswani et al.'s Transformer
Era 2 — Parameter scalingSame architecture, more parametersGPT-2 → GPT-3 → frontier-scale LLMs generally
Era 3 — Data + compute scalingSystematic, jointly-optimized scaling of parameters, data, and compute togetherKaplan et al.'s scaling laws; Chinchilla's correction; large-scale pretraining generally
Era 4 — Post-training scalingHuman feedback and preference optimization as a distinct, essential training stageRLHF, InstructGPT, DPO and other preference-optimization variants, reasoning-focused RL
Era 5 — Inference scalingCompute spent at serving time, not just training timeChain-of-thought, self-consistency, verifier-guided search, o1's test-time compute, recurrent depth, agentic tool use

The question this article's evidence points toward, and which it will not answer definitively because no one can yet: is the next paradigm Adaptive Computation — architectures that dynamically decide, per input, how many parameters to activate, how many tokens to generate before answering, how many recurrent passes to execute, which external tools to invoke, how much search to perform over candidate solutions, and when to stop — rather than any single one of these levers scaled in isolation? Every piece of evidence surveyed in this article — MoE's conditional parameter activation, chain-of-thought's variable-length reasoning, o1's RL-trained stopping behavior, and Astra's reported recurrent depth with (per the underlying academic template) potential adaptive halting — points toward convergence on exactly this kind of system, even though no confirmed, fully-disclosed architecture combining all of these axes simultaneously exists in the public record as of this writing.

Fig. 1 — Each era did not replace the previous lever; it added a new one on top, culminating in the still-open question of whether the next paradigm is dynamic allocation across all of them at once.

Open research questions

  1. Can recurrent depth achieve the same scaling behavior (predictable power-law improvement with compute) as parameter depth does under Kaplan/Chinchilla-style scaling laws?
  2. How should a recurrent-depth model learn when to halt — is Adaptive Computation Time's original formulation sufficient at frontier scale, or does halting need to be learned via RL against downstream task success instead?
  3. Can recurrent representations destabilize (diverge, collapse, or cycle) after too many iterations, and what training-time interventions (normalization schemes, initialization) reliably prevent this at scale?
  4. What is the optimal balance between latent recurrent compute and explicit reasoning tokens for a fixed inference budget — are they substitutes, complements, or task-dependent in which regime dominates?
  5. Can latent reasoning be reliably interpreted with current activation-probing and logit-lens techniques, or does recurrent depth specifically require new interpretability methods not yet developed?
  6. Can process reward models (§18), designed around discrete textual reasoning steps, be adapted to supervise or shape latent recurrent computation that has no discrete step structure?
  7. How should recurrent computation be scheduled on GPU inference infrastructure — does it require fundamentally different batching and scheduling strategies than token-generation-oriented serving stacks (see Part XXI)?
  8. Can recurrent depth reduce KV-cache memory pressure in practice at frontier scale, or does the recurrent block's own working-state memory offset that advantage?
  9. Does recurrent depth measurably improve compositional generalization (systematic recombination of learned sub-skills) relative to equivalent fixed-depth models, beyond what has so far been shown on smaller controlled benchmarks?
  10. How does depth recurrence interact with Mixture-of-Experts routing — can a recurrent block itself contain conditional expert routing, and does this compound or conflict with the recurrence's own inductive bias?
  11. Can recurrent blocks develop genuinely algorithmic internal behavior (e.g., something functionally equivalent to an iterative numerical solver) purely from gradient training, and can this be verified rather than merely inferred from downstream task performance?
  12. Are recurrent-depth models measurably more difficult to monitor for misaligned behavior than equivalent chain-of-thought models, under controlled red-teaming conditions rather than architectural argument alone?
  13. Can recurrent-depth models learn to allocate computation genuinely proportional to per-input problem difficulty, and how would this be rigorously measured rather than assumed from architecture?
  14. Can recurrent-depth computation outperform search-based test-time scaling (best-of-N, tree search) at matched inference compute budgets, or are the two better understood as complementary rather than competing?
  15. How should the field evaluate reasoning quality that does not manifest as inspectable textual chain-of-thought at all — what would a rigorous benchmark for "latent reasoning correctness" even look like?
  16. Should frontier AI safety evaluation formally incorporate latent-computation interpretability as a required component, given the monitorability gap recurrent-depth architectures introduce (Part XIX)?
  17. Does increased inference-time compute eventually exhibit predictable scaling laws analogous to Kaplan/Chinchilla pretraining scaling laws, and if so, what is the functional form relating test-time compute to task accuracy?
  18. What architectures could combine Mixture-of-Experts, recurrence, external memory, tool use, search, and multimodality within one coherently-trained system, and what does the interaction between these conditional-computation mechanisms look like when several are present simultaneously rather than studied in isolation?

Engineering and research takeaways

For researchers

The scientifically unresolved core of this article is Part XVII through Part XXI: whether recurrent depth is a genuine third axis of test-time scaling (alongside chain-of-thought and search) with its own predictable scaling behavior, or a narrower optimization that works well on the specific benchmarks it has so far been evaluated on. The interpretability gap it opens (Part XIX) is a real, currently under-resourced research area relative to its likely importance if latent-computation architectures become more prevalent at frontier scale.

For PhD students

The open-questions list above is written as a literal thesis-topic menu. The recurrent-depth/latent-reasoning track specifically (questions 1-13) is unusually tractable for academic-scale compute relative to most frontier-AI research directions, precisely because Huginn-3.5B and the Geiping et al. training code are public — a genuine opportunity to do primary architectural research without needing frontier-lab-scale compute budgets.

For MTech/MS students

Read the Essential list below in order — it traces one coherent argument, not a disconnected reading list, and the PPO → RLHF → InstructGPT chain plus the CoT → self-consistency → o1 chain are each worth reading as connected units rather than isolated papers.

For AI engineers

The architectural idea most likely to matter for systems you build in the next two years is not recurrent depth (still pre-production-maturity) but the general pattern in Part XX — conditional computation. Whether via MoE, tool-calling, or configurable reasoning-effort settings on reasoning-model APIs, designing systems that spend variable compute per request rather than fixed compute is now a mainstream, not experimental, design consideration.

For MLOps / infrastructure engineers

Part XXI is written directly for you. The practical near-term action item: reasoning-model inference (long, variable-length chain-of-thought) already breaks assumptions baked into batching and autoscaling logic tuned for fixed-length, low-variance-latency workloads — this is true today, independent of whether recurrent depth ever reaches production scale. If recurrent-depth models do reach production, expect a second, distinct round of serving-infrastructure rework, since their compute pattern (repeated full-network passes, no token growth) doesn't map cleanly onto either standard decode-phase or long-CoT serving assumptions.

For inference engineers

Track where the bottleneck moves across these three regimes: standard decoding is memory-bandwidth-bound (KV-cache reads dominate); long chain-of-thought reasoning is a mix of memory-bandwidth pressure (growing KV-cache) and raw token-generation latency; recurrent-depth reasoning, if and when it reaches production, is more likely to be bound by the sequential-dependency chain of repeated full-network forward passes — a genuinely different profiling and optimization target than either of the first two, closer in character to reducing per-layer latency in a very deep, narrow pipeline than to anything in standard LLM-serving optimization playbooks today.
