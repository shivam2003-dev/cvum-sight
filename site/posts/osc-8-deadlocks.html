Operating System Concepts Chapter 8 — Deadlocks.

operating-systemschapter-8computer-sciencecompanion-notes
A deadlock happens when tasks wait on each other in a cycle and nobody can move forward. The system is not crashed, but it is stuck.

1. Introduction

Deadlocks are a classic resource-allocation problem. They appear when multiple tasks each hold something and wait for something else in a way that forms a loop.

This chapter matters because deadlocks are expensive to debug and often happen only under certain timing conditions.

2. Detailed explanation

Four necessary conditions

Deadlock needs mutual exclusion, hold and wait, no preemption, and circular wait. If one of these is broken, a true deadlock cannot form.

Resource-allocation graphs

Graphs help visualize who holds what and who is waiting for what. Cycles often signal danger.

Prevention

Deadlock prevention designs the rules so one of the required conditions never holds.

Avoidance

Deadlock avoidance allows flexible allocation but checks whether a request would push the system into an unsafe state.

Detection and recovery

Some systems allow deadlocks to happen, then detect them and recover by killing or rolling back selected work.

3. Key concepts

ConceptMeaning
DeadlockA cycle of waiting where nobody can proceed.
Safe stateA resource state from which all processes can still finish.
PreventionDesigning rules so deadlock cannot happen.
AvoidanceChecking requests before granting them.
DetectionFinding deadlocks after they happen.
RecoveryBreaking a deadlock by force.

4. Real-world examples

Two database transactions

Each transaction locks one row and waits for the other. Neither can finish until the DB intervenes.

Two threads with two locks

Thread A holds lock 1 and wants lock 2, while thread B holds lock 2 and wants lock 1.

Distributed services

Service-level waits can mimic deadlock-like cycles if dependencies are poorly designed.

5. FAQs

Is deadlock the same as starvation?

No. In starvation, one task may never get service while others continue. In deadlock, the waiting cycle blocks everyone involved.

Why not always prevent deadlocks completely?

Because the restrictions may hurt performance or flexibility.

Why is detection useful?

Because some systems prefer speed and flexibility, then recover only when needed.

What is the easiest practical defense?

A consistent resource-acquisition order often reduces many deadlock risks.

6. Key takeaways

  • Deadlock is a circular waiting problem.
  • Four conditions are required for it to exist.
  • Systems can prevent, avoid, detect, or recover from deadlock.
  • Engineering tradeoffs decide which strategy is best.

7. Conclusion

Chapter 8 teaches a key systems lesson: correctness can fail even when every individual component looks reasonable on its own. The trouble appears in the interaction pattern.
