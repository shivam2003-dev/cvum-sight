Chapter 5 — Monte Carlo Methods, explained.

monte-carloreturnsimportance-samplingoff-policy

// the one-minute version

Learning values and policies from complete sampled episodes, with on-policy control and off-policy importance sampling.

Suppose you do not know the transition model, but you can play a game to the end. The final outcome tells you how earlier choices worked out. Monte Carlo methods learn directly from those complete stories.

01 The chapter in one mental model

core loopV(Sₜ) ← V(Sₜ) + α[Gₜ − V(Sₜ)], after the episode reveals Gₜ.

The notation can look dense, but the purpose is practical: turn experience into a better prediction or a better decision. Read every equation by naming what is known now, what new evidence arrived, and which estimate is being moved toward that evidence.

02 Learning from episodes

Monte Carlo methods wait until termination, compute the actual sampled return from each visited state, and average those returns. No transition model and no bootstrapped estimate are required.

03 First-visit and every-visit

First-visit MC updates a state only for its first occurrence in an episode; every-visit updates all occurrences. Both converge under standard assumptions, but their samples and variance differ.

04 Action values for control

Without a model, state values alone cannot compare unseen actions. Estimating q(s,a) lets the agent improve its policy directly from experienced state–action returns.

05 Exploration and soft policies

Exploring starts are a theoretical device. In practice, ε-soft behavior keeps every action possible, giving the learner continued coverage while the policy gradually favors better actions.

06 Off-policy importance sampling

A behavior policy can generate data for a different target policy. Ratios of target to behavior probabilities correct the mismatch, but products of ratios can create enormous variance.

07 Worked example

walk it throughIn blackjack, an episode ends in win, loss, or draw. Record each hand state—player sum, dealer card, usable ace—and the chosen action. The final reward becomes a sample return for all visited decisions, eventually revealing when hitting or sticking is better.

When you work through an example, keep the roles separate: the behavior policy produces experience, the target says what the current estimate should move toward, and the step size controls how far it moves. Many algorithms differ mainly in one of those three choices.

08 The easy mistake

watch outMonte Carlo does not mean “random search.” It means estimating expectations with samples. The policy may be carefully controlled; randomness is how uncertain returns are observed.

09 Questions people actually ask

Do I need to memorize every equation?

No. First learn the update pattern: old estimate plus step size times an error. Then identify how the target is built and whether data comes from the same policy being evaluated. Derive or look up the exact form after the mental model is stable.

What should I implement first?

Use the smallest environment that exposes the chapter's idea. Print intermediate values, fix random seeds for debugging, and compare against a simple baseline before scaling to a neural network.

How do I know whether learning is real?

Evaluate on multiple seeds, separate training behavior from the target or greedy policy, report distributions rather than one run, and inspect learned values or policies—not only total reward.

10 Key takeaways

  • Learning from episodes: Monte Carlo methods wait until termination, compute the actual sampled return from each visited state, and average those returns.
  • First-visit and every-visit: First-visit MC updates a state only for its first occurrence in an episode; every-visit updates all occurrences.
  • Action values for control: Without a model, state values alone cannot compare unseen actions.
  • Exploration and soft policies: Exploring starts are a theoretical device.
  • Off-policy importance sampling: A behavior policy can generate data for a different target policy.
