Chapter 6 — Temporal-Difference Learning, explained.

td-learningsarsaq-learningexpected-sarsadouble-learning

// the one-minute version

Learning before an episode ends by bootstrapping, then extending TD prediction to Sarsa, Q-learning, Expected Sarsa, and Double Q-learning.

Monte Carlo waits for the full trip before learning whether an early turn was good. Temporal-difference learning updates after every step by combining the reward just seen with its current prediction of what comes next.

01 The chapter in one mental model

core loopV(Sₜ) ← V(Sₜ) + α[Rₜ₊₁ + γV(Sₜ₊₁) − V(Sₜ)].

The notation can look dense, but the purpose is practical: turn experience into a better prediction or a better decision. Read every equation by naming what is known now, what new evidence arrived, and which estimate is being moved toward that evidence.

02 The TD error

δₜ = Rₜ₊₁ + γV(Sₜ₊₁) − V(Sₜ) measures the surprise between the old prediction and a one-step-improved target. It is simultaneously an error signal and a compact carrier of new information.

03 Bootstrapping

The target uses another learned estimate instead of waiting for a final return. That introduces bias but enables online, continuing learning and often greatly reduces variance.

04 Sarsa

Sarsa updates Q(S,A) toward the reward plus the value of the action actually selected next. Because the behavior policy appears in the update, it learns the value of its own exploratory behavior.

05 Q-learning and Expected Sarsa

Q-learning targets the best next action regardless of what behavior does, making it off-policy. Expected Sarsa averages over next actions under the target policy, trading computation for lower sampling variance.

06 Maximization bias

Using the same noisy estimates to select and evaluate a maximum creates optimism. Double learning separates selection from evaluation, reducing this bias.

07 Worked example

walk it throughIn cliff walking, Sarsa learns a safer route because its updates include occasional exploratory steps that can fall off the cliff. Q-learning learns the value of the greedy cliff-edge route even while behavior still explores. Neither is universally “better”; they answer different policy questions.

When you work through an example, keep the roles separate: the behavior policy produces experience, the target says what the current estimate should move toward, and the step size controls how far it moves. Many algorithms differ mainly in one of those three choices.

08 The easy mistake

watch outQ-learning is not automatically superior because it is off-policy. Its max target can be noisy, function approximation can destabilize it, and its learned greedy policy may ignore the cost of exploration during training.

09 Questions people actually ask

Do I need to memorize every equation?

No. First learn the update pattern: old estimate plus step size times an error. Then identify how the target is built and whether data comes from the same policy being evaluated. Derive or look up the exact form after the mental model is stable.

What should I implement first?

Use the smallest environment that exposes the chapter's idea. Print intermediate values, fix random seeds for debugging, and compare against a simple baseline before scaling to a neural network.

How do I know whether learning is real?

Evaluate on multiple seeds, separate training behavior from the target or greedy policy, report distributions rather than one run, and inspect learned values or policies—not only total reward.
