Week 1 · Foundations · Days 1–7

Week 1 — Foundations.

The four moves every conversation stands on: open it, listen, find shared ground, and pull stories out — yours and theirs. Get these reflexive and everything later gets easier.

Day 1↑

Mastering Icebreakers

The hardest part of any conversation is the first ten seconds — the "who speaks first, and what do I even say" tension. A good icebreaker dissolves it and hands the other person an easy on-ramp. You're not trying to impress; you're trying to lower the cost of replying to near zero.

Principle: warmth opens doors that cleverness can't. A plain friendly line beats a witty one delivered tensely.

How — step by step
  • Use the situation you share. The single most reliable opener is observation + question: notice something in your shared context and ask about it. You're already in the same place, so it never feels random.
  • Make it cheap to answer. No deep thinking, no risk, no commitment. "First time here?" is easier to answer than "What do you do?"
  • Lead with the body, not just the words. Soft eye contact, a real smile, open posture. People decide whether to engage from your face before your sentence finishes.
  • Have three go-to openers ready so you never freeze: a situational one, a friendly logistical one, and a genuine compliment.
Scripts
"This queue's moving faster than I expected — you here for the same thing?" "That's a great jacket — where's it from?" "Quite a turnout tonight. How do you know the host?"
Scenario
Coffee shop, you're both waiting. Instead of staring at your phone: glance at their cup → "That looked good — what did you get?" → they answer → "Oh nice, I always default to the same thing, I should branch out." You've started a conversation from nothing, using only the shared moment.
Drills

Starter: start three conversations today with observation+question (barista, lift, queue). Reps, not outcomes.

Stretch: open with a genuine, specific compliment to one stranger and follow it with a question.

Common mistakes

• Over-rehearsing until it sounds robotic. • Opening with a heavy question ("what do you do?") that demands effort. • Mumbling it while looking away — kills it before it starts.

What good looks like: you can walk up to a stranger, say one warm easy thing, and they smile and answer without hesitation.

Day 2↑

The Art of Active Listening

Most people don't listen — they wait to talk. Because that's the norm, simply actually listening makes you stand out and makes the other person feel rare and valued. It's the highest-leverage social skill there is, and almost no one practises it.

Principle: people don't remember what you said; they remember how understood you made them feel.

How — step by step
  • Listen to understand, not to reply. Consciously drop the urge to compose your next line while they're still talking. Trust that a response will come.
  • Reflect back. Briefly paraphrase what they said in your own words — "So the timing was the real problem, not the work itself?" This proves you got it and invites correction.
  • Use minimal encouragers. Nods, "mhm," a tilt of the head, soft eye contact — small signals that say "keep going."
  • Ask the follow-up they didn't expect. A second question about what they just said ("wait, say more about that") shows you were truly tracking.
Scripts
"So it sounds like what bugged you most was…" "Wait — say more about that part." "What did that feel like at the time?"
Scenario
A colleague vents about a rough project. Bad: "Oh that happened to me too, one time I—". Good: "That sounds exhausting — was it the workload or the constant changes?" They feel heard, open up more, and remember you as easy to talk to.
Drills

Starter: in one conversation, speak only to ask or reflect — no opinions, no stories. Watch how much they open up.

Stretch: after someone finishes a point, summarise it in one sentence before responding, all day.

Common mistakes

• Fake listening (visibly waiting your turn) — people feel it. • Hijacking with "that reminds me of my…". • Solving before they finish (see Day 32).

What good looks like: people say "you're so easy to talk to" without being able to say why. The why is this.

Day 3↑

Finding Common Ground

Shared ground turns a stranger into "someone like me," which is the fastest route to ease and trust. The brain relaxes around similarity. Your job early on is to hunt for the overlap and then build on it.

Principle: connection is built on "me too," not on "look at me."

How — step by step
  • Fish broadly, then dig. Ask wide-net questions (work, place, hobbies, how they know the host) until something clicks — then drill into that one thing.
  • Ladder up to universals. No specific overlap? Go general — food, travel, weekend plans, the shared event you're both at. Everyone has a take on those.
  • Name the overlap out loud. "Oh, you run too?" — saying it makes the bond explicit and gives you a thread to pull.
  • Stack overlaps. Each shared thing you find compounds the sense of "we click."
Scripts
"How do you spend time when you're not working?" "Wait, you're from there too? Whereabouts?" "Same — I thought I was the only one who…"
Scenario
At a wedding, seated next to a stranger. Fish: "How do you know the couple?" → "We worked together in Berlin." → dig: "Berlin! I spent a summer there — which part?" One question found the overlap; now you have twenty minutes of easy talk.
Drills

Starter: in each chat today, find one genuine shared thing and build 2+ minutes on it.

Stretch: with someone who seems very different from you, find a real overlap anyway.

Common mistakes

• Faking interest in their thing — transparent. • Stopping at the first overlap instead of digging. • Making it about proving you're similar rather than being curious.

What good looks like: within a few minutes you've found something real you share, and the conversation has somewhere natural to go.

Day 4↑

Asking Open-Ended Questions

Closed questions ("Do you like your job?") get one-word answers and dead air. Open ones ("How did you end up in that field?") hand the other person room to run — and give you material to follow. Open questions are the engine that keeps a conversation moving without effort from you.

Principle: the quality of a conversation is set by the quality of your questions.

How — step by step
  • Start with how / what / why. These structurally can't be answered in one word. "How did you get into that?" beats "Do you like it?"
  • Ask about experience, not facts. "What was that like?" pulls a story; "When did you start?" pulls a date.
  • Follow the energy. When they light up, ask more there — that's where the good conversation is.
  • Trade, don't grill. After two or three questions, share something of your own so it's a conversation, not an interview.
Scripts
"What got you started on that?" "What's the best part of it for you?" "How did that change things for you?"
Scenario
Meeting someone new. Closed: "So you're a teacher? Do you like it?" → "Yeah, it's good." (dead end). Open: "What made you want to teach?" → a real story about a mentor, which you can follow anywhere.
Drills

Starter: ban yes/no questions for a day. Convert each into how/what/why.

Stretch: keep one conversation alive for five minutes using only open questions + brief shares.

Common mistakes

• Rapid-fire interrogation with no sharing. • Asking "why" in a way that sounds like a challenge. • Defaulting to the same tired three questions everyone asks (Day 26 fixes this).

What good looks like: the other person is doing most of the talking and enjoying it, and you never run dry on what to ask next.

Day 5↑

Reading Body Language

A large share of any message is non-verbal — posture, gaze, distance, the angle of someone's feet. Reading it tells you in real time whether to lean in, lighten up, go deeper, or wrap up. It's the difference between talking at someone and talking with them.

Principle: read clusters, not single signals. One crossed arm means nothing; arms + leaning away + short answers means disengaged.

How — step by step
  • Watch feet and torso. Where they point is where the person wants to go. Aimed at you = engaged; aimed at the exit = mentally leaving.
  • Track engagement shifts. Brightening eyes and leaning in = go deeper here. Glances away, shifting weight = change pace or wrap up.
  • Mind distance. If they step back, you're too close; if they step in, they're comfortable.
  • Project open, relaxed body yourself — uncrossed, facing them, unhurried. It invites the same back.
Scenario
Mid-conversation, their answers get shorter and their body angles toward the door. Don't push harder — that's the cue to either re-energise ("on a totally different note, did you…") or exit gracefully (Day 11). Reading it saves you from being "that person who wouldn't let me leave."
Drills

Starter: in one chat, silently track their engagement and adjust — speed up if they fade, go deeper if they lean in.

Stretch: spend ten minutes in a public place just reading pairs of people — guess who's engaged, who's not, from body alone.

Common mistakes

• Over-reading a single gesture (cold room ≠ rejection). • Getting so analytical you stop being present. • Ignoring your own closed body language.

What good looks like: you adjust the conversation to the other person without them ever telling you to — and they feel comfortable as a result.

Day 6↑

Handling Awkward Silences

Silence always feels longer than it is. The skill isn't preventing every pause — it's staying calm inside one, which signals confidence and stops you from panic-babbling. Comfortable people let small silences breathe.

Principle: a two-second pause is normal and fine. Your discomfort, not the silence, is the problem to fix.

How — step by step
  • Reframe the pause as natural. Conversations breathe. Not every gap needs filling instantly.
  • Keep a reset question in your pocket. A light, broad one revives any lull: "So what's been the best part of your week?"
  • Call back. Return to something they mentioned earlier: "You said you just moved — how's that going?" Shows you were listening and reopens a thread.
  • Let some silences stand. A held pause often draws out what the other person was about to say anyway.
Scripts
"So what's been keeping you busy lately?" "Going back to what you said earlier about…"
Scenario
The conversation stalls; both go quiet. Panic move: blurt something random and fumbling. Calm move: a relaxed two-count, a small smile, then "What got you into [thing they mentioned]?" The pause becomes a pivot, not a death.
Drills

Starter: when a silence hits, count one-two before speaking. Practise being comfortable, not frantic.

Stretch: deliberately let one silence last three seconds and see who fills it (often them).

Common mistakes

• Filling every gap with nervous filler. • Treating a pause as proof you're failing. • Asking a question so broad it stalls again ("so… yeah").

What good looks like: silences don't rattle you; you either let them sit or reopen with a question, calm throughout.

Day 7↑

Storytelling 101

Facts slide off; stories stick. A short, well-told anecdote makes you engaging and relatable, and gives the other person something to react to. You don't need dramatic material — a 30-second story about your week, well-shaped, beats a grand tale told flat.

Principle: every story needs one point and a clear shape. No shape, no payoff — just talking.

How — step by step
  • Use a simple arc: set-up → small tension → payoff. "I was running late… [tension]… turns out I'd had the meeting in my calendar for the wrong day entirely."
  • Keep it under a minute. Brevity is the soul of a good anecdote; cut every detail that doesn't serve the point.
  • Land one feeling. Funny, surprising, relatable — pick one and aim the whole story at it.
  • Be slightly the fool. A little self-deprecation makes stories warm, not braggy, and gives people permission to relate.
  • Hand it back. End with a question or hook so it becomes a trade, not a monologue.
Scenario
Someone asks "how was your weekend?" Flat: "Fine, just relaxed." Story: "Honestly chaotic — I tried to assemble a bookshelf, ran out of screws halfway, and ended up with a very modern 'floating shelf' look I'm pretending was intentional. You do anything?" You shared, made them smile, and handed it back.
Drills

Starter: prepare one 30-second story from your week (set-up, tension, payoff). Tell it once today.

Stretch: tell the same story to two people; tighten it the second time by cutting one detail.

Common mistakes

• The never-ending story with no point. • Too much set-up before the tension. • Making yourself the hero instead of the human.

What good looks like: you can turn an ordinary moment into a tight, warm, 30-second story that earns a laugh or a "same!" and then passes the turn.
