Week 2 · Rapport & Flow · Days 8–14

Week 2 — Rapport & Flow.

Foundations are set; now make conversations warm and keep them moving. Compliments that land, remembering people, holding a group, leaving cleanly, light topics, your own stories, and humour.

Day 8↑

Compliments That Land

A specific compliment is a small gift; a generic one is background noise. Done well, it warms a room instantly and makes the other person glad they ran into you. The trick is specificity and timing — then getting out of the way.

Principle: praise the choice, not the luck. "I loved how you handled that question" beats "you're so smart."

How — step by step
  • Be specific. Name the exact thing: "the way you explained that to the new person was so patient" lands; "good job" evaporates.
  • Compliment effort, taste, or skill — things they chose — over things they didn't (looks, luck). It means more and feels less loaded.
  • Then move on. Give it, let it land, and continue. Don't linger or fish for one in return.
Scripts
"That was a really sharp point — I hadn't thought of it that way." "You explained that so clearly. You've clearly done this a lot."
Scenario
After a meeting. Generic: "Nice job today." Specific: "The way you re-framed the budget question kept the whole thing from derailing — that was great." The second one they'll remember for a week.
Drills

Starter: give two specific, choice-based compliments today. Watch the reaction vs a generic one.

Stretch: compliment someone on something non-obvious you genuinely noticed.

Common mistakes

• Flattery that feels transactional. • Over-complimenting until it's awkward. • Immediately fishing for one back.

What good looks like: your compliments visibly lift people because they're specific and clearly genuine.

Day 9↑

Remembering Names & Details

Using someone's name, and recalling a detail they shared, is one of the simplest ways to make a person feel they matter. Most people forget a name within seconds — so remembering is a small superpower that signals real attention.

Principle: a person's own name is, to them, the warmest word in the language. Use it — sparingly and well.

How — step by step
  • Say it back immediately. "Nice to meet you, Priya." Repetition at the moment of hearing locks it in.
  • Attach a hook. Link the name to a feature, a rhyme, someone you know with the same name — anything memorable.
  • Use it once more before parting — "Great talking, Priya" — which both cements it and warms the goodbye.
  • Note it after. Jot names + one detail in your phone after an event; review before the next one.
Scenario
Introduced to three people at once. Instead of nodding through it: "Tom, Aisha, Raj — got it." Then use one name in your next sentence to a specific person. The act of using it is what makes it stick.
Drills

Starter: remember three new names today by repeating each aloud once and using it again before you part.

Stretch: the next day, message or greet one of them using a detail they shared.

Common mistakes

• Zoning out during the introduction. • "I'm terrible with names" as a self-fulfilling excuse. • Overusing the name until it sounds like a sales script.

What good looks like: you reliably catch and reuse names, and people are quietly flattered you remembered.

Day 10↑

Navigating Group Conversations

Groups have different physics than one-on-one: timing and inclusion matter more than depth, and the rewards go to the person who makes the group feel good, not the one who talks most.

Principle: in a group, the connector beats the performer. Bringing others in wins more than holding the floor.

How — step by step
  • Enter on a pause. Find the natural gap; don't talk over. A short relevant add earns you a place.
  • Pull quiet people in. "Sam, you'd know about this — what do you think?" Including someone makes you the host of the moment.
  • Keep your turns short. Brevity keeps you welcome; long turns in a group lose the room.
  • Track who hasn't spoken and create an opening for them.
Scenario
A group of four, one person has gone quiet. You: "We've been hearing from us loud ones — Maya, what's your take?" Maya lights up, the group warms, and you're remembered as the person who made it inclusive.
Drills

Starter: in one group today, deliberately bring a quiet person into the conversation.

Stretch: in a group, speak less than average but make two others look good.

Common mistakes

• Monologuing — fastest way to lose a group. • Talking only to one person and ignoring the rest. • Interrupting to insert yourself.

What good looks like: groups feel better when you're in them, and people you included seek you out afterward.

Day 11↑

Exiting Conversations Gracefully

How you leave is the last impression you give — and it sticks. A clean exit frees you both without awkwardness and leaves the door open for next time. Most people fumble this and either trap themselves or vanish rudely.

Principle: end on warmth and a reason, not on a fade-out.

How — step by step
  • Signal + appreciate + reason. "This has been great — I'm going to grab a drink / go say hi to someone." Honest, kind, clear.
  • Leave the door open. "Let's pick this up later" or swap contacts so it's a pause, not a close.
  • End on their note. Close by referencing something they said — it makes the exit feel warm, not abrupt.
Scripts
"I've really enjoyed this — I should mingle a bit, but let's swap numbers." "I'm going to grab some food — come find me if you want to continue that."
Scenario
You want to move on but it's going well. Don't trap yourself for another 20 minutes out of politeness. "This has been the best conversation I've had tonight — I'm going to circulate, but find me before you leave." You exit clean and flattering.
Drills

Starter: end one conversation on purpose with appreciate + reason + door-open.

Stretch: exit a going-well conversation gracefully (harder than exiting a dull one).

Common mistakes

• Fading out / vanishing. • Inventing an obvious fake excuse. • Staying trapped because you can't leave politely.

What good looks like: you leave conversations whenever you need to, and people feel good about it rather than dismissed.

Day 12↑

Discussing Current Events

Light current-events talk is universal small-talk fuel — everyone's exposed to the same big stories, so it's common ground on tap. The skill is keeping it curious and interesting rather than heated.

Principle: discuss what's interesting, not what's inflammatory. Curiosity connects; debate divides.

How — step by step
  • Favour the fascinating. Science breakthroughs, sport, tech, culture, a wild local story — over hot-button politics with people you barely know.
  • Ask their take first. "Did you see that? What did you make of it?" invites a conversation instead of delivering a lecture.
  • Keep 2–3 light items ready. Skim headlines before an event so you always have a thread.
Scripts
"Did you catch that story about—? I couldn't stop thinking about it." "What's your read on it?"
Scenario
Lull at a dinner. "Random, but did you see they found that new thing in the deep sea? What do you reckon's down there we still don't know about?" Light, open-ended, zero conflict — and weirdly memorable.
Drills

Starter: bring up one light, interesting current item and ask the other person's view.

Stretch: have two such items ready before walking into a social event.

Common mistakes

• Steering into divisive territory with new people (see Day 15). • Lecturing instead of asking. • Having nothing topical to offer.

What good looks like: you can spark an easy, interesting exchange about the world without it ever getting tense.

Day 13↑

Sharing Personal Anecdotes

Trading small personal stories is how acquaintances become friends. It signals trust and gives the relationship texture. The art is matching depth and keeping it a trade, not a turn-steal.

Principle: match their depth, then hand it back. Sharing is a volley, not a serve.

How — step by step
  • Mirror their level. If they shared something light, share light; if they went a bit personal, you can too. Don't overshoot a near-stranger.
  • Tie it to the topic. An anecdote that connects to what they just said feels generous; an unrelated one feels self-centred.
  • End with a question. "…anyway, has that ever happened to you?" turns your share into a trade.
Scenario
They mention a nerve-wracking first day at a job. You: "Oh I know that feeling — my first day I locked myself out of the building twice before lunch. Did yours settle down quickly?" Relatable, brief, handed back.
Drills

Starter: share one relevant personal anecdote, then return the floor with a question.

Stretch: consciously match a slightly more personal share with one of your own (without over-sharing).

Common mistakes

• One-upping with a bigger story. • Over-sharing with someone you just met. • Forgetting to hand it back.

What good looks like: your stories deepen the connection and always loop back to them.

Day 14↑

Using Humor Effectively

Shared laughter is instant bonding and tension relief — when it's inclusive. You don't need to be funny; you need to be light. A small, warm joke does more for rapport than a polished bit ever could.

Principle: laugh with, never at. Self-deprecating and observational humour are safe; teasing strangers isn't.

How — step by step
  • Self-deprecate lightly. Gentle jokes at your own expense make you warm and approachable — just don't overdo it into self-attack.
  • Joke about the shared situation, not about the person. The long queue, the chaotic event, the weather — safe and unifying.
  • Read the room first. Match the group's tone before you joke; a wisecrack in a serious moment misfires.
  • Aim for a smile, not a laugh. Low stakes, high warmth.
Scenario
Both stuck at a slow buffet. "At this rate we'll have a real bond by the time we reach the salad." Light, situational, includes you both — and breaks the ice instantly.
Drills

Starter: make one light situational or self-deprecating joke today. Aim for a smile.

Stretch: use humour to defuse one mildly awkward moment.

Common mistakes

• Sarcasm/teasing with someone who doesn't know you yet. • Forcing jokes when the room's serious. • Punching down.

What good looks like: you lighten moments naturally and people relax around you — no stand-up required.
