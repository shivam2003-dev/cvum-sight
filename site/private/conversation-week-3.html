Week 3 · Depth & Presence · Days 15–21

Week 3 — Depth & Presence.

Move past pleasant into genuinely connecting. Steer around landmines, show real curiosity, use silence and mirroring, handle interruptions, follow up, and meet people's feelings with empathy.

Day 15↑

Avoiding Controversial Topics

Early in a relationship, divisive topics generate far more heat than connection. Knowing what to sidestep — and how to redirect smoothly when someone else raises it — keeps conversations warm and keeps you out of arguments you never wanted.

Principle: with new people, connection beats being right. Save the heavy stuff for trust you've actually built.

How — step by step
  • Default-safe with strangers: avoid politics, religion, and money until there's real rapport.
  • Redirect smoothly. "That's a whole conversation in itself — anyway, you were telling me about…" pivots without snubbing.
  • Stay neutral if pulled in. "I can honestly see both sides" then change lanes. You don't owe anyone a hot take.
  • Watch for the bait. Some people fish for a fight; don't bite.
Scenario
A new acquaintance launches into a polarising rant. Instead of engaging or freezing: "Yeah, it's a lot — people feel really strongly. Anyway, you mentioned you just got back from travelling, where'd you go?" Neutral, warm, and you're safely onto better ground.
Drills

Starter: practise one smooth redirect away from a charged topic without making it weird.

Stretch: stay neutral and pivot when someone tries to bait you into a debate.

Common mistakes

• Taking the bait to "win." • Sharing strong opinions before trust exists. • Freezing instead of redirecting.

What good looks like: you glide past landmines without tension and keep the conversation pleasant.

Day 16↑

Showing Genuine Interest

Curiosity is magnetic. People are drawn to those who are genuinely interested in them — and they can tell the difference between real interest and polite performance instantly. The good news: real interest is a choice you can make.

Principle: assume everyone you meet knows something you don't. Your job is to find it.

How — step by step
  • Get genuinely curious. Decide, before the conversation, to find the interesting thing in this person. It reframes the whole interaction.
  • Ask the second and third follow-up. Anyone can ask one question; interest shows in going deeper on their answer.
  • Remember and reference. Bringing back a detail they mentioned earlier proves you actually cared.
Scenario
Someone says they keep bees. Polite: "Oh, cool." Interested: "Wait, really? How did you get into that? Aren't you scared of getting stung? What got you hooked?" Three follow-ups and they're delighted — most people never ask the second question.
Drills

Starter: pick one person and go three follow-up questions deep on something they care about.

Stretch: find the genuinely interesting thing about someone you'd normally write off as boring.

Common mistakes

• Performing interest while visibly waiting to talk. • Stopping at one question. • Asking but not listening to the answer.

What good looks like: people leave conversations with you feeling unusually interesting — because you were unusually interested.

Day 17↑

The Power of Pauses

A pause gives weight to your words, room for thought, and an air of calm authority. Rushing to fill every gap reads as nervous; the person who can sit comfortably in a beat of silence reads as secure and considered.

Principle: silence is a tool, not a failure. The confident person owns the pause.

How — step by step
  • Pause before answering. A one-second beat signals you considered the question rather than reacting on autopilot.
  • Pause for emphasis. A short silence before a key word makes it land harder.
  • Let the pause draw them out. After they finish, a held beat often pulls out the deeper thing they were about to say.
Scenario
You're asked a real question in a group. Instead of an instant scattered answer: a calm one-second pause, eye contact, then a clear reply. The pause alone makes you sound more thoughtful than the content does.
Drills

Starter: add a deliberate one-second pause before answering questions today.

Stretch: after someone answers a question, hold a two-second pause and watch them add more.

Common mistakes

• Rushing answers to seem smart (reads nervous). • Filling every silence. • Pausing so long it becomes theatrical.

What good looks like: your pauses make you sound calm and deliberate, and they often surface what the other person really meant.

Day 18↑

Mirroring and Matching

Subtly matching someone's pace, tone, and energy builds rapport beneath conscious awareness — it signals "I'm like you, you're safe with me." Done lightly it's powerful; done obviously it's unsettling.

Principle: mirror the vibe, not the gestures. Match energy and pace, not poses.

How — step by step
  • Match energy and pace. Be calm with the calm person, lively with the lively one. Mismatched energy is what makes conversations feel "off."
  • Echo a key word. Reusing a distinctive word they chose quietly signals alignment.
  • Keep it subtle and natural. A light lean, a matched tone — never copy posture move-for-move.
Scenario
They speak slowly and thoughtfully; you're naturally fast. Consciously slow down to meet them. The conversation suddenly feels easier to both of you — you've removed the friction of mismatched tempo.
Drills

Starter: in one chat, gently match the other person's speaking pace and energy.

Stretch: notice a mismatch mid-conversation and adjust toward them.

Common mistakes

• Obvious imitation (creepy). • Ignoring energy mismatch and wondering why it feels awkward. • Forcing your tempo on a slower person.

What good looks like: conversations feel smooth and "in sync," and you can't quite say why — that's the mirroring working.

Day 19↑

Dealing with Interruptions

Getting cut off rattles most people into either going silent and resentful or talking over the other person louder. Handling it with calm grace protects your point and your composure, and quietly models better behaviour.

Principle: stay warm and finish anyway. Composure beats volume.

How — step by step
  • Hold calmly. "Let me just finish this thought" — friendly tone, firm intent. Most interrupters yield.
  • Reclaim after. If you do get derailed, circle back: "Going back to what I was saying…"
  • Don't retaliate. Interrupting back starts a talk-over war. Model the patience you want.
Scenario
A colleague keeps jumping in mid-sentence in a meeting. Calm hold: "One sec, let me land this point — then I want to hear your take." You stay unruffled, finish, and even hand them the floor — you look like the grown-up in the room.
Drills

Starter: if interrupted today, calmly finish your point once using a hold phrase.

Stretch: reclaim a point you got fully derailed from, without irritation.

Common mistakes

• Going quiet and resentful. • Talking over them louder. • Snapping, which makes you look rattled.

What good looks like: interruptions don't fluster you; you finish your thought warmly and keep control of the exchange.

Day 20↑

Following Up

The follow-up is where acquaintances quietly become relationships — and almost nobody does it, which is exactly why it makes you memorable. A short, specific message a day or two later does more than an hour of charm in the moment.

Principle: the relationship is built after the conversation, not during it.

How — step by step
  • Reference a specific. "Hope the house move went smoothly!" beats "great to meet you" — it proves you listened.
  • Time it right. Within a day or two: soon enough to be remembered, not so soon it's odd.
  • Give, don't just ask. Send the article, the contact, the recommendation you mentioned. Lead with value.
Scenario
You met someone who mentioned a band they love. Two days later: "Saw this gig announced and thought of our chat — they're playing near you next month." That tiny gesture turns a one-off into an actual contact.
Drills

Starter: message one person from a recent conversation, referencing something specific they shared.

Stretch: follow up with something useful to them, not just a hello.

Common mistakes

• Generic "nice meeting you" with no hook. • Never following up at all. • Only reaching out when you need something.

What good looks like: people are pleasantly surprised you reached out, and your network quietly grows from single conversations.

Day 21↑

Practicing Empathy

Empathy — feeling with someone rather than just hearing them — is the deepest connector there is. When a person feels truly understood, the relationship changes gear. Most people skip straight to fixing or relating-it-back; empathy means staying with their experience first.

Principle: people usually want to be understood, not solved. Meet the feeling before the problem.

How — step by step
  • Name the feeling. "That sounds really frustrating" shows you got the emotion, not just the facts.
  • Resist the fix reflex. Hold off on advice (Day 32) until you've shown you understand — or until they ask.
  • Briefly imagine their position. A second of genuinely picturing their situation changes your tone for the better.
Scripts
"That sounds genuinely exhausting." "I can see why that would really get to you."
Scenario
A friend is stressed about a sick parent. Fix-first: "Have you tried calling a different doctor?" Empathy-first: "That's a lot to carry — I'm really sorry you're dealing with this." The second response is what they'll remember; advice can come later, if wanted.
Drills

Starter: when someone shares a frustration today, name the emotion before anything else.

Stretch: sit with someone's difficult feeling for a full minute without trying to fix it.

Common mistakes

• Jumping to advice. • "At least…" minimising. • Relating it back to yourself too fast ("oh I know, when I…").

What good looks like: people open up to you about real things, because they trust you'll understand rather than fix or judge.
