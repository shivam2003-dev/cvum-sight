Course 06 · Business Writing & Email · 3 modules · 12 lessons

Business Writing & Email.

Most professional communication is written, and most of it is bad — long, buried, vague. This course makes your writing clear, concise, and actionable: emails that get read and acted on, docs that get decisions, updates people actually finish.

Module 1 · Principles of Clear Writing

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

Write for the Reader

Bad writing serves the writer (dumping everything in your head); good writing serves the reader (giving them exactly what they need to understand and act). Before writing a word, ask who reads this and what they need from it.

Principle: writing is a tool to change what the reader knows or does — not a transcript of your thoughts.

How
  • Name the reader and their need: what do they know, what do they want, how much time do they have?
  • Decide the one action/takeaway you want from them.
  • Cut everything that doesn't serve that — context for you isn't context for them.
Scenario
A long email explaining your whole investigation. Reader-first version: they only need the decision and the one risk. The 8 paragraphs of process become 3 sentences. They read it, they act.
Drills

Starter: before your next email, write one line: "reader needs ___ and should do ___."

Stretch: cut a long message in half by removing what only matters to you.

Common mistakes

• Writing to offload your brain. • Including all context "to be safe." • Forgetting the desired action.

What good looks like: readers get exactly what they need, fast, and know what to do.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Bottom Line Up Front (BLUF)

Readers skim, and they decide in the first line whether to keep reading. Putting the conclusion, ask, or key point first — BLUF — means even a skimmer gets the point. Burying it loses them.

Principle: answer first, explain second. The first sentence carries the message; everything else is support.

How
  • Open with the point: the decision, request, or headline result.
  • Put the ask in the first two sentences if you need something.
  • Layer detail below for those who want it; use it as backup, not buildup.
Scripts
"Need your approval by Thursday to keep the launch on track. Details below." "Short version: the test passed; we're clear to ship. Context if useful: …"
Drills

Starter: rewrite your next email so the first sentence is the bottom line.

Stretch: take a buried-point message and move the point to the top.

Common mistakes

• Building up to a buried point. • Putting the ask at the very bottom. • Making the reader hunt.

What good looks like: even a 3-second skim gets your point and your ask.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

Clarity & Concision

Long words and long sentences feel "professional" but cost comprehension. Clear writing uses plain words, short sentences, and active voice. Concise writing cuts every word that isn't pulling weight.

Principle: shorter and plainer wins. If a simpler word works, use it; if a word can go, cut it.

How
  • Plain words: "use" not "utilise," "buy" not "procure," "help" not "facilitate."
  • Short sentences — one idea each. Break the 40-word monster into three.
  • Active voice: "We missed the deadline" not "the deadline was missed."
  • Cut filler: "in order to" → "to," "due to the fact that" → "because," "at this point in time" → "now."
Scenario
Before: "In order to facilitate the optimisation of our processes, it was decided that a meeting should be convened." After: "Let's meet to improve our process." Same meaning, a third the words, twice the clarity.
Drills

Starter: take one paragraph and cut 30% of the words without losing meaning.

Stretch: rewrite a passive, jargon-heavy message in plain active voice.

Common mistakes

• Big words to sound smart. • Sprawling sentences. • Passive voice hiding who did what.

What good looks like: your writing is effortless to read — plain, short, active, no waste.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

Structure & Formatting

A wall of text doesn't get read; structure invites it. Headers, short paragraphs, bullets, and bold for the key bits let a reader scan and find what matters — which is how people actually read at work.

Principle: format for the skimmer. If it can't be scanned, it won't be read.

How
  • Short paragraphs (1–3 sentences) with white space between.
  • Bullets for lists, not prose — anything you'd separate with "and… and… and."
  • Bold the key phrase in long messages so the eye catches it.
  • Headers/sections for anything over a screen.
Scenario
A dense five-paragraph update. Reformatted: a one-line BLUF, then bolded "Status / Risk / Need" sub-points with bullets. The reader scans it in 10 seconds and replies to your ask. The content didn't change — the scannability did.
Drills

Starter: reformat one long message with bullets, white space, and one bold key line.

Stretch: add scannable headers to a doc so a reader can find any section instantly.

Common mistakes

• Walls of text. • No emphasis, so everything reads as equal. • Lists written as run-on prose.

What good looks like: a reader can scan your writing and extract the point and the ask in seconds.

Module 2 · Email That Works

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

Subject Lines

The subject line decides whether your email is opened, when, and how it's filed. A vague subject ("update") gets ignored; a specific, action-signalling one gets opened and answered.

Principle: the subject is a headline + a signal of what's needed. Make it specific and action-clear.

How
  • Be specific: "Q3 budget approval needed by Thu" beats "budget."
  • Signal the action with a tag: [Action needed], [FYI], [Decision], [EOD].
  • Front-load the key words — many read subjects truncated on mobile.
Scripts
"[Action by Fri] Sign-off needed: launch checklist" "[FYI] Migration done — no action needed"
Drills

Starter: rewrite your next subject line to be specific + action-signalling.

Stretch: adopt [Action]/[FYI]/[Decision] tags for a week and see response speed change.

Common mistakes

• Vague subjects ("hi", "update", "quick question"). • No action signal. • Burying the key word at the end.

What good looks like: people open your emails promptly and know from the subject what's needed.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

The Short Email

Long emails don't get read — or get read late. A disciplined short email (roughly five sentences or fewer) forces clarity and gets faster responses. Brevity is a courtesy and a tactic.

Principle: if it's longer than a screen, it's probably a doc or a call — not an email.

How
  • Aim for ~5 sentences: context (1) → point/ask (1–2) → next step/deadline (1) → close.
  • One email, one purpose. Multiple asks get half-answered; split them.
  • If it's getting long, switch to a doc (with a BLUF summary) or a quick call.
Scenario
A sprawling six-paragraph request. Trimmed: "Quick one — can you approve the attached by Thursday? It unblocks the launch. Shout if anything's unclear. Thanks." Answered in minutes instead of ignored for days.
Drills

Starter: hold your next email to five sentences.

Stretch: split a multi-ask email into separate, single-purpose ones.

Common mistakes

• Essay-length emails. • Multiple asks in one. • Using email for what needs a call.

What good looks like: your emails are short, single-purpose, and get fast clear answers.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Tone in Writing

Writing strips out tone of voice and body language, so neutral text often reads as cold or curt. Deliberate warmth, clarity about intent, and care with terse phrasing prevent misreads that cause real friction.

Principle: in writing, assume your tone reads colder than you mean — so warm it up on purpose.

How
  • Add small warmth: a brief greeting, "thanks," "hope this helps" — cheap, high-impact.
  • Soften terse asks that could read as orders: "Could you…" / "When you get a chance…".
  • State intent when something could be misread: "Genuine question, not a criticism —".
  • Re-read as the recipient before sending, especially when annoyed.
Scenario
You write "Why wasn't this done?" Reads as an attack. Warmed: "Hey — I noticed this isn't done yet, anything blocking it? Happy to help." Same question, no fight started.
Drills

Starter: re-read your next email as the recipient and warm one cold line.

Stretch: when annoyed, draft, wait an hour, then re-read before sending.

Common mistakes

• Terse text that reads as hostile. • Sending while angry. • No warmth at all, so it feels robotic.

What good looks like: your writing reads as warm and clear, and rarely causes a tone-based misunderstanding.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

Driving Action & Follow-up

An email's job is usually to make something happen. If the recipient closes it unsure what to do, it failed — no matter how well-written. Be explicit about who does what by when, and follow up cleanly.

Principle: never leave the next step ambiguous. Name the who, the what, and the when.

How
  • Spell out the action: "Priya, can you review by Wed; Sam, you're cc'd for awareness."
  • One clear deadline, not "soon" or "when you can."
  • Separate FYI from action — make clear who actually needs to do something.
  • Follow up on no-response with a short, friendly nudge referencing the deadline.
Scenario
You need a decision from a group. Not: "thoughts, everyone?" (silence). Instead: "Decision needed: A or B? @Maya you're the decider; others, input by Tue EOD." Now it moves.
Drills

Starter: end your next request email with explicit who/what/when.

Stretch: send a clean, friendly follow-up on a stalled thread referencing the deadline.

Common mistakes

• Vague "thoughts?" with no owner. • "Soon" instead of a date. • Everyone cc'd, no one responsible.

What good looks like: your emails reliably produce the action you needed, on time.

Module 3 · Documents & Beyond

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Status Updates That Get Read

Status updates are read by busy people who want the signal, not the diary. A good update leads with status, flags risks, and names what's needed — so a reader gets the picture in seconds and knows if they must act.

Principle: an update answers "are we okay, and do you need me?" — fast.

How
  • Lead with a one-line status — on track / at risk / blocked (a colour or word).
  • Structure it: Status · Progress · Risks · Asks. Predictable so readers find what they want.
  • Flag risks honestly and early — surprises destroy trust.
  • Keep it short and regular — cadence beats length.
Scenario
Weekly project update. "🟡 At risk. Progress: core done. Risk: vendor slow, may slip 2 days. Ask: can you escalate to their account manager?" A reader knows everything that matters instantly.
Drills

Starter: write your next update with a one-line status + Status/Progress/Risks/Asks.

Stretch: standardise that format for your recurring updates.

Common mistakes

• Diary-style "did this, then this." • Hiding risks. • No clear status or ask.

What good looks like: readers grasp status and any needed action in seconds, every time.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Proposals & Persuasive Docs

A proposal has to inform and persuade. The winning structure mirrors how decisions get made: the recommendation up front, the problem and reasoning, the options considered, and the ask — so a reader can approve without digging.

Principle: recommendation first, reasoning second. Decision-makers want your answer, then your working.

How
  • Open with a summary: recommendation + the one-line why + the ask.
  • State the problem in terms the decider cares about (Persuasion L2).
  • Show options + your pick, with honest trade-offs — it builds trust.
  • End with a clear ask and next steps.
Scenario
Proposing a tool purchase. Page one: "Recommend we buy X (£Y). It cuts Z hours/week and pays back in 3 months. Asking for sign-off by month-end." A busy exec can approve from the first paragraph; the rest is backup.
Drills

Starter: add a one-paragraph recommendation summary to the top of your next proposal.

Stretch: present options with honest trade-offs and your clear pick.

Common mistakes

• Recommendation buried on page 4. • Hiding trade-offs. • No clear ask.

What good looks like: a decision-maker can approve from your summary, with the detail there if they want it.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Editing Ruthlessly

First drafts are bloated — that's their job. The quality comes in the edit: cutting, tightening, and clarifying. Most writing problems are fixed not by adding but by removing.

Principle: write to think, edit to communicate. The delete key is your best writing tool.

How
  • Cut 10–20% on a second pass — there's always slack.
  • Delete throat-clearing openings ("I just wanted to reach out to say…").
  • One idea per sentence; split any sentence you can't say in one breath.
  • Read it aloud — clumsy bits announce themselves.
Scenario
Draft opens: "I just wanted to quickly reach out and let you know that I was thinking that perhaps we might want to consider…" Edit: "Suggestion:…". Twenty words become one, and it's stronger.
Drills

Starter: edit your next message down by 15% before sending.

Stretch: read a draft aloud and cut every clumsy or hedging phrase.

Common mistakes

• Sending the first draft. • Adding instead of cutting. • Throat-clearing openers.

What good looks like: your final writing is tight — every sentence earns its place.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 · Finish↑

Async & Continuous Improvement

Remote and async work runs on writing — your words often stand in for you entirely, across time zones, without the chance to clarify live. Great async writing is self-contained, unambiguous, and findable later. And like all skill, it improves with deliberate review.

Principle: in async, write so no follow-up question is needed. Anticipate what they'll ask and answer it.

How
  • Be self-contained — include the context, links, and decisions so no one has to chase you.
  • Anticipate questions and pre-answer the obvious ones.
  • Write to be found — clear titles/headers so it's searchable later.
  • Review your own writing: when someone misreads you, treat it as a writing bug to fix next time.
Drills

Starter: make your next async message self-contained — no "let me know and I'll explain."

Stretch: next time you're misunderstood in writing, rewrite it to see what was ambiguous.

Common mistakes

• Messages that need a follow-up call to understand. • No context for later readers. • Never learning from misreads.

What good looks like: your async writing stands on its own, prevents back-and-forth, and keeps getting clearer.
