Course 04 · Difficult Conversations & Conflict · 3 modules · 12 lessons

Difficult Conversations & Conflict.

The conversations we avoid — feedback, disagreement, bad news, tension — are the ones that matter most. This course makes them survivable and even productive: prepare well, stay safe and curious, and resolve without damage.

Module 1 · Before You Open Your Mouth

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

Why Hard Conversations Go Wrong

Most difficult conversations derail for predictable reasons: we go in to "win," we assume bad intent, we lead with our conclusion, or we let emotion drive. Naming the failure modes is the first step to avoiding them.

Principle: the goal isn't to win — it's mutual understanding and a workable outcome. Reframe before you start.

How
  • Drop the "win" frame. Aim for understanding and a path forward, not victory.
  • Assume good (or neutral) intent. Most people aren't villains; they have reasons that make sense to them.
  • Manage your state first. If you're flooded with emotion, wait — nothing good gets said while triggered.
Scenario
A teammate missed a deadline that hurt you. Win-mode: "You let us all down." Reframe: "I want to understand what happened and make sure we're set up better next time." Same issue, completely different conversation.
Drills

Starter: before your next hard talk, write your goal as "understand + move forward," not "be right."

Stretch: when you notice you're triggered, postpone the conversation until you're calm.

Common mistakes

• Going in to win. • Assuming malice. • Speaking while flooded with emotion.

What good looks like: you enter hard conversations calm, curious, and aimed at a shared outcome.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Separate Facts, Story & Feelings

We blend three different things and treat them all as "the truth": what actually happened (facts), the story we tell ourselves about why (interpretation), and how we feel. Untangling them prevents most blow-ups.

Principle: your story about the facts is not the facts. Share the facts; hold the story lightly.

How
  • State observable facts — what a camera would have recorded — not your interpretation of them.
  • Own your story as a story: "The story I'm telling myself is…" invites correction instead of accusation.
  • Name feelings as yours, not as things they "made" you feel.
Scripts
"When the report came in two days late [fact], I started worrying you weren't on board [story], and I felt anxious [feeling]."
Drills

Starter: for one issue, write the facts, your story, and your feelings in three separate lines.

Stretch: in a real talk, explicitly label "the story I'm telling myself is…".

Common mistakes

• Presenting your story as fact. • "You made me feel…" blame. • Mixing all three into one accusation.

What good looks like: you separate what happened from what you assumed, so the other person can engage without getting defensive.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

Start With Safety

People can't think or talk honestly when they feel attacked. The first job in any hard conversation is making it safe — signalling you respect them and share a goal — before tackling the hard content.

Principle: no safety, no conversation. Establish respect and shared purpose first.

How
  • Open with shared purpose: "I'm raising this because I value our working relationship and want it to work well."
  • Affirm respect — make clear you're not attacking them as a person.
  • Watch for safety dropping (defensiveness, silence, withdrawal) and pause to rebuild it before continuing.
Scenario
Raising a recurring problem with a peer. Open: "I want to sort this out together because we work well otherwise and I don't want this one thing to fester." They relax — it's a problem to solve, not an ambush.
Drills

Starter: open your next hard talk by stating shared purpose and respect.

Stretch: mid-conversation, notice safety dropping and pause to restore it.

Common mistakes

• Diving into the hard content cold. • Ignoring rising defensiveness. • Sounding like an attack from word one.

What good looks like: the other person feels safe enough to be honest, because you made it clear you're on the same side.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

Lead With Curiosity

You almost never have the full picture. Leading with genuine questions — instead of conclusions — surfaces information that often changes the whole conversation, and it disarms defensiveness.

Principle: get curious before you get certain. Ask their side first.

How
  • Ask, then listen, before stating your view: "Help me understand what happened from your side."
  • Assume there's information you're missing — usually there is.
  • Use active listening (Conversation Day 2) — reflect what you hear before responding.
Scenario
You think someone dropped the ball. Instead of accusing: "I noticed X didn't happen — what got in the way?" Turns out they were blocked by something you didn't know. Curiosity just saved you from a wrong accusation.
Drills

Starter: open with a genuine question about their perspective before sharing yours.

Stretch: hold your conclusion entirely until you've fully understood their side.

Common mistakes

• Leading with your conclusion. • Asking questions you've already decided the answer to. • Not actually listening to the reply.

What good looks like: your questions surface the real story, and the conversation is grounded in facts, not assumptions.

Module 2 · In the Moment

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

Giving Feedback (SBI)

Vague feedback ("be more proactive") helps no one. The Situation–Behaviour–Impact model makes feedback specific, factual, and non-judgemental: when this happened, what you did, and the effect it had.

Principle: describe behaviour and impact, not character. "You did X and it caused Y" — not "you're careless."

How
  • Situation: anchor it — "In yesterday's client call…"
  • Behaviour: the specific, observable action — "you answered before the client finished their question."
  • Impact: the effect — "they looked unheard, and the rest of the call felt tense."
  • Then discuss — ask their view and agree a change.
Scripts
"In standup today [S], you cut off Priya twice [B], and she went quiet for the rest of it [I]. What were you seeing?"
Drills

Starter: rewrite one piece of feedback you owe someone in S-B-I form.

Stretch: deliver SBI feedback live, then ask for their perspective.

Common mistakes

• Judging character, not behaviour. • Vague feedback with no example. • Feedback sandwich that buries the point.

What good looks like: feedback is specific and behavioural, so people can actually act on it without feeling attacked.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

Receiving Feedback Well

How you take feedback determines whether you'll ever get useful feedback again. Defensiveness teaches people to stop being honest with you; curiosity invites the truth that helps you grow.

Principle: treat feedback as a gift of information, even when it's clumsy or partly wrong.

How
  • Listen fully without defending. Let them finish; resist the instant rebuttal.
  • Ask for specifics: "Can you give me an example?" turns vague into actionable.
  • Find the kernel of truth even in poorly-delivered feedback — there usually is one.
  • Thank them — it makes honesty safe and keeps the feedback coming.
Scenario
You get criticism that feels unfair. Instead of arguing: "That's hard to hear — can you walk me through a specific time you saw that?" You learn what's really meant, and even if it's 60% off, the 40% is gold.
Drills

Starter: next feedback you get, ask one clarifying question instead of defending.

Stretch: actively ask someone for feedback and practise receiving it without flinching.

Common mistakes

• Defending/explaining immediately. • Dismissing it because the delivery was poor. • Never thanking them.

What good looks like: people feel safe being honest with you, so you get the feedback that actually helps you improve.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Delivering Bad News

Bad news handled badly destroys trust twice — once for the news, once for the clumsy delivery. Handled well (clear, direct, human), it can actually strengthen a relationship because people see you don't dodge.

Principle: be direct and kind, not vague and "kind." Sugar-coating leaves people confused and more anxious.

How
  • Get to it quickly. Don't bury the news under five minutes of preamble — it raises dread.
  • Be clear and honest about what's happening and why, without hiding behind jargon or passive voice.
  • Acknowledge the impact and the feelings it'll cause; don't rush past them.
  • Offer what you can: next steps, support, options — control where there is some.
Scenario
A project is cancelled. Not: a long vague ramble ending in "so, unfortunately…". Instead: "I've got hard news — the project's been cancelled. Here's what happened, here's what it means for you, and here's what I'll do to help." Direct, honest, human.
Drills

Starter: draft a bad-news message that states the news in the first two sentences.

Stretch: deliver bad news in person, acknowledging the impact and offering next steps.

Common mistakes

• Long preamble that raises dread. • Vagueness/passive voice to soften it. • Rushing past the human impact.

What good looks like: people trust you more after bad news, because you were clear, honest, and humane.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

De-escalating Anger

When someone's angry, logic bounces off — their emotional brain is in charge. Trying to reason or defend escalates it. The way down is through the emotion: acknowledge it, lower your own temperature, and create space.

Principle: you can't reason with heat. Cool the emotion first, solve the problem second.

How
  • Stay calm and lower your volume/pace — people unconsciously mirror it (Conversation Day 18).
  • Acknowledge the feeling: "I can see this really matters to you, and you're frustrated." Naming it drains some heat.
  • Don't defend yet. Let them be fully heard before introducing any "but."
  • Create space if needed: "Let's take five and pick this up calmly."
Scenario
A colleague is venting angrily at you. Not: matching their volume or defending. Instead: calm, quieter voice — "You're clearly really frustrated, and I want to understand why. Tell me what happened." The temperature starts dropping because you didn't add to it.
Drills

Starter: next time someone's heated, lower your own voice and acknowledge their feeling before anything else.

Stretch: practise the "let's take five" reset on a conversation that's overheating.

Common mistakes

• Matching their volume. • Defending/reasoning while they're hot. • Telling them to "calm down" (guaranteed to inflame).

What good looks like: heated moments cool down around you because you stay calm and meet the emotion before the problem.

Module 3 · Resolving & Repairing

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Finding Shared Interests

Conflicts feel like opposing positions ("I want A, you want B") but underneath are usually compatible interests (we both want the project to succeed). Digging beneath positions to interests is where resolution lives.

Principle: argue interests, not positions. Beneath clashing wants, there's often a shared goal.

How
  • Ask "why does that matter to you?" to get from position to underlying interest.
  • Name the shared goal you both serve, out loud.
  • Look for options that satisfy both interests rather than splitting the difference.
Scenario
You want to ship now; they want to wait. Dig: you want momentum; they fear a quality issue. Shared interest: a successful launch. Option: ship to a small group now (momentum) while watching quality (their fear). Both interests met.
Drills

Starter: in one disagreement, ask "why does that matter to you?" to find the interest behind the position.

Stretch: find an option that serves both sides' interests, not a 50/50 compromise.

Common mistakes

• Arguing positions, not interests. • Splitting the difference when a both-win option exists. • Never naming the shared goal.

What good looks like: conflicts resolve because you find the shared interest beneath the clashing positions.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Apologizing Well

A real apology repairs trust; a fake one ("sorry you feel that way") deepens the damage. A good apology names the specific wrong, owns it without excuses, and commits to change.

Principle: own it fully — no "but," no "if." A real apology has no escape hatch.

How
  • Name what you did specifically — show you understand the actual harm.
  • Own it without excuses. No "but I was busy," no "if you were offended."
  • Acknowledge the impact on them.
  • Commit to change and, where possible, make it right.
Scripts
"I cut you off in the meeting and dismissed your idea. That was disrespectful and it shut you down. I'm sorry — I'll make sure you're heard next time."
Drills

Starter: draft an apology you owe, with no "but" or "if" anywhere in it.

Stretch: deliver it in person, naming the specific impact.

Common mistakes

• "Sorry you feel that way" (non-apology). • "Sorry, but…" excuses. • Apologising vaguely without naming the wrong.

What good looks like: your apologies actually repair trust, because they're specific, ownership-taking, and change-committing.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Saying No

Inability to say no leads to overcommitment, resentment, and dropped balls. A good no is clear, kind, and guilt-free — it protects your priorities without damaging the relationship.

Principle: "no" to one thing is "yes" to another. Decline the request, not the person.

How
  • Be clear and direct — a wishy-washy "maybe" is crueller than a clean no.
  • Give a brief honest reason, not a pile of over-justification.
  • Offer an alternative where you can: a later time, a partial yes, someone else.
  • Don't over-apologise — you're allowed to have priorities.
Scripts
"I can't take this on right now without dropping something that matters more — but I could help next week, or point you to someone who can."
Drills

Starter: say one clear, kind no this week instead of a reluctant yes.

Stretch: decline something with a brief reason + an alternative, and no over-apology.

Common mistakes

• Saying yes resentfully. • A vague "maybe" that strings them along. • Over-apologising and over-explaining.

What good looks like: you decline clearly and kindly, protect your priorities, and keep the relationship intact.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 · Finish↑

Repair & Follow-up

The conversation isn't the end — the repair is. After a hard talk, what you do next (follow through, check in, rebuild) is what actually mends the relationship and prevents the issue recurring.

Principle: a hard conversation is judged by what changes afterward, not by how it felt in the room.

How
  • Summarise agreements at the end so both leave with the same understanding.
  • Follow through visibly on whatever you committed to — actions rebuild trust faster than words.
  • Check in afterward: a short "how are we doing on this?" shows you care about the relationship, not just the issue.
  • Treat each hard conversation as a rep — note what worked, what to do better.
Scenario
You and a colleague worked through a tension. A week later: "Hey — I wanted to check, things feel better on my end, how about yours?" That small follow-up cements the repair and signals the relationship matters.
Drills

Starter: end your next hard talk by summarising what you both agreed.

Stretch: follow up a week after a difficult conversation to check the repair held.

Common mistakes

• No clear agreement at the end. • Not following through (worse than never talking). • Never checking back in.

What good looks like: hard conversations leave relationships stronger, because you follow through and circle back.
