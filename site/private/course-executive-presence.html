Course 05 · Executive Presence & Leadership Voice · 3 modules · 12 lessons

Executive Presence & Leadership Voice.

The way people communicate when others look to them to lead — calm, clear, credible. Presence isn't a title; it's a set of communicable behaviours. This course builds the gravitas, the messaging, and the listening of someone worth following.

Module 1 · The Presence

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

What Executive Presence Actually Is

Executive presence is the impression that you're calm, capable, and worth following — built from three things: gravitas (how you carry weight), communication (how you speak), and appearance (how you show up). It's earned and trainable, not innate charisma.

Principle: presence is "do I trust this person to handle it?" — answered before you've made your argument.

How
  • Audit the three pillars: gravitas (composure, decisiveness), communication (clarity, voice), appearance (fit-for-context).
  • Composure is the core. Staying calm under pressure signals capability more than anything you say.
  • It's contextual — presence in a startup ≠ presence in a boardroom; read the room (Conversation Day 24).
Drills

Starter: rate yourself 1–5 on gravitas, communication, appearance; pick the weakest to work on.

Stretch: in your next high-stakes moment, focus only on staying visibly composed.

Common mistakes

• Thinking presence is innate charisma. • Over-indexing on appearance, ignoring composure. • One-style-fits-all across contexts.

What good looks like: people instinctively look to you in uncertain moments — because you read as calm and capable.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Gravitas: Composure Under Pressure

Gravitas is the felt sense of substance and steadiness. Its engine is composure — the ability to stay calm, think clearly, and act decisively when things are hard or hostile. It's the single most visible marker of leadership.

Principle: the calmest person in the room is usually read as the most in control of it.

How
  • Slow down under pressure — pace, breath, movement. Rushing signals panic; deliberate calm signals control.
  • Pause before reacting to bad news or attacks (Conversation Day 17). The pause itself reads as gravitas.
  • Be decisive. Make the call, own it, and explain the reasoning — indecision erodes presence fast.
Scenario
A live problem blows up in a meeting. While others spin, you pause, lower your voice, and say "Okay. Here's what we know, here's what we do next." The calm decisiveness is gravitas in action.
Drills

Starter: in your next stressful moment, consciously slow your pace and breath.

Stretch: practise making one decision visibly and owning it, rather than deferring.

Common mistakes

• Visible panic/rushing. • Endless deliberation that reads as weakness. • Reacting hot to provocation.

What good looks like: under pressure you get calmer and clearer, and people steady themselves by watching you.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

Speaking With Authority

How you say it shapes whether it's taken seriously. Authoritative speech is clear, declarative, and free of the verbal tics that undercut you — the hedges, qualifiers, and upspeak that signal "don't take me too seriously."

Principle: cut the qualifiers. "We should do X" carries; "I just thought maybe we could possibly try X?" evaporates.

How
  • Trim hedges: "just," "kind of," "I think maybe," "does that make sense?" — say the thing plainly.
  • Use declarative statements for your core points; turn nervous questions into clear positions.
  • End sentences down, not up — upspeak turns statements into questions.
  • Speak in headlines first (see BLUF, Lesson 10), detail after.
Scenario
Recommending a direction. Weak: "So I was kind of thinking maybe we could possibly look at option B? If that's okay?" Authoritative: "I recommend option B. Here's why." Same idea, taken twice as seriously.
Drills

Starter: record yourself and count your hedge-words; cut them in the next conversation.

Stretch: state one recommendation as a plain declarative with no qualifiers.

Common mistakes

• Hedging everything. • Upspeak turning statements into questions. • Burying the point under preamble.

What good looks like: your words land with weight because they're clear, declarative, and qualifier-free.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

The Body & Voice of a Leader

Before you speak, your body and voice have already made a claim about your authority. Grounded posture, steady eye contact, an unhurried pace, and a resonant (not rushed, not thin) voice all signal "I belong here."

Principle: take up your space, calmly. Presence lives in stillness and steadiness, not in volume.

How
  • Ground your posture — feet planted, shoulders down, open chest (Public Speaking L10).
  • Steady eye contact with individuals; don't dart or stare at screens.
  • Slow your pace and lower your pitch slightly — both read as calm authority.
  • Use stillness — fidgeting leaks nerves; purposeful stillness projects control.
Drills

Starter: in your next meeting, sit/stand grounded and still, with steady eye contact.

Stretch: record a short talk; fix one body or voice habit that undercuts your authority.

Common mistakes

• Shrinking/closed posture. • Fidgeting. • Fast, thin, rushed voice.

What good looks like: you look and sound grounded and calm, so people read authority before you've said a word.

Module 2 · Communicating as a Leader

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

Communicating Up

Senior people are time-poor and decision-focused. Communicating up well means leading with the point, being brief, and making it easy for them to decide or act — the opposite of a long build-up to a buried ask.

Principle: give the headline and the ask first. Respect their time and they'll trust your judgement.

How
  • Lead with the bottom line (BLUF) — decision needed, status, or risk — then offer detail if wanted.
  • Bring solutions, not just problems — "here's the issue and here's my recommendation."
  • Be honest about risk — leaders trust those who flag bad news early.
  • Know the one thing you want from the interaction and get to it.
Scenario
Updating a busy director. Not: a chronological story. Instead: "We're on track to ship Friday. One risk: the vendor's slow. I'm handling it, but flagging in case it slips — I'll let you know by Wednesday." Headline, risk, ownership. Done.
Drills

Starter: start your next update to a senior person with the bottom line.

Stretch: bring a recommendation, not just a problem, to your next escalation.

Common mistakes

• Long build-up, buried point. • Dumping problems with no recommendation. • Hiding risk until it's too late.

What good looks like: senior people value your updates because they're brief, decision-ready, and honest.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

Communicating Down: Clarity & Vision

Leading a team through communication means giving people clarity (what, why, what's expected) and meaning (how it connects to something bigger). People follow direction they understand and believe in.

Principle: people need the "why" to give their best to the "what." Context is a leadership tool.

How
  • Be unambiguous about expectations — vague direction breeds anxiety and rework.
  • Always give the why. Connect tasks to the goal/mission so work has meaning.
  • Repeat the vision — leaders over-communicate the direction; people need to hear it many times.
  • Invite questions so understanding is real, not assumed.
Scenario
Assigning a grind of a task. Not just "do this by Friday." Instead: "This unblocks the launch the whole team's counting on — that's why it matters and why Friday. Anything in the way?" The why turns a chore into a contribution.
Drills

Starter: attach a clear "why" to your next request or assignment.

Stretch: restate your team's goal/vision in a meeting where it would normally go unsaid.

Common mistakes

• Vague expectations. • Tasks with no "why." • Assuming people remember the vision.

What good looks like: your team knows exactly what's expected and why it matters — and acts with ownership.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Influence Without Authority

Most things get done across teams you don't control. Leading "across" means influencing peers and other functions through credibility, relationships, and shared goals — not position power.

Principle: when you can't tell, you must sell. Influence across runs on trust and mutual benefit.

How
  • Build the relationship before you need it (reciprocity, Persuasion L5).
  • Frame asks around their goals (Persuasion L2), not just yours.
  • Be reliably credible — do what you say; your track record is your leverage.
  • Make it collaborative — "how do we both win here?" beats "do this for me."
Scenario
You need another team's help, no authority over them. "I know you're slammed — but this also fixes the thing that's been generating your support tickets. Can we tackle it together?" You framed it as their win, backed by your reliable reputation.
Drills

Starter: frame one cross-team ask around the other team's benefit.

Stretch: invest in one peer relationship before you need anything from it.

Common mistakes

• Relying on position you don't have. • Framing only around your needs. • Cold asks with no relationship.

What good looks like: you get things done across the org through trust and mutual benefit, no authority required.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

Communicating in a Crisis

In a crisis, people watch the leader for cues. Crisis communication is about calm, honesty, and frequency: acknowledge reality, say what you know and don't, give the next step, and keep updating.

Principle: in a crisis, your calm is contagious — and silence is the worst message of all.

How
  • Be visibly calm — your composure sets the room's temperature.
  • Be honest about what you know and don't. "Here's what we know, here's what we don't yet" beats false certainty.
  • Give the next concrete step — direction reduces panic.
  • Communicate often. Frequent short updates beat one perfect delayed one; silence breeds rumour.
Scenario
A production outage, everyone anxious. "Here's what's down, here's what we're doing, here's what I need from each of you, and I'll update every 30 minutes." Calm, honest, directive, frequent — the panic drops.
Drills

Starter: draft a crisis update template: what we know / don't / next step / next update.

Stretch: in a real stressful incident, commit to a regular update cadence.

Common mistakes

• Going silent to "wait for full info." • False certainty. • Panicking visibly.

What good looks like: in a crisis people calm down because you're calm, honest, and keep them informed.

Module 3 · Sustaining It

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Listening as a Leader

The higher you go, the less honest information reaches you — people tell leaders what they think leaders want to hear. Great leaders fight this with deliberate, visible listening that makes truth safe to share.

Principle: the more senior you are, the harder you must work to actually hear the truth.

How
  • Ask and genuinely listen (Conversation Day 2) — and don't shoot the messenger.
  • Speak last in meetings so your view doesn't anchor everyone and silence dissent.
  • Actively invite challenge: "What am I missing?" / "Who sees it differently?"
  • Reward honesty visibly so people keep bringing it.
Scenario
You have a strong opinion in a team decision. Instead of stating it first (and ending the discussion): "I want to hear everyone's read before I weigh in." You get the real picture, and people feel heard.
Drills

Starter: in one meeting, hold your opinion until everyone else has spoken.

Stretch: explicitly ask "what am I missing?" and reward whoever challenges you.

Common mistakes

• Speaking first and anchoring everyone. • Punishing bad news. • Assuming silence means agreement.

What good looks like: people tell you the truth — including hard truths — because you've made it safe and worthwhile.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Concise Messaging (BLUF)

BLUF — Bottom Line Up Front — is the leader's default structure: state the conclusion, decision, or ask first, then support it. It respects everyone's time and signals clear thinking.

Principle: lead with the answer, not the journey to it. Detail is available on request; the headline is mandatory.

How
  • First sentence = the point: the decision, recommendation, status, or ask.
  • Then layer support — context and detail for those who want it.
  • Cut ruthlessly. If a sentence doesn't serve the bottom line, drop it.
  • Apply it everywhere — emails, updates, meetings, hallway answers.
Scenario
Asked "where are we on the migration?" Not a five-minute history. BLUF: "On track for next Tuesday. One open risk I'm managing. Want the detail?" They get the answer instantly and ask for more only if they need it.
Drills

Starter: rewrite your next update so the first sentence is the bottom line.

Stretch: answer three questions today BLUF-first, detail-on-request.

Common mistakes

• Chronological build-up to a buried point. • Over-explaining. • Making people dig for the answer.

What good looks like: people always know your point in the first sentence — and trust you to be concise.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Presence on Video & Remote

Most leadership communication now happens through a webcam, where presence is harder: smaller signals, more distraction, easy to fade into a grid. Remote presence takes deliberate adjustment.

Principle: the camera flattens presence — so you dial everything up a notch to come through.

How
  • Look at the camera, not the faces, for "eye contact" that actually lands.
  • Frame and light yourself well — eye-level camera, light on your face; this is the "appearance" pillar online.
  • Energy up ~10% — the camera drains animation; a touch more makes you read as present, not flat.
  • Be deliberate with turns — name people to bring them in (Conversation Day 10), since natural cues are muted.
Drills

Starter: on your next call, look at the camera for key points and raise your energy slightly.

Stretch: fix your framing/lighting once, properly, so you always show up well.

Common mistakes

• Looking at faces, never the lens. • Flat low energy on camera. • Bad lighting/angle undercutting credibility.

What good looks like: you have the same presence on a call as in the room — engaged, well-framed, and clearly leading.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 · Finish↑

Building Your Reputation

Executive presence compounds into reputation — the story people tell about you when you're not in the room. It's built slowly through consistency: reliable delivery, calm under pressure, honesty, and treating people well, over and over.

Principle: reputation is presence, repeated. What you do consistently becomes who people believe you are.

How
  • Be consistent. Presence in one big moment is forgettable; the same behaviour for a year is a reputation.
  • Deliver reliably — competence is the foundation everything else sits on.
  • Be known for one thing — clarity, calm, follow-through — and reinforce it.
  • Keep developing — rotate a focus from this academy and stay a student of communication.
Drills

Starter: name the reputation you want in one phrase, and one behaviour that builds it.

Stretch: ask a trusted colleague how you actually come across, and close the gap.

Common mistakes

• Performing presence occasionally, not consistently. • Style over reliable substance. • Stopping growth once "senior."

What good looks like: people describe you, unprompted, as calm, clear, and worth following — that's earned presence.
