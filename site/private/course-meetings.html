Course 10 · Meetings & Facilitation · 3 modules · 12 lessons

Meetings & Facilitation.

Meetings are where work gets coordinated — or where time goes to die. This course makes you the person whose meetings people don't dread: tightly planned, well-facilitated, decision-driving, and followed through. It's the capstone where every academy skill converges.

Module 1 · Before the Meeting

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

Should This Even Be a Meeting?

The best meeting skill is often not having the meeting. Most recurring meetings persist by inertia, and many one-offs could be an email, a doc, or a quick async thread. Protecting people's time starts with this filter.

Principle: a meeting is for things that genuinely need real-time, multi-way discussion. Everything else is async.

How
  • Ask "what's the decision or discussion that needs us live?" If there isn't one, don't meet.
  • Default to async for status, FYIs, and simple approvals — a doc or message (Business Writing course).
  • Audit recurring meetings — kill or shorten ones that have lost their purpose.
Scenario
A weekly status meeting where everyone just reads updates aloud. Replace with a written async update (Business Writing L9); reclaim the live time for actual discussion only when there's something to decide. People silently thank you.
Drills

Starter: before scheduling your next meeting, ask if it could be async instead.

Stretch: cancel or shorten one recurring meeting that's lost its purpose.

Common mistakes

• Meeting out of habit. • Using meetings for one-way status. • Never auditing recurring ones.

What good looks like: the meetings you do hold are the ones that genuinely need to happen — and people's calendars breathe.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Agendas & Goals

A meeting without a clear goal and agenda drifts and overruns. The single highest-leverage prep is defining the one outcome the meeting must produce, and a short agenda that serves it — shared in advance.

Principle: every meeting needs a stated outcome. "We'll have decided X" beats "discuss X."

How
  • State the desired outcome up top: decision made, plan agreed, problem solved.
  • List a few agenda items as questions to answer, not vague topics, each timeboxed.
  • Share it in advance so people come prepared.
  • Keep it tight — fewer items, more focus.
Scenario
A vague "project sync." Reframed agenda: "Outcome: decide launch date. 1) Are the blockers cleared? (10m) 2) Go/no-go for the 14th? (15m) 3) Owners for remaining tasks (5m)." Now it's a machine for producing a decision.
Drills

Starter: write a one-line desired outcome for your next meeting before the agenda.

Stretch: turn a vague agenda into timeboxed questions-to-answer.

Common mistakes

• No goal, just a topic. • Vague agenda items. • Agenda shared only at the start (or never).

What good looks like: everyone arrives knowing the outcome the meeting must produce and what to prepare.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

Inviting the Right People

Too many attendees slows decisions and wastes time; too few and you can't decide or you'll re-litigate later. Thoughtful invites — only those who decide, contribute, or must be informed live — keep meetings sharp.

Principle: invite the fewest people who can achieve the outcome. Optional should mean optional.

How
  • Include deciders and essential contributors; make everyone else optional or just send notes.
  • Name roles — who decides, who advises, who's there for awareness.
  • Smaller is faster — for decisions, fewer voices reach a conclusion quicker.
Scenario
A decision meeting with 12 people. Trim to the 4 who actually decide and contribute; send the rest the notes. The decision that would have taken an hour with 12 takes 20 minutes with 4.
Drills

Starter: for your next meeting, cut the invite list to only those who must be there.

Stretch: label each attendee's role (decide/advise/inform) on the invite.

Common mistakes

• Inviting everyone "to be safe." • No clear roles. • Excluding a key decider (causing re-litigation).

What good looks like: the right small group is in the room, so the meeting moves fast and decides cleanly.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

Pre-reads & Preparation

Reading material aloud in a meeting wastes everyone's time. Sending a pre-read — and expecting it to be read — means the live time is spent on discussion and decision, not information transfer. It's a force-multiplier for meeting quality.

Principle: inform before the meeting, decide during it. Don't burn live time on reading.

How
  • Send context/data as a pre-read (well-written — Business Writing course) ahead of time.
  • Open with a few minutes of silent reading if you can't guarantee people read it (a known effective tactic).
  • Come prepared yourself — nothing kills a meeting like an unprepared organiser.
Scenario
A decision needs background everyone lacks. Send a one-page pre-read; open the meeting with 5 minutes of silent reading; then go straight to discussion. You've turned a 60-minute "let me walk you through the deck" into a 25-minute decision.
Drills

Starter: send a short pre-read before your next decision meeting.

Stretch: open a meeting with a few minutes of silent reading instead of a presentation.

Common mistakes

• Reading slides aloud. • No pre-read for complex decisions. • Organiser unprepared.

What good looks like: people arrive informed, so the meeting is all discussion and decision.

Module 2 · Running the Room

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

Opening & Setting Norms

The first two minutes set the tone. A strong open states the goal, the agenda, and how you'll work (norms) — so everyone's aligned on purpose and behaviour before the discussion starts. It signals "this meeting is run."

Principle: start by saying why you're here and how this will go. Clarity up front prevents drift.

How
  • Restate the outcome and agenda in the first minute.
  • Set light norms when useful — "let's hold questions for the end of each item," "phones away," "decision by 2pm."
  • Name the decision process — how will we decide (vote, owner decides, consensus)?
  • Start on time — it respects attendees and sets the standard.
Scenario
Kicking off a decision meeting. "We're here to decide the launch date by the end of this 30 minutes. Three items, I'll keep us to time, and Maya makes the final call. Sound good?" Everyone knows the goal, the time, and how it ends.
Drills

Starter: open your next meeting by restating the outcome + how you'll decide.

Stretch: set one explicit norm that prevents your usual meeting problem.

Common mistakes

• Drifting in with no clear open. • Unstated decision process. • Starting late.

What good looks like: your meetings start crisply with everyone aligned on goal, time, and process.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

Facilitating Discussion

Facilitation is steering the conversation without dominating it — keeping it on track, drawing out the best thinking, and moving it forward. The facilitator serves the discussion, not their own opinion.

Principle: facilitate, don't dominate. Your job is to make the group productive, not to win.

How
  • Ask good questions (Conversation Day 26) to open up thinking, then listen.
  • Keep it on track — gently park tangents: "Good point — let's note that for later and stay on the decision."
  • Summarise periodically — "So far we agree on X; the open question is Y" keeps everyone oriented.
  • Separate your facilitator hat from your opinion — if you must weigh in, flag it: "Taking my participant hat off for a sec…".
Scenario
Discussion spirals into a side debate. "That's a real issue — parking it on the list so we don't lose it. Back to the decision: where are we on the date?" You protected the goal without shutting anyone down.
Drills

Starter: in your next meeting, park one tangent and summarise progress once.

Stretch: facilitate a discussion where you hold your own opinion until the end.

Common mistakes

• Dominating with your own view. • Letting tangents run. • Never summarising, so the group loses the thread.

What good looks like: discussions stay focused and productive, and people feel heard — because you facilitated, not bulldozed.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Managing Dominators & Including the Quiet

In most meetings a couple of voices dominate and the rest go quiet — and the quiet ones often hold the insight you need. Good facilitation balances airtime: gently containing over-talkers and actively drawing out quieter people.

Principle: the loudest voice isn't the wisest. Your job is to surface all the thinking, not just the assertive thinking.

How
  • Contain dominators gracefully: "Thanks — let me get a few other views before we go deeper."
  • Invite the quiet by name (Conversation Day 10): "Sam, you've been close to this — what's your read?"
  • Use structure — round-robin, or written input first — to stop a few voices monopolising.
  • Protect dissent — actively welcome the differing view so it's safe to share.
Scenario
Two people have talked for ten minutes; others are silent. "Good — I want to make sure we hear everyone. Priya, Raj, what are you seeing that we haven't said?" The quiet ones surface the risk the loud ones missed.
Drills

Starter: in one meeting, deliberately bring in a quiet person by name.

Stretch: gracefully contain a dominator and redistribute airtime.

Common mistakes

• Letting a couple of voices dominate. • Mistaking silence for agreement. • Not protecting dissent.

What good looks like: airtime is balanced, quiet people contribute, and you get the full group's thinking.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

Driving to Decisions

The most common meeting failure is discussing without deciding — everyone leaves and the issue resurfaces next week. A good facilitator drives toward closure: forcing the decision, naming it clearly, or explicitly deferring with a reason.

Principle: a meeting's worth is measured in decisions made, not minutes talked.

How
  • Watch the clock against the goal and steer toward a decision before time runs out.
  • Force the close: "We've discussed enough — are we deciding A or B?"
  • Name the decision and the decider clearly; capture it.
  • If you can't decide, decide how/when you will — never just trail off.
Scenario
Ten minutes left, still circling. "Okay, we won't get more clarity by talking longer. Maya, your call — A or B?" A decision gets made. Without that push, the meeting would've ended in a vague "let's think about it" and repeated next week.
Drills

Starter: in your next meeting, explicitly force one decision before the end.

Stretch: when a decision can't be made, define exactly how and when it will be.

Common mistakes

• Discussing without deciding. • Vague "let's think about it" endings. • No named decider.

What good looks like: your meetings end with clear decisions (or a clear plan to decide), not open loops.

Module 3 · After & Beyond

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Capturing Actions

Decisions and discussions evaporate without a record. Capturing clear action items — who does what by when — is what turns a meeting from talk into outcomes. Vague "we should…" with no owner is how good intentions die.

Principle: every action needs an owner and a date. No owner = it won't happen.

How
  • Capture actions live — who, what, by when — as they're agreed, visible to all.
  • Confirm out loud at the end: "So: Sam owns X by Friday, Priya owns Y by Tuesday — right?"
  • Record decisions too, not just tasks, so they're not re-litigated.
  • Keep notes minimal — decisions + actions, not a transcript.
Scenario
Meeting ends with lots of "we should" statements. Before anyone leaves: "Let's lock actions — who owns what, by when?" Each gets a name and a date. Next week, they're actually done, because they were owned.
Drills

Starter: end your next meeting by confirming each action's owner and date aloud.

Stretch: capture actions live and visibly during the meeting.

Common mistakes

• "We should…" with no owner. • No date. • Transcribing everything instead of decisions + actions.

What good looks like: every meeting ends with a crisp list of owned, dated actions — and they get done.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Follow-through

The meeting's value is realised after it ends, in whether the actions actually happen. A quick written recap and light tracking turn agreements into delivered outcomes — and build your reputation as someone whose meetings produce results.

Principle: send the recap, track the actions. Follow-through is what makes meetings matter.

How
  • Send a short recap promptly (Business Writing) — decisions + actions + owners + dates.
  • Track actions to completion — a light check before the next meeting beats discovering nothing happened.
  • Open the next meeting with action review so accountability is visible.
Scenario
Right after the meeting, a five-line recap: "Decided: ship the 14th. Actions: Sam—blockers by Fri; Priya—comms by Tue. Next check: Monday." Everyone has the same record, and the actions are now trackable.
Drills

Starter: send a short recap within an hour of your next meeting.

Stretch: open your next recurring meeting by reviewing last time's actions.

Common mistakes

• No recap. • Actions never tracked. • Discovering at the next meeting that nothing happened.

What good looks like: your meetings reliably produce delivered outcomes, because you recap and follow through.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Remote & Hybrid Meetings

Remote and hybrid meetings are harder: easier to disengage, hybrid creates a two-tier dynamic where remote people get sidelined, and cues are muted. Good facilitation actively levels the playing field and keeps energy up (see Executive Presence L11).

Principle: in hybrid, work hard to include the remote people — they're the ones who get forgotten.

How
  • Actively bring in remote attendees by name — they can't jump into the cross-talk as easily.
  • Use the chat and shared docs for input so quieter/remote voices have a channel.
  • Level the field: in hybrid, consider everyone joining the call individually so no one's a second-class participant.
  • Keep it tighter — remote attention spans are shorter; energy and pace matter more.
Scenario
Hybrid meeting, room is dominating, remote folks silent. "Let me pause the room — I want to hear from the people on the call. Priya, Raj, what's your take?" You rescue the remote voices that were being talked over.
Drills

Starter: in your next remote/hybrid meeting, bring in each remote person by name.

Stretch: use a shared doc or chat to gather input so all voices have a channel.

Common mistakes

• Remote people sidelined in hybrid. • Long, low-energy remote meetings. • Ignoring the chat channel.

What good looks like: remote and in-room participants contribute equally, and the meeting stays tight and engaged.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 · Finish↑

Continuous Improvement & the Whole Academy

Great meeting-runners review and refine — occasionally asking "was that useful? what would make it better?" And meetings are where this entire academy converges: listening, questions, presence, persuasion, conflict, writing, storytelling, and facilitation all show up in a single well-run room.

Principle: a well-run meeting is communication mastery in miniature. Keep refining it — and keep refining all of it.

How
  • Occasionally ask for meeting feedback — "what would make these more useful?" — and act on it.
  • Cut what isn't working — shorten, merge, or kill meetings that don't earn their time.
  • Notice the academy in action — every course's skills appear in a good meeting; keep one in active practice.
You've reached the end of the academy. Ten courses, all built on one truth: communication is the highest-leverage professional skill, and every part of it is trainable. The path doesn't end here — pick one skill from any course and practise it this week, then another the next. Mastery is maintenance.
Courses: 01 Conversation · 02 Public Speaking · 03 Persuasion · 04 Difficult Conversations · 05 Executive Presence · 06 Business Writing · 07 Negotiation · 08 Storytelling · 09 Networking · 10 Meetings.
Drills

Starter: ask one meeting group what would make the meetings more useful.

Stretch: pick one skill from anywhere in the academy and run it for a week; then rotate.

Common mistakes

• Never seeking meeting feedback. • Treating any skill as "finished." • Letting good habits lapse.

What good looks like: your meetings keep getting better, and you keep one communication skill in active practice for life.
