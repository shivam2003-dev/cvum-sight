Course 07 · Negotiation · 3 modules · 12 lessons

Negotiation.

Salary, deals, scope, deadlines, who-does-what — you negotiate constantly, often without noticing. This course replaces "haggling" with a method: prepare hard, create value, claim your share, and keep the relationship intact for next time.

Module 1 · Preparation Wins

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

The Negotiation Mindset

Most people dread negotiation because they picture a zero-sum fight. The reframe that changes everything: most negotiations have room to create value, and the goal is a deal both sides are glad to keep — not crushing the other party.

Principle: negotiate to expand the pie first, then divide it. Win-win is usually possible and always more durable.

How
  • Drop the battle frame. See it as joint problem-solving toward a deal you both honour.
  • Separate people from the problem — be soft on the person, firm on the issue.
  • Aim for durable, not just favourable — a deal the other side resents won't hold.
Scenario
Negotiating project scope with another team. Battle: "We need all of it by Friday." Problem-solving: "What can we both live with — what's truly must-have vs nice-to-have for each of us?" The second finds a deal that actually sticks.
Drills

Starter: reframe your next negotiation as "how do we both win?" before it starts.

Stretch: in one negotiation, separate the relationship from the issue explicitly.

Common mistakes

• Treating every negotiation as a fight. • Attacking the person, not the problem. • Winning a deal the other side won't keep.

What good looks like: you reach deals both sides are happy to honour — and they want to deal with you again.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Know Your BATNA

Your BATNA — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement — is what you'll do if this deal falls through. It's the source of all your power: the better your alternative, the more confidently you can walk, and walking ability is leverage.

Principle: your power comes from your alternative, not your argument. Know your walk-away before you start.

How
  • Define your BATNA concretely before negotiating — what exactly happens if no deal?
  • Improve it if you can — a second offer, another option — to strengthen your hand.
  • Estimate their BATNA too — how badly do they need this deal?
  • Set your walk-away point (reservation value) from your BATNA, and hold it.
Scenario
Salary negotiation. With a competing offer in hand (strong BATNA), you negotiate calmly and can walk. Without one, you're negotiating from fear. The offer is leverage even if you never mention it.
Drills

Starter: write your BATNA and walk-away point before your next negotiation.

Stretch: actively improve your BATNA (line up an alternative) before a big negotiation.

Common mistakes

• Negotiating with no idea of your alternative. • Not improving a weak BATNA. • Bluffing a walk-away you can't take.

What good looks like: you negotiate from genuine confidence because you know exactly what you'll do if it fails.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

Understand Their Interests

Behind every stated position is a deeper interest. They say "we need a 20% discount" (position); they actually need to hit a budget number, or justify it internally (interest). Understanding interests unlocks creative deals positions can't.

Principle: positions are what they ask for; interests are why. Negotiate the why.

How
  • Ask "why" and "what matters most" to surface interests behind positions.
  • Look for differences in priority — what's cheap for you but valuable to them (and vice versa) is the raw material of a good deal.
  • Research before the table — know their constraints, pressures, and deadlines.
Scenario
A client pushes hard on price. Dig: they need to show savings this quarter. You can't drop price, but you can defer billing into this quarter — meets their real interest at no cost to your total. Positions clashed; interests didn't.
Drills

Starter: list the likely interests behind the other side's stated position.

Stretch: find one thing cheap for you but valuable to them to trade.

Common mistakes

• Negotiating positions head-on. • Assuming their interest is just price. • Not researching their constraints.

What good looks like: you uncover the real interests and craft deals that satisfy both, beyond the surface positions.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

Set Targets & Anchors

Going in without clear targets means drifting to whatever the other side proposes. Decide your aspiration (best realistic outcome), your target, and your walk-away — and plan your opening anchor deliberately.

Principle: aim high but justifiable. People who set ambitious targets achieve more — if they can back them up.

How
  • Set three numbers: aspiration (ambitious), target (realistic), reservation (walk-away).
  • Plan your opening anchor — ambitious but defensible, with a reason attached.
  • Justify your ask with objective criteria (market rate, comparable, data) so it's not arbitrary.
Scenario
Asking for a raise. Don't wing it: aspiration £X (top of market), target £Y, walk-away £Z. Open at a justified high number with market data, not a timid "whatever you think is fair." The anchor sets the range.
Drills

Starter: set your aspiration/target/reservation before your next negotiation.

Stretch: back your opening ask with one piece of objective criteria.

Common mistakes

• No targets, so you drift. • Anchoring too timidly. • An ambitious ask with no justification.

What good looks like: you enter with clear, ambitious, justified targets and steer toward them.

Module 2 · At the Table

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

Anchoring

The first number on the table pulls the whole negotiation toward it — that's the anchoring effect. A strong, justified opening anchor shifts the range in your favour; letting them anchor first cedes that ground.

Principle: a justified first offer shapes the deal. Anchor first when you have the information to do it well.

How
  • Open first when you're informed, with an ambitious-but-defensible number.
  • Always attach a reason to the anchor — a bare big number feels greedy; a justified one feels fair.
  • If they anchor low/high, don't counter near it — re-anchor with your own justified number to reset the range.
Scenario
They open with a lowball. Don't counter just above it (that accepts their range). Instead: "That's well below market — based on [data], the right range is X." You've re-anchored, and the midpoint moves toward you.
Drills

Starter: prepare a justified opening anchor (number + reason) for your next negotiation.

Stretch: practise re-anchoring when the other side opens extreme.

Common mistakes

• Letting them anchor and negotiating from it. • A bare number with no justification. • Countering right next to a bad anchor.

What good looks like: you set or reset the range with justified anchors, pulling the deal your way.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

Trading, Not Conceding

Giving things away for free trains the other side to push harder and shrinks your outcome. Instead, trade — every concession you make is matched by something you get. "If… then…" is the negotiator's core sentence.

Principle: never give, always trade. Every concession buys you a concession.

How
  • Use "if… then…": "If you can commit to a two-year term, then I can move on price."
  • Trade cheap-for-you, valuable-for-them items first (and ask for their cheap-for-them, valuable-for-you in return).
  • Make concessions slowly and shrink them — big early gives signal lots more to come.
Scenario
They want a faster deadline. Don't just say yes. Trade: "I can hit that date if we trim two of the lower-priority features. Deal?" You gave speed, got reduced scope — a trade, not a giveaway.
Drills

Starter: phrase your next concession as "if… then…".

Stretch: identify two low-cost-to-you items you can trade for things you value.

Common mistakes

• Conceding for free. • Big fast concessions. • Not asking for anything in return.

What good looks like: every concession you make wins you something back — the deal stays balanced.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Handling Pressure & Tactics

Some negotiators use pressure — deadlines, "take it or leave it," good-cop/bad-cop, flinching at your number. Recognising tactics for what they are lets you stay calm and not be stampeded into a bad deal.

Principle: name the tactic to yourself, and slow down. Pressure works only if you let it rush you.

How
  • Recognise common tactics: false deadlines, extreme anchors, manufactured outrage, "limited authority."
  • Don't react under pressure — "I need to think about that" is always available (Conversation pause).
  • Test deadlines/ultimatums — many are softer than presented.
  • Return to interests and criteria when things get manipulative.
Scenario
"This price is only good today." Instead of panicking: "If it's right for both of us, a day or two won't change that. I'd rather get it right." You've tested the false urgency without hostility.
Drills

Starter: next pressure tactic you face, silently name it and slow down before responding.

Stretch: calmly test one artificial deadline or ultimatum.

Common mistakes

• Reacting fast under pressure. • Believing every deadline. • Matching aggression with aggression.

What good looks like: tactics don't rattle you — you recognise them, stay calm, and steer back to substance.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

Silence & Patience

Silence is one of the most powerful and underused negotiation tools. After making an offer or hearing one, the urge to fill the quiet often leads to talking yourself down. The patient negotiator lets silence do the work.

Principle: after you state your number, stop talking. The next person to speak often concedes.

How
  • State your offer and go silent. Don't soften it, don't explain it away, don't fill the gap.
  • Use silence after their offer too — a thoughtful pause often prompts them to improve it.
  • Be patient overall — rushing favours the other side; time is usually on the side of whoever can wait.
Scenario
You ask for the raise number. Then: silence. The instinct is to add "but I understand if that's not possible…" — which negotiates against yourself. Instead you hold the quiet; they fill it, often by moving toward you.
Drills

Starter: make one ask today and stay completely silent after it.

Stretch: use a deliberate pause after the other side's offer to prompt improvement.

Common mistakes

• Talking after your own offer and weakening it. • Filling every silence. • Rushing to close.

What good looks like: you're comfortable with silence and let it pull concessions toward you.

Module 3 · Closing & Keeping the Relationship

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Creating Value (Expand the Pie)

Before fighting over slices, make the pie bigger. By combining different priorities and bringing in extra issues, you often find deals that give both sides more than splitting a fixed amount ever could.

Principle: trade across issues you value differently — that's how both sides win more.

How
  • Add issues to the table, not just price — timing, scope, terms, future work, referrals.
  • Trade on priority differences: give them what they value most (cheap for you) for what you value most (cheap for them).
  • Brainstorm options together before deciding — separate inventing from committing.
Scenario
Stuck £5k apart on a contract. Expand: you care about a long term; they care about cash now. Deal: their price, but a two-year commit and a deposit. Both got their priority — the pie grew instead of one side losing £5k.
Drills

Starter: list extra issues (beyond the obvious one) you could bring to the table.

Stretch: turn a stuck single-issue negotiation into a multi-issue trade.

Common mistakes

• Fighting over one number. • Treating the pie as fixed. • Committing before exploring options.

What good looks like: you find creative multi-issue deals that leave both sides better off.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Concessions & Endgame

How you give ground near the close shapes the final number. Concede in shrinking increments, signal you're near your limit, and use the structure of the endgame (splitting, final offers) deliberately rather than caving in relief.

Principle: shrinking concessions signal you're near the bottom. The pattern of your gives tells a story — control it.

How
  • Shrink each concession (e.g. £500 → £200 → £50) to signal you're reaching your limit.
  • Always get something back for late concessions (Lesson 6).
  • Use "final offer" honestly — only when it's true, or it costs you credibility.
  • Beware the relief-cave — the urge to give a final big chunk just to end the discomfort.
Scenario
Near close, they push for one more drop. "I've moved a lot already; I can do a final small adjustment if we sign today — that's my limit." Shrinking, conditional, and a credible close.
Drills

Starter: plan your concession sizes to shrink toward your limit.

Stretch: hold firm through the "relief-cave" urge at the end of a negotiation.

Common mistakes

• Equal or growing concessions (signals more to come). • Caving at the end from discomfort. • Crying "final offer" falsely.

What good looks like: your endgame is controlled — concessions shrink, each buys something, and you don't cave at the wire.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Closing the Deal

Deals can die at the finish from hesitation, ambiguity, or never actually asking to close. A clean close confirms agreement clearly, writes it down, and locks in next steps so nothing unravels afterward.

Principle: close explicitly and in writing. "I think we're agreed" isn't a deal until it's confirmed and recorded.

How
  • Summarise the agreement aloud: "So we've agreed X, Y, Z — yes?" to confirm shared understanding.
  • Ask for the close directly — "Shall we go ahead?" Many deals stall for lack of asking.
  • Put it in writing promptly — recap email/contract while it's fresh and warm.
  • Lock next steps and owners so momentum doesn't leak.
Scenario
You've reached verbal agreement. "Great — to confirm: price X, two-year term, start next month. I'll send a recap today. Sound right?" Confirmed, written, locked. No "wait, I thought…" next week.
Drills

Starter: end your next agreement by summarising terms and asking to confirm.

Stretch: send a written recap within an hour of any verbal deal.

Common mistakes

• Never explicitly asking to close. • Leaving terms verbal/ambiguous. • Delaying the written confirmation.

What good looks like: your deals close cleanly, confirmed and written, with no loose ends to unravel.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 · Finish↑

Preserving the Relationship

Most negotiations are with people you'll deal with again — colleagues, clients, partners. A deal won at the cost of the relationship is often a loss. The best negotiators leave the other side feeling respected and satisfied, banking goodwill for next time.

Principle: win the deal and the relationship. Reputation as fair-but-firm is a lifetime asset.

How
  • Let them win something visible — people need to feel they got a fair deal, not that they were beaten.
  • Be firm on substance, warm on the person throughout (Lesson 1).
  • Never gloat — even a great outcome should be framed as mutual.
  • Follow through impeccably on what you agreed — delivery is the real reputation-builder.
Drills

Starter: in your next deal, ensure the other side has a clear win to point to.

Stretch: after a negotiation, follow through flawlessly to bank the goodwill.

Common mistakes

• Winning at the relationship's expense. • Gloating. • Sloppy follow-through that sours the deal.

What good looks like: people want to negotiate with you again because you're fair, firm, and reliable — goodwill compounds.
