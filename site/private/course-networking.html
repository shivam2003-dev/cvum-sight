Networking & Relationships.

Your network shapes your opportunities more than almost anything else — and most people do it badly or not at all. This course reframes networking as building genuine relationships by giving, then makes it a sustainable system, not a cringe-inducing scramble at events.

Module 1 · Mindset & Meeting People

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

Networking Reframed

Most people hate "networking" because they picture it as using people — collecting contacts to extract favours. Reframe it: networking is building genuine relationships and being useful. Done that way it's not slimy, it's just being a good professional who knows people.

Principle: don't network to get — build relationships to give. The getting follows, over years.

How
  • Shift from "what can I get" to "who can I help" — it removes the ick and works better.
  • Play the long game — relationships pay off over years, not at the event.
  • Be genuinely interested in people (Conversation Day 16) — that's the whole foundation.
Scenario
At an industry meetup. Extractive: hand out cards, ask everyone for leads. Relational: have real conversations, learn what people are working on, offer one helpful intro. You leave with one real connection instead of ten dead cards.
Drills

Starter: at your next event, aim to help one person, not collect contacts.

Stretch: reconnect with someone purely to see how they're doing, no agenda.

Common mistakes

• Networking only when you need something. • Collecting contacts like trophies. • Treating people as means to an end.

What good looks like: you build real relationships and have a network of people who genuinely like and trust you.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Your Story & Intro

"What do you do?" is your most-asked networking question, and most answers are forgettable job titles. A crisp, human intro — what you do, who you help, jargon-free — makes you memorable and gives people a hook to engage.

Principle: describe the value you create, not your title. Make it repeatable by a stranger.

How
  • Lead with the problem you solve / value you create, not your role label.
  • Drop the jargon — a non-expert should understand and be able to repeat it.
  • Keep it one or two warm sentences, and leave a hook to ask more.
Scripts
"I help support teams stop drowning in repetitive tickets — basically I automate the boring stuff so they can do the human stuff."
Drills

Starter: write a one-sentence, jargon-free intro and test it on someone outside your field.

Stretch: craft two versions — one for peers, one for a general audience.

Common mistakes

• Reciting a job title. • Jargon nobody outside your team gets. • A monologue instead of a hook.

What good looks like: people instantly get what you do, find it interesting, and can repeat it to others.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

Where to Meet People

Networking isn't only conferences. The best relationships often form in lower-pressure, repeated-contact settings — communities, online spaces, shared work, alumni groups. Knowing where to invest beats forcing yourself to "work the room."

Principle: repeated, low-pressure contact builds relationships better than one-off events.

How
  • Favour recurring settings — communities, meetups you attend regularly, online groups — where you see people again.
  • Use shared activity — collaborating, volunteering, learning together — which builds bonds faster than small talk.
  • Go where your field gathers online and contribute, not just lurk.
  • Pick a few channels and show up consistently rather than spreading thin.
Scenario
You dread big conferences. Instead, you join a small monthly meetup in your niche. By month three, you actually know people — relationships built through repetition, no forced "working the room."
Drills

Starter: pick one recurring community or group to show up to consistently.

Stretch: contribute something (a comment, help, a post) in an online space your field uses.

Common mistakes

• Only "networking" at big one-off events. • Lurking online, never contributing. • Spreading across too many channels.

What good looks like: you have a few settings where people know you through repeated, natural contact.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

Starting Cold

Approaching strangers — or messaging someone you don't know — is the hardest part for most. The keys: a low-pressure opener (Conversation Day 1), genuine curiosity, and for cold messages, brevity plus a clear, easy-to-answer reason for reaching out.

Principle: make it easy and specific. A short, relevant, low-ask message beats a long generic one.

How
  • In person: use observation+question openers; lead with curiosity, not your pitch.
  • Cold message: keep it short, say why them specifically, and make the ask tiny (or none).
  • Reference something real — their work, a shared connection, a talk they gave.
  • Lower your own bar — aim for one warm exchange, not a relationship instantly (Conversation Day 25).
Scripts
"Hi — really liked your post on X. I'm working on something similar and your point about Y stuck with me. No ask, just wanted to say it landed."
Drills

Starter: send one short, specific cold message referencing something real about the person.

Stretch: start one in-person conversation with someone you don't know at an event.

Common mistakes

• Long generic cold messages. • Leading with a big ask. • Nothing specific to the person.

What good looks like: your cold outreach gets replies because it's short, specific, and low-pressure.

Module 2 · Building & Keeping

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

The Follow-up System

A connection that isn't followed up is a connection lost. The difference between people with strong networks and everyone else is usually a simple, consistent follow-up habit — not charisma (Conversation Day 20).

Principle: the relationship is made in the follow-up, not the first meeting.

How
  • Follow up within a day or two, referencing something specific you discussed.
  • Capture contacts + a note after meeting people (who they are, one detail).
  • Make it a light system — a simple list/CRM, not memory, so no one slips through.
  • Give in the follow-up — the article, intro, or resource you mentioned.
Scenario
You met three good people at an event. The next day: a short personal message to each referencing your chat, plus the link you promised one of them. You've just turned three handshakes into three real contacts — which 90% of attendees won't.
Drills

Starter: follow up with everyone worth keeping from your last event, with a specific reference.

Stretch: set up a simple contact list/note system you'll actually maintain.

Common mistakes

• Never following up. • Generic "nice to meet you." • Relying on memory and losing people.

What good looks like: you reliably convert first meetings into relationships through quick, specific follow-up.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

Staying Warm

Relationships decay without contact. Staying warm means light, periodic, no-agenda touchpoints that keep you present in people's minds — so that when an opportunity (theirs or yours) arises, the relationship is alive, not awkwardly resurrected.

Principle: don't only reach out when you need something. Keep relationships warm with no-ask contact.

How
  • Send no-agenda touches: a relevant article, congrats on news, a "thought of you when I saw this."
  • Engage with their work — comment on a post, celebrate a win, react genuinely.
  • Set light reminders to check in with key people every few months.
  • Be useful periodically, not just present — the occasional helpful thing keeps you valued.
Scenario
A contact you haven't spoken to in months posts a big win. A genuine "huge congrats — so well deserved!" keeps the relationship warm at zero cost. Compare to only messaging them when you suddenly need a referral.
Drills

Starter: send one no-agenda warm touch to someone today.

Stretch: set a recurring reminder to check in with your key contacts quarterly.

Common mistakes

• Only reaching out when you need something. • Letting relationships go cold then awkwardly reviving them. • Never engaging with people's work.

What good looks like: your key relationships stay warm, so help and opportunity flow naturally both ways.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Giving Value First

The strongest networkers are the most generous — they help, connect, and share before they ever ask. This reciprocity (Persuasion L5) builds deep goodwill and a reputation as someone worth knowing.

Principle: be a giver. Help freely and your network becomes a community that helps you back.

How
  • Make introductions — connecting two people who should know each other is high-value and costs you little.
  • Share knowledge and resources proactively when they'd help someone.
  • Offer specific help, not vague "let me know if I can help" — "I could intro you to X" lands.
  • Give without scorekeeping — generosity that's obviously transactional doesn't build trust.
Scenario
You know two people with a perfect overlap. You intro them with a warm note on why. Both remember you as the connector who made something good happen — that goodwill compounds for years.
Drills

Starter: make one useful introduction or share one resource this week, unprompted.

Stretch: offer one specific piece of help to a contact with no ask attached.

Common mistakes

• Always taking, never giving. • Vague "let me know if I can help." • Obvious quid-pro-quo.

What good looks like: you're known as generous and well-connected — and people go out of their way to help you.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

Asking for Help Well

Even great networkers need to ask — for advice, intros, opportunities. Done well, a good ask is specific, easy to say yes to, and respectful of the other person's time. Done badly, it's a vague burden that gets ignored.

Principle: make your ask small, specific, and easy to decline. Easy asks get said yes to.

How
  • Be specific: "Could you intro me to one person in X?" beats "can you help me find a job?"
  • Make it easy — do the work for them (draft the intro blurb they can forward).
  • Give an easy out — "no worries at all if not" lowers the pressure and ironically raises yeses.
  • Don't over-ask — match the size of the ask to the depth of the relationship.
Scripts
"Would you be open to a 15-min chat about how you moved into X? I've got three specific questions. Totally fine if you're too slammed."
Drills

Starter: turn a vague ask into a specific, easy-to-answer one.

Stretch: when asking for an intro, draft the forwardable blurb yourself.

Common mistakes

• Vague, huge asks. • Making them do all the work. • Over-asking on a thin relationship.

What good looks like: your asks are easy to grant, so people actually help you.

Module 3 · Growing the Network

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Building a Diverse Network

A network of people exactly like you is an echo chamber — and weaker for opportunities. The most valuable networks are diverse: different fields, levels, backgrounds, and crucially "weak ties" (acquaintances), who tend to bring the new information and opportunities your close circle can't.

Principle: weak ties open doors strong ties can't. Diversity of network beats density.

How
  • Cultivate weak ties — keep a wide, loose web of acquaintances warm, not just your inner circle.
  • Connect across fields and levels — different worlds bring different opportunities and perspectives.
  • Resist the comfort of sameness — deliberately meet people unlike you.
Scenario
A job opportunity appears. It comes not from a close friend (who knows the same things you do) but from a loose acquaintance in an adjacent field — exactly the "weak tie" effect. Breadth paid off.
Drills

Starter: reach out to one acquaintance (weak tie) you've lost touch with.

Stretch: deliberately build a connection in a field or level different from yours.

Common mistakes

• Only networking within your bubble. • Neglecting weak ties. • Avoiding people unlike you.

What good looks like: your network spans fields, levels, and many loose ties — a wide net that surfaces real opportunity.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Online & LinkedIn

Much of modern networking is online, where reach is huge but signal-to-noise is low. Standing out means contributing value publicly (not just consuming), engaging genuinely, and keeping a profile that makes a good impression when people check you out — which they will.

Principle: online, you build a network by being useful in public, not by mass-connecting.

How
  • Contribute, don't just consume — share what you learn, comment thoughtfully, help in public.
  • Keep your profile sharp — clear on what you do and value you create (Lesson 2); people check before replying.
  • Engage genuinely on others' posts (Conversation course) rather than spraying connection requests.
  • Personalise connection requests — a one-line reason beats the default blank.
Scenario
You want to be known in your niche. Instead of cold-adding 500 people, you post one genuinely useful thing a week and comment thoughtfully on others'. Over months, people start knowing your name — inbound, not outbound.
Drills

Starter: leave one thoughtful public comment or share one useful thing this week.

Stretch: sharpen your profile headline/summary to clearly convey your value.

Common mistakes

• Lurking, never contributing. • Mass connection requests. • A vague/empty profile.

What good looks like: people find and remember you online because you're visibly useful, with a clear profile.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Reconnecting

Everyone has dormant contacts — people they liked but lost touch with. Reconnecting feels awkward but rarely is; a warm, no-pressure message usually lands well, and dormant ties are a goldmine precisely because they bring fresh, non-overlapping networks.

Principle: it's never as awkward as you fear. A genuine "thinking of you" reopens most dormant ties.

How
  • Lead with warmth, not apology — skip "sorry I've been bad at keeping in touch," just reconnect warmly.
  • Reference the past genuinely and ask how they are now.
  • Don't immediately ask for something — reconnect first, help can come later.
  • Offer value if you can — a reason you thought of them.
Scripts
"Hey! You popped into my head — saw something about [topic you used to discuss]. How are things? Would love to catch up properly."
Drills

Starter: reconnect with one dormant contact this week with a warm, no-ask message.

Stretch: revive three dormant relationships over the month.

Common mistakes

• Over-apologising for the gap. • Reconnecting only to immediately ask a favour. • Never reaching back out from fear of awkwardness.

What good looks like: you comfortably revive dormant relationships, reopening valuable, fresh parts of your network.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 · Finish↑

The Long Game

Networking isn't a campaign you run when job-hunting — it's a lifelong practice of being a generous, connected professional. The relationships you tend for years, with no immediate payoff, are exactly the ones that change your trajectory when it matters.

Principle: plant relationships long before you need the shade. Consistency over years beats intensity for a week.

How
  • Network when you don't need anything — that's when it's most genuine and most effective.
  • Make it a light, sustainable habit — a few warm touches and one new connection a week beats a panicked blitz.
  • Be patient — the biggest returns come from relationships you invested in years earlier.
  • Keep giving — a reputation as a generous connector is a career-long asset.
Drills

Starter: set one small weekly networking habit (one warm touch + one new hello).

Stretch: invest in a relationship with zero immediate payoff, purely for the long game.

Common mistakes

• Only networking when job-hunting. • Intense bursts then silence. • Expecting immediate returns.

What good looks like: networking is a steady, genuine habit, and your relationships quietly open doors for years.
