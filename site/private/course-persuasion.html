Course 03 · Persuasion & Influence · 3 modules · 12 lessons

Persuasion & Influence.

Moving people to "yes" — ethically. Not tricks, but the timeless levers of how humans decide: credibility, emotion, logic, framing, and the principles that quietly shape every choice. Use them to make good ideas win.

Module 1 · Foundations

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

Ethos, Pathos, Logos

Aristotle nailed it 2,300 years ago: persuasion runs on three engines — your credibility (ethos), the audience's emotion (pathos), and logical argument (logos). Most people lean on one and ignore the others; the persuasive use all three.

Principle: people decide with emotion and justify with logic — but they only listen at all if they trust you first.

How
  • Ethos first. Establish why you're worth listening to — competence, track record, or simply being fair and prepared.
  • Pathos to move. Connect to what they feel and want; a story or stake makes them care.
  • Logos to justify. Give the rational reasons that let them defend the decision to themselves and others.
Scenario
Pitching a process change. Ethos: "I've run this in two teams." Pathos: "You're all drowning in this manual work." Logos: "It saves ~6 hours a week, here's the math." All three, and the room moves.
Drills

Starter: take an argument you're making and write one line each of ethos, pathos, logos.

Stretch: identify which engine you naturally over-rely on, and deliberately add the weakest one.

Common mistakes

• All logic, no emotion (correct but unmoving). • Emotion with no credibility. • Forgetting to earn trust before arguing.

What good looks like: your case is credible, emotionally resonant, and logically airtight — so it persuades on every level.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Know What Moves Them

You can't persuade a person you haven't understood. Persuasion starts with their motivations, fears, and constraints — not your argument. The same proposal needs a different angle for different people.

Principle: speak to their "why would I?", not your "why I want it."

How
  • Map their interests. What do they want, fear, get measured on, and worry others will think?
  • Find the overlap between what you want and what they want — persuasion is easiest there.
  • Tailor the frame to their motivation: same idea, pitched as risk-reduction to one, growth to another.
Scenario
Same tool, two stakeholders. To the CFO: "cuts cost and risk." To the head of growth: "ships features faster." You didn't change the tool — you changed which truth you led with.
Drills

Starter: before your next ask, write one sentence on what the other person most cares about.

Stretch: re-pitch one idea three ways for three different motivations.

Common mistakes

• Pitching your reasons, not theirs. • One frame for everyone. • Assuming their motivation instead of checking.

What good looks like: people feel your proposal serves their goals, because you led with the part that does.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

Framing

The same fact, framed differently, lands completely differently. "90% fat-free" vs "10% fat." "A small fee" vs "an investment." Framing isn't lying — it's choosing which true angle to emphasise.

Principle: you don't change the facts, you choose the frame — and the frame shapes the feeling.

How
  • Lead with gain or loss depending on the audience — people fear loss more than they value equivalent gain.
  • Anchor to the right comparison. "Cheaper than one bad hire" reframes a cost as a bargain.
  • Name it well. Words carry frames — "investment," "opportunity," "protection" do quiet work.
Scenario
Asking for budget. Weak: "It costs £20k." Framed: "For about the price of one month's downtime, we remove the risk for the whole year." Same number, reframed against the cost of not doing it.
Drills

Starter: reframe one cost you need to justify as a loss-avoided or a comparison.

Stretch: take a proposal and write a gain-frame and a loss-frame; pick the stronger for your audience.

Common mistakes

• Presenting raw facts with no frame. • Using a frame that feels manipulative/dishonest. • Anchoring to the wrong comparison.

What good looks like: your honest framing makes the right choice feel obviously worth it.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

The Power of "Because"

A classic study found people comply far more with a request when it's followed by a reason — even a weak one — simply because the word "because" signals there's a justification. Reasons lubricate "yes."

Principle: never make a bare ask. Always attach a "because" — a real reason the other person can accept.

How
  • Pair every request with a reason that matters to them, not just to you.
  • Make the reason concrete. "Because it'll save the team Friday overtime" beats "because it's better."
  • Lead with the reason when the ask is big — justify before you request.
Scripts
"Could we move the review to Thursday? Because it gives us a full day to fix anything that comes up." "I'd like to own this — because I've shipped the last two and can move fast."
Drills

Starter: attach a clear "because" to every request you make today.

Stretch: for one big ask, lead with the reason before the request.

Common mistakes

• Bare asks with no reason. • Self-serving reasons ("because I want to"). • Vague reasons that don't justify anything.

What good looks like: your requests come with reasons people can say yes to — so they do.

Module 2 · The Principles of Influence

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

Reciprocity

People feel a deep pull to return favours. Give first — genuinely and without keeping score — and goodwill, help, and yeses flow back over time. It's the oldest social contract there is.

Principle: give before you ask. Value offered first changes how every later request lands.

How
  • Lead with value — help, an intro, a resource, your time — before you need anything.
  • Make it genuine, not transactional. Obvious "I'll scratch yours" reciprocity feels cheap; sincere giving compounds.
  • Be specific and useful, not generically nice.
Scenario
You'll need a team's help next quarter. This quarter, you proactively unblock them on something hard. When you ask later, it's not a cold request — it's reciprocity.
Drills

Starter: give one genuine, useful thing to someone today with no ask attached.

Stretch: build a habit of helping before you need help, for a month.

Common mistakes

• Only reaching out when you need something. • Transparent quid-pro-quo. • Keeping a visible scorecard.

What good looks like: people want to help you because you've genuinely helped them first.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

Social Proof

When unsure, people look to what others — especially similar others — are doing. "Most teams like yours already do X" is more persuasive than any feature list. We're herd animals; use it honestly.

Principle: show, don't just tell, that others have chosen this — especially others like them.

How
  • Cite relevant peers. "Three teams in your division switched and saw…" beats abstract claims.
  • Use specifics — names, numbers, examples — not vague "everyone's doing it."
  • Reduce perceived risk: social proof reassures "you won't be the foolish first."
Scenario
Proposing a new practice. "I was skeptical too, but the two teams who tried it last quarter now won't go back — here's what changed for them." The proof does the persuading.
Drills

Starter: add one relevant example of others who've done what you're proposing.

Stretch: collect 2–3 concrete proof points before your next pitch.

Common mistakes

• Vague "everyone does it." • Irrelevant proof (peers they don't identify with). • Fabricated/exaggerated proof — destroys trust.

What good looks like: people feel reassured by credible examples of others like them succeeding with your idea.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Commitment & Consistency

People strive to act consistently with what they've already said or done. Get a small initial "yes" or a stated value, and larger aligned commitments follow more easily.

Principle: start with a small yes. Public, voluntary commitments pull future behaviour into line.

How
  • Get a small first step — a pilot, a trial, an agreement on the goal — before the big ask.
  • Surface shared values and link your proposal to them: "You've said you care about X — this is X."
  • Make commitments explicit (and ideally written) — people honour what they've stated.
Scenario
Want a team to adopt a big change. First get agreement on the problem ("we all agree the current way is painful?"). Once they've said yes to that, your solution is the consistent next step.
Drills

Starter: before a big ask, secure agreement on the underlying goal first.

Stretch: design a small low-risk first step that leads naturally to the bigger commitment.

Common mistakes

• Going for the big ask cold. • Forcing commitment (backfires). • Not linking to values they already hold.

What good looks like: your big asks feel like the natural continuation of small yeses people already gave.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

Scarcity, Authority & Liking

Three more of Cialdini's classic levers: we want what's scarce, we defer to credible authority, and we say yes to people we like. Used honestly, each makes a good case land harder.

Principle: rarity, credibility, and warmth all move people — but only stay persuasive when they're real.

How
  • Scarcity: highlight genuine limited time, slots, or the real cost of waiting — never fake urgency.
  • Authority: cite credible expertise (yours or others') and signal competence through preparation.
  • Liking: warmth, common ground, and genuine compliments (Conversation course) make people receptive.
Scenario
Real deadline approaching. "The vendor's price holds until month-end" (true scarcity) + "our security lead reviewed and approved it" (authority) + a warm, collaborative tone (liking). Honest, and far more persuasive than any one alone.
Drills

Starter: identify one genuine scarcity or authority signal you can honestly include.

Stretch: audit a recent pitch — which of the six principles did you use, which did you miss?

Common mistakes

• Fake urgency/scarcity (erodes trust fast). • Borrowed authority you can't back up. • Cold delivery that kills liking.

What good looks like: you deploy real scarcity, credible authority, and genuine warmth — ethically — and your case becomes hard to refuse.

Module 3 · In Practice

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Making the Ask

Many persuasive cases fail at the last step: the person never clearly asks for anything. A vague "so, thoughts?" leaves the decision adrift. The ask must be clear, specific, and easy to say yes to.

Principle: always close with a clear, specific ask. Make the "yes" the path of least resistance.

How
  • State exactly what you want — the decision, resource, or action — in one sentence.
  • Make yes easy. Lower the cost: a small first step, a default option, a ready-to-go plan.
  • Ask, then stop. After the ask, be silent and let them respond (don't talk yourself out of it).
Scripts
"So my ask is simple: approve a two-week pilot with one team. If it doesn't work, we stop."
Drills

Starter: end your next pitch with one clear, specific ask, then go silent.

Stretch: design the ask so the easy default is "yes" (small, reversible, low-risk).

Common mistakes

• Never actually asking. • A vague or huge ask. • Talking past the ask and diluting it.

What good looks like: people know exactly what you want and find it easy to agree.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Handling Objections

Objections aren't rejection — they're requests for reassurance. The persuasive person welcomes them, understands the real concern, and addresses it, rather than steamrolling or getting defensive.

Principle: an objection is a question in disguise. Answer the concern, not the surface words.

How
  • Welcome it. "Good — let's talk about that" beats getting defensive.
  • Find the real concern behind it: "What's the worry underneath?" Cost often masks risk or effort.
  • Acknowledge, then address. Validate (Conversation Day 31), then answer with evidence or a concession.
  • Pre-empt the big ones — raise and answer the obvious objection before they do.
Scenario
"It's too expensive." Dig: "Is it the absolute cost, or the risk it won't pay off?" If risk: "Then let's de-risk it — pilot first, scale only if it works." You addressed the real concern, not the price tag.
Drills

Starter: next objection you hear, ask one question to find the real concern before answering.

Stretch: list the top three objections to your idea and prepare an answer for each.

Common mistakes

• Getting defensive. • Answering the surface words, not the real worry. • Steamrolling objections instead of addressing them.

What good looks like: objections become the moment you win trust, because you take them seriously and resolve them.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Storytelling to Persuade

A statistic informs; a story convinces. Wrapping your case in a concrete human story makes it vivid, memorable, and emotionally persuasive in a way bullet points never are (see also the Storytelling course).

Principle: don't just argue the point — tell the story that makes the point inevitable.

How
  • Find the human in the data. One real person's experience beats an aggregate.
  • Use a before → turning point → after arc that mirrors the change you're proposing.
  • Land the point the story was built to prove.
Scenario
Arguing for a support tool. "Last month a customer waited three days for an answer we already had — they nearly churned. With this, that answer is instant." The story makes the cost of inaction real.
Drills

Starter: attach one concrete story to your next persuasive case.

Stretch: build your whole pitch around a single before/after story.

Common mistakes

• All data, no story. • A story with no clear point. • Generic hypotheticals instead of real specifics.

What good looks like: people remember and repeat your story — and it carries your argument with it.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 · Finish↑

Ethical Influence & Continuous Practice

Influence is power, and power can be misused. The line: persuasion helps people make a decision that's genuinely good for them; manipulation tricks them into one that isn't. Stay on the right side and your influence compounds; cross it and it collapses the moment it's noticed.

Principle: persuade toward what's true and good for them. Manipulation wins once; trust wins forever.

How
  • Test yourself: would they thank you if they knew exactly how you persuaded them? If not, don't.
  • Use the principles to clarify, not distort — real proof, honest scarcity, genuine reasons.
  • Play the long game. Reputation for honesty is the strongest persuasive asset there is.
  • Keep practising one lever at a time until they're second nature.
Drills

Starter: run your next pitch through the "would they thank me?" test.

Stretch: pick one influence principle to deliberately practise each week.

Common mistakes

• Winning short-term with manipulation. • Treating influence as tricks rather than clarity. • Damaging trust for one win.

What good looks like: you're known as persuasive and trustworthy — the combination that makes your good ideas reliably win.
