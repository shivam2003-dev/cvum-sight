Course 02 · Public Speaking & Presentations · 4 modules · 16 lessons

Public Speaking & Presentations.

The single most visible professional skill: standing up and making people care. This course takes you from stage fright to commanding a room — building the talk, crafting the content, delivering it with presence, and handling slides, Q&A, and the inevitable wobble. One lesson at a time; rehearse each out loud.

Module 1 · Foundations & Fear

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

Conquering Stage Fright

Almost everyone feels fear before speaking — it's not a flaw, it's biology: your body treats "all eyes on me" as a threat. The goal isn't to erase the nerves but to channel the adrenaline into energy and stop it hijacking your delivery.

Principle: you can't calm the adrenaline away — you reframe it. "I'm nervous" and "I'm excited" are the same physical state with a different label.

How
  • Reframe arousal as excitement. Literally tell yourself "I'm excited." It reroutes the same adrenaline toward performance instead of panic.
  • Prepare to over-confidence. Most fear is under-preparation in disguise. Knowing your first 60 seconds cold removes the scariest moment.
  • Slow your breath before you start. A few long exhales drop your heart rate and steady your voice.
  • Shift focus to service. "How do I help this audience?" crowds out "how do I look?"
Scenario
Heart pounding before you go on. Instead of fighting it: a slow exhale, "I'm excited to share this," and you launch into a first line you've rehearsed 20 times. The nerves are still there — but now they're fuel, and the rehearsed opening carries you past the worst moment.
Drills

Starter: next time you present (even to one person), do three slow exhales and silently relabel the nerves as excitement.

Stretch: rehearse your opening 60 seconds until you could deliver it half-asleep.

Common mistakes

• Trying to eliminate nerves entirely. • Winging the opening — the highest-fear moment. • Apologising for being nervous (it spotlights it).

What good looks like: you still feel the buzz, but it sharpens you rather than shaking you, and your opening is rock-solid.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Knowing Your Audience

A talk isn't about what you want to say — it's about what they need to hear. The same content, aimed wrong, falls flat. Audience analysis is the unglamorous prep that quietly decides whether you land.

Principle: speak to their problem, not your expertise. The audience silently asks "what's in this for me?" — answer it early.

How
  • Profile them before you write a word: who are they, what do they already know, what do they care about, what do they fear or want?
  • Pick the right level. Match jargon and depth to their expertise — too basic bores, too advanced loses.
  • Find the "so what." For every point, know why this audience should care. If you can't say, cut it.
Scenario
Same project, two audiences. To engineers: architecture and trade-offs. To executives: cost, risk, timeline, outcome. Reusing the engineer deck for the exec room is the classic way a good project gets a bad reception — the content was right, the audience fit was wrong.
Drills

Starter: for your next talk, write one sentence: "This audience cares most about ___."

Stretch: take an existing talk and re-pitch it for a completely different audience.

Common mistakes

• Presenting what you find interesting, not what they need. • Wrong level of jargon. • One deck for every room.

What good looks like: the audience feels the talk was made for them specifically — because it was.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

The One Core Message

If your audience remembers one thing tomorrow, what should it be? Most talks fail by trying to convey ten things and landing none. A single, sharp core message is the spine everything else hangs on.

Principle: one talk, one message. If you can't say it in a sentence, the audience can't carry it out the door.

How
  • Write your message in one sentence before building anything — the "if you forget everything else, remember this."
  • Test every section against it. Does this point support the core message? If not, cut or park it.
  • Repeat it. State it early, reinforce it in the middle, land it at the end. Repetition is how a message survives.
Scenario
You have 30 slides of findings. Core message: "We should pause this project for a month to fix the data pipeline — it'll save us a quarter later." Now every slide either supports that or gets cut. The talk goes from a data dump to a clear ask.
Drills

Starter: write the one-sentence core message for your next talk before any slides.

Stretch: cut one entire section that doesn't serve the core message.

Common mistakes

• Trying to say everything. • No clear takeaway. • A message so vague it means nothing ("data is important").

What good looks like: a week later, someone can still tell you your one point — in a sentence.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

Structuring a Talk

Structure is what lets an audience follow you without effort. A talk without structure feels like a list; a structured talk feels like a journey with a destination. The classic shapes exist because they work.

Principle: tell them where you're going, take them there, remind them where you've been. Structure carries meaning the words alone can't.

How
  • Use a simple arc: hook → context → 3 key points → call to action. Three is the magic number for memory.
  • Signpost. "There are three things…" and "now for the second…" let the audience track where they are.
  • Make transitions explicit so sections don't blur together.
  • End where you said you would — close the loop you opened in the hook.
Scenario
A meandering update. Restructured: "I'll cover where we are, the one risk I'm worried about, and what I need from you." The audience now knows the shape, can follow, and knows there's a decision coming — they listen differently.
Drills

Starter: outline your next talk as hook → 3 points → call to action on one index card.

Stretch: add explicit signposting and transitions, then deliver it without notes.

Common mistakes

• A flat list with no arc. • Too many points (5+). • No signposting, so the audience gets lost.

What good looks like: the audience always knows where they are and where you're taking them — it feels effortless to follow.

Module 2 · Crafting the Content

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

Strong Openings

The first 30 seconds decide whether the room leans in or reaches for their phones. A weak open ("So, um, today I'm going to talk about…") wastes your most valuable moment. A strong one earns attention you can spend for the rest of the talk.

Principle: open with a hook, not housekeeping. Earn attention before you explain the agenda.

How
  • Pick a hook: a surprising fact, a sharp question, a short vivid story, or a bold claim. Anything but "today I'll talk about."
  • Make it relevant to them (Lesson 2) — the hook should point straight at their problem.
  • Get to the point fast. Save the long intro; the audience grants attention to a strong start, not a warm-up.
Scripts
"Last quarter we lost £40,000 to a problem most of us didn't know existed." "What would you do with three extra hours a week? That's what this change gives you."
Scenario
A safety briefing nobody listens to. Weak: "Today's mandatory safety update covers…" Strong: "Two months ago this exact mistake cost a team three days of downtime. Here's how we make sure it never happens to us." Same content; the room is now awake.
Drills

Starter: rewrite your next talk's opening as a hook (fact, question, story, or claim).

Stretch: draft three different hooks for the same talk and pick the sharpest.

Common mistakes

• Opening with logistics/agenda. • A slow throat-clearing warm-up. • A hook unrelated to the audience's interest.

What good looks like: within 30 seconds the room is leaning in, curious where you're going.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

Storytelling on Stage

Stories are how humans remember. Wrap your point in a story and it lodges; deliver it as a bullet and it slides off. On stage, a well-placed story is also a breather — for you and the audience.

Principle: make your point, then prove it with a story — or tell the story and let it carry the point.

How
  • Use the arc (from Conversation Mastery Day 7): set-up → tension → resolution → point.
  • Make it concrete. A specific person, place, and moment beats an abstract example every time.
  • Land the point explicitly after the story so no one misses why you told it.
  • Keep one story per key point — enough to anchor it, not so many you ramble.
Scenario
Arguing for better onboarding. Data version: "Onboarding NPS is low." Story version: "Maya joined in March. By week two she still didn't know who to ask for help, and nearly quit. Here's what we changed —". The story makes the abstract problem impossible to ignore.
Drills

Starter: attach one concrete story to your talk's main point.

Stretch: open the whole talk with a story that sets up your core message.

Common mistakes

• Abstract examples with no human in them. • Story with no clear point. • Too many stories, no through-line.

What good looks like: people quote your story back to you weeks later — and remember the point because of it.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Making Data Memorable

Numbers persuade, but only if the audience can grasp them. A wall of stats numbs; a single number made vivid sticks. Your job is to translate data into meaning.

Principle: one number, made human, beats ten numbers on a slide.

How
  • Pick the one number that matters and build around it; bury the rest in an appendix.
  • Make it relatable. "Enough wasted hours to hire two more people" lands harder than "12,000 hours."
  • Use comparison. Anchor the number to something familiar — a percentage, a per-person figure, a "that's like…".
  • Round ruthlessly when speaking — "about a third" is more memorable than "31.7%."
Scenario
A slide of 15 metrics. Replace with: "One number matters today — our churn doubled. That's roughly one in eight customers gone, every month. Here's why." The single vivid figure does what the table couldn't.
Drills

Starter: take your key statistic and rewrite it as a relatable comparison.

Stretch: cut a data-heavy slide down to a single number plus its meaning.

Common mistakes

• Dumping every metric. • Precise numbers nobody can hold in their head. • Stats with no "so what."

What good looks like: the audience remembers your one number — and what it means — long after the talk.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

Powerful Closings

People remember beginnings and endings most. A talk that fizzles out ("so, yeah, that's it, any questions?") wastes the moment of maximum impact. A strong close lands your message and tells people exactly what to do next.

Principle: never end on "that's all." End on the message and the ask.

How
  • Restate the core message (Lesson 3) in fresh words — the one thing to remember.
  • Make a clear call to action. What should they do, decide, or feel now? Be specific.
  • Close the loop from your opening hook for a satisfying full-circle feel.
  • End on a strong line and stop. Don't trail off or tack on "um, yeah."
Scenario
You opened with the £40k loss. Close: "We lost £40,000 to something invisible. With one week of work now, we make sure it stays in the past. I'm asking for that week — who's in?" Full-circle, clear ask, strong final line.
Drills

Starter: write a closing that restates your message + one specific call to action.

Stretch: craft a close that loops back to your opening hook.

Common mistakes

• Ending on "that's it, questions?" • No call to action. • Trailing off weakly after your real ending.

What good looks like: the talk ends on a line that lands, and everyone knows exactly what to do next.

Module 3 · Delivery

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Voice & Vocal Variety

A monotone kills the best content; a varied, expressive voice keeps a room alert. Your voice is an instrument with four dials — pace, pitch, volume, and pause — and most people leave all four stuck in the middle.

Principle: variety holds attention. Change at least one vocal dial every time the idea changes.

How
  • Vary pace. Slow down for the important line; speed up through the familiar. Sameness is what sends people to sleep.
  • Use pitch and emphasis to mark the words that matter — flat delivery flattens meaning.
  • Drop volume for impact, not just raise it. A quiet, deliberate line can be the loudest moment in the room.
  • Pause around key points (Conversation Day 17) — silence makes words land.
Scenario
Delivering your core message. Instead of saying it at the same pace as everything else: slow right down, drop your volume slightly, pause before the key word — "This… changes… everything." The vocal shift alone signals "this matters," and the room locks in.
Drills

Starter: record yourself reading your key paragraph; vary pace and add two pauses; compare.

Stretch: deliver your core message three ways and pick the most powerful.

Common mistakes

• Monotone. • Rushing the important lines. • Only ever getting louder for emphasis.

What good looks like: your voice rises and falls with the meaning, and the room stays awake and tracking.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Body Language & Stage Presence

Before you say a word, your body has spoken. Presence — how you stand, move, and use the space — signals confidence and credibility, and it can be built deliberately even when you feel shaky inside.

Principle: be still with purpose, move with purpose. Random fidgeting reads as nerves; grounded stillness reads as authority.

How
  • Plant your feet. A stable base stops the nervous sway and grounds you.
  • Move deliberately, to mark transitions or step toward the audience for a key point — not to pace anxiously.
  • Open gestures, hands visible and used to illustrate; avoid crossed arms or pockets.
  • Make eye contact with individuals across the room, holding each for a moment, rather than scanning or staring at slides.
Scenario
You catch yourself pacing and gripping the clicker. Reset: plant both feet, lower your shoulders, rest your free hand open, and look at one real person as you make your next point. The audience instantly reads you as more in control — and you start to feel it too.
Drills

Starter: deliver a point with feet planted and one deliberate gesture, no pacing.

Stretch: film a two-minute talk; watch with sound off and fix one distracting habit.

Common mistakes

• Anxious pacing/swaying. • Hands in pockets or clenched. • Talking to the slides, not the people.

What good looks like: you look grounded and open, your movement has meaning, and you connect with individuals in the room.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Pacing & Pauses

Nervous speakers rush — and rushing buries the message and signals anxiety. Deliberate pacing, with real pauses, gives the audience time to absorb and gives you time to think. The pause is the most underused tool in speaking.

Principle: silence is not dead air — it's emphasis. The confident speaker is comfortable letting a point land.

How
  • Pause after a key point, not just between sentences — let it sink in before moving on.
  • Slow your overall pace below what feels natural under adrenaline; nerves speed you up, so consciously counter it.
  • Use a pause to recover — lost your thread? A calm two-second pause looks deliberate, not panicked.
  • Let questions breathe. Ask a rhetorical question, then actually pause before answering.
Scenario
You hit your big reveal. Rushed: you blurt it and barrel on. Paced: you say it, then stop — two full seconds of silence while it lands — then continue. The pause makes the moment feel important, because it is.
Drills

Starter: insert two deliberate two-second pauses into your next talk after key points.

Stretch: deliver a talk consciously 20% slower than feels natural; notice the difference.

Common mistakes

• Racing through from nerves. • Filling every gap with "um." • Never pausing, so nothing lands.

What good looks like: your pacing feels calm and deliberate, and your pauses make key moments feel weighty.

Module 3 · Lesson 12↑

Managing Notes & Nerves Mid-Talk

You don't need to memorise a talk word-for-word — that's brittle and sounds robotic. You need a light scaffold you can glance at, and a plan for when your mind blanks (it happens to everyone). Knowing you have a safety net frees you to be present.

Principle: know your structure, not your script. Memorise the map, improvise the streets.

How
  • Use keyword notes, not full sentences — a few words per section as a trigger, on a card or speaker view.
  • Memorise only the opening and closing word-for-word; improvise the connective middle from your structure.
  • Have a recovery move for a blank: pause, glance at your note, or buy time with "Let me put that another way." No one notices a calm recovery.
  • Trust your prep. You know more than the audience — a blank feels huge to you and is invisible to them.
Scenario
Mid-talk, your mind goes blank. Panic spiral makes it worse. Instead: a calm pause, a glance at your keyword card, "So — the key thing here is…" and you're back. The audience saw a thoughtful speaker, not a stumble.
Drills

Starter: condense your next talk's notes to keywords only, one line per section.

Stretch: deliberately practise recovering from a "blank" using a pause + recovery phrase.

Common mistakes

• Reading a full script word-for-word. • Memorising everything (brittle). • Visibly panicking at a blank.

What good looks like: you speak naturally from a light scaffold, and recover from any wobble so smoothly no one notices.

Module 4 · Slides, Q&A & Mastery

Module 4 · Lesson 13↑

Designing Slides That Help

Slides should support you, not replace you. The most common failure is the "wall of text" slide the speaker reads aloud — at which point the audience either reads ahead or tunes out. Good slides are visual, sparse, and reinforce the spoken word.

Principle: the slide is the backdrop; you are the talk. If the audience is reading, they're not listening.

How
  • One idea per slide. If a slide has two messages, it should be two slides.
  • Few words, big visuals. A single image, chart, or short phrase beats a paragraph. Never read bullets aloud.
  • Make text readable from the back — large font, high contrast.
  • Use a blank/black slide when you want all attention on you, not the screen.
Scenario
A slide with eight bullet points. Fix: split into key phrases across a few slides, or replace with one strong chart and say the rest. The audience now watches you and grasps the point, instead of speed-reading bullets while you talk over them.
Drills

Starter: take your busiest slide and cut it to one idea + one visual.

Stretch: redesign a whole deck so no slide has more than six words (where possible).

Common mistakes

• Walls of text read aloud. • Multiple ideas per slide. • Tiny unreadable fonts/charts.

What good looks like: slides reinforce your words at a glance, and the audience watches you, not the screen.

Module 4 · Lesson 14↑

Handling Q&A

Q&A is where credibility is won or lost — it's unscripted, so it shows whether you really know your stuff and how you handle pressure. Done well, it's a chance to reinforce your message; done badly, it undoes the talk.

Principle: listen to the whole question, answer briefly, bridge back to your message. You control the answer, not just react to the question.

How
  • Listen fully before answering; don't start formulating mid-question. Repeat/rephrase it so everyone heard it and you understood.
  • Answer concisely, then stop. Rambling signals uncertainty.
  • Bridge back: "…which connects to the main point — that…". Use answers to reinforce your message.
  • It's fine to say "I don't know." "Great question — I'll find out and follow up" beats bluffing, which destroys trust.
  • Defuse hostile questions by staying calm, finding the legitimate part, and answering that.
Scenario
A pointed question you don't fully know. Bluffing: a vague waffle everyone sees through. Honest + strong: "I don't have that number to hand — I'll get it to you by tomorrow. What I can say is…" and you bridge to a point you're sure of. Credibility intact, even enhanced.
Drills

Starter: before your next talk, list the three hardest questions and prep crisp answers.

Stretch: practise the "I don't know, here's what I do know, I'll follow up" move out loud.

Common mistakes

• Answering before hearing the full question. • Rambling. • Bluffing instead of admitting a gap. • Getting defensive with hostile questions.

What good looks like: Q&A makes you look more credible — calm, concise, honest, and always circling back to your message.

Module 4 · Lesson 15↑

Recovering from Mistakes

Tech fails, you lose your place, a demo breaks, you misspeak. What separates polished speakers isn't avoiding mistakes — it's recovering from them without losing the room. The audience takes its cue from you: panic, and they panic; stay calm, and they barely notice.

Principle: the audience mirrors your reaction. A composed recovery makes a mistake a non-event.

How
  • Stay calm and acknowledge lightly if needed — a brief smile or "let's try that again" beats visible panic.
  • Have a backup for tech. Know your key points well enough to carry on if slides die; keep a non-digital fallback for demos.
  • Don't over-apologise. One light acknowledgement, then move on — dwelling magnifies it.
  • Use humour gently to defuse, never self-flagellation.
Scenario
The projector dies mid-talk. Panic: you freeze and apologise repeatedly. Pro: "Well — slides are taking a break, so you've just got me. Here's the part that matters anyway…" and you carry on from memory. The room relaxes because you did.
Drills

Starter: rehearse delivering your core 2 minutes with no slides at all.

Stretch: plan one calm recovery line you can use for any stumble ("let me restart that thought").

Common mistakes

• Visible panic. • Over-apologising. • No fallback when tech fails.

What good looks like: mistakes barely register because you handle them with calm and even a light touch of humour.

Module 4 · Lesson 16 · Finish↑

Rehearsal & Continuous Improvement

Great speakers aren't born fluent — they rehearse, and they review. The talk that looks effortless was practised out loud several times, and the speaker who keeps improving treats every talk as a rep to learn from. This is where the whole course becomes a habit.

Principle: rehearse out loud, not in your head — and review every real talk for one lesson.

How
  • Rehearse aloud, standing up, at least twice — silent reading hides the rough edges your mouth will find.
  • Time it and cut to fit; overrunning is a classic, avoidable failure.
  • Record yourself at least once; watching it back reveals habits you can't feel.
  • Review after every talk: one thing that worked, one to improve next time. Keep a running note.
  • Seek slightly bigger stages over time — more people, higher stakes — to keep growing.
Scenario
Big talk next week. Instead of just polishing slides: stand up and run it aloud twice, time it, trim 3 minutes, record the third run and watch it back. On the day it feels familiar because you've already "given" it — and afterward you note one thing to improve for next time.
Drills

Starter: rehearse your next talk out loud, standing, at least twice.

Stretch: record one talk, watch it back, and write down one strength and one fix.

Common mistakes

• "Rehearsing" only in your head. • Never timing it. • Never reviewing, so the same flaws repeat.

What good looks like: your talks look effortless because they're well-rehearsed, and each one is a little better than the last.
