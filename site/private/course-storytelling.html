Course 08 · Storytelling for Work · 3 modules · 12 lessons

Storytelling for Work.

Facts inform; stories move. This course teaches the practical craft of business storytelling — turning data, ideas, and proposals into narratives people remember and act on, for pitches, updates, and influence.

Module 1 · Why & What

Module 1 · Lesson 1↑

Why Stories Beat Facts

People forget statistics and remember stories. A narrative gives facts a shape, an emotion, and a reason to care — which is why the same point, told as a story, lands and lasts where a bullet list slides off.

Principle: facts tell, stories sell. Wrap the point you want remembered inside a story.

How
  • Use a story to make a point land, not to decorate — the story must carry the message.
  • Lead with people. A specific human in a situation beats an abstract metric.
  • Pair story + data: the story makes them feel it, the data proves it's real.
Scenario
Justifying a fix. Data alone: "error rate is 4%." Story + data: "A customer hit that error mid-checkout and gave up — multiply by our 4% and that's hundreds of lost sales a week." Now they care.
Drills

Starter: take one fact you need to land and attach a one-line human story to it.

Stretch: open your next update or pitch with a story instead of a stat.

Common mistakes

• All data, no narrative. • Stories that don't carry the point. • Abstract examples with no human.

What good looks like: people remember and repeat your point because it came wrapped in a story.

Module 1 · Lesson 2↑

Core Story Structures

You don't need to invent structure — a few reliable shapes carry almost any work story. Knowing them means you can build a compelling narrative on demand instead of rambling.

Principle: structure is what turns events into a story. Pick a shape and pour your content into it.

How
  • Situation → Complication → Resolution: the workhorse — things were fine, a problem hit, here's how it resolved (or should).
  • Before → After → Bridge: here's the painful now, here's the better future, here's how we get there. Great for proposals.
  • Hero's journey (lite): someone faced a challenge, struggled, learned, changed — great for personal/team stories.
Scenario
Pitching a change. Before/After/Bridge: "Today the team loses Fridays to manual reports [before]. Imagine reports generating themselves [after]. This tool is the bridge." Clean, persuasive shape.
Drills

Starter: retell a recent work event in Situation-Complication-Resolution.

Stretch: structure your next proposal as Before/After/Bridge.

Common mistakes

• Rambling with no shape. • Forcing a structure that doesn't fit. • Skipping the complication (no tension = no story).

What good looks like: you can shape any work message into a clear, compelling structure on the fly.

Module 1 · Lesson 3↑

Know Your Point

A story without a point is just an anecdote that wastes everyone's time. Before telling any work story, know the single message it must land — and cut anything that doesn't serve it.

Principle: one story, one point. If you can't name the point, don't tell the story.

How
  • Write the point in one sentence before building the story.
  • Cut anything off-point — tangents dilute the message and lose the room.
  • State the point at the end (or top) so no one can miss why you told it.
Scenario
A war story about a tough launch. Point: "we ship faster when we cut scope early." Every detail either supports that lesson or gets cut — and you land the lesson explicitly: "the takeaway: cut scope sooner."
Drills

Starter: before telling a work story, write its one-sentence point.

Stretch: cut a favourite tangent from a story to keep it on-point.

Common mistakes

• Stories with no point. • Tangents that dilute it. • Leaving the point implicit and unsaid.

What good looks like: every story you tell at work earns its time by landing one clear point.

Module 1 · Lesson 4↑

Character & Stakes

Stories work because we care about someone facing something. In business that someone is usually a customer, a teammate, or the audience themselves — and the stakes are what they stand to gain or lose. No character and stakes, no engagement.

Principle: give the audience someone to root for and something at risk. That's what makes them lean in.

How
  • Pick a relatable character — a real customer, a named teammate, "you."
  • Make the stakes clear and concrete — what happens if it goes wrong / right?
  • Create a little tension — the problem must matter, or the resolution won't.
Scenario
Selling a reliability project. Character: an on-call engineer. Stakes: woken at 3am every week by the same failure. Tension: burnout, attrition risk. Now the "fix the flaky service" proposal has a human and stakes — it's a story, not a ticket.
Drills

Starter: add a specific character and clear stakes to a story you tell often.

Stretch: make the audience themselves the character ("imagine you're the one who…").

Common mistakes

• No character, just abstractions. • Vague stakes. • No tension, so no one cares about the resolution.

What good looks like: your audience roots for someone and feels the stakes — so they're invested in your point.

Module 2 · Building the Story

Module 2 · Lesson 5↑

The Hook

The opening line decides whether people listen. A strong hook — a surprising fact, a sharp question, a tense moment — earns attention; a slow "so, basically…" loses it (see Public Speaking L5).

Principle: open in the action or the tension, not the throat-clearing.

How
  • Start in the middle of the moment: "It was 2am and the dashboards were all red."
  • Or open with a provocative question/fact tied to your point.
  • Skip the warm-up — get to something interesting in the first sentence.
Scenario
Telling a turnaround story. Weak: "So this project started last year and basically…" Hook: "Six months ago this product was three weeks from being killed." Everyone leans in to find out what happened.
Drills

Starter: rewrite a story's opening to start in the tension or with a hook.

Stretch: draft three different hooks for the same story; pick the sharpest.

Common mistakes

• Slow chronological build-up. • Burying the interesting bit. • A hook unrelated to the point.

What good looks like: your first line makes people want the next one.

Module 2 · Lesson 6↑

Tension & Arc

Tension is the engine of a story — the gap between how things are and how they should be. Without a complication or obstacle, there's nothing to resolve, and attention dies. The arc rises through the tension to a resolution.

Principle: no tension, no story. The problem is what makes the resolution worth hearing.

How
  • Name the obstacle clearly — what was hard, what was at risk, what almost went wrong.
  • Let it build a little before resolving — don't fix it in the next sentence.
  • Resolve cleanly and tie the resolution to your point.
Scenario
A success story told flat: "We launched and it went well." With arc: "Two days before launch, the main feature broke. We had a choice: slip the date or cut it. We cut it, shipped lean — and it outperformed the original plan." The tension makes the success mean something.
Drills

Starter: add a clear obstacle/tension to a story that currently has none.

Stretch: tell a story where you let the tension build before the resolution.

Common mistakes

• Conflict-free stories (boring). • Resolving instantly. • Tension unrelated to the point.

What good looks like: your stories have a real obstacle that makes the resolution land with weight.

Module 2 · Lesson 7↑

Data as Story

Numbers persuade, but only when given meaning and narrative. Turning data into story means choosing the number that matters, giving it a human face, and framing it as a change over time or a stake — not a table to be read (see Public Speaking L7).

Principle: don't present data — narrate it. One number, made meaningful, beats a dashboard.

How
  • Find the headline number and build the story around it.
  • Give it a trajectory: "it was X, now it's Y, and if we don't act it'll be Z."
  • Anchor it to something human or familiar so it's felt, not just read.
Scenario
A metrics review. Not a wall of charts. Instead: "Churn doubled this quarter — that's one in eight customers walking out the door every month, and the trend is accelerating. Here's the story behind it." The data became a narrative with stakes.
Drills

Starter: turn one key metric into a one-line story with trajectory and meaning.

Stretch: replace a data-heavy slide with a single number framed as a story.

Common mistakes

• Data dumps. • Numbers with no trajectory or meaning. • Too many figures, no narrative.

What good looks like: your data tells a clear story people grasp and remember.

Module 2 · Lesson 8↑

The Resolution & Call to Action

A work story exists to drive a decision or change. The ending must resolve the tension and point to what happens next — the action you want from the audience. A story that ends with "anyway…" wastes its build-up.

Principle: end on the point and the ask. The resolution should hand the audience their next step.

How
  • Resolve the tension you built — show how it turned out or could.
  • Land the lesson explicitly — what this means.
  • Make the ask — the decision, support, or action you want now (Persuasion L9).
Scenario
After the on-call burnout story. Resolution + CTA: "We fixed one flaky service and the 3am pages stopped. There are three more like it. I'm asking for two weeks to fix them — before we lose someone good." Story → lesson → ask.
Drills

Starter: end your next story with the lesson + a clear ask.

Stretch: rework a story that "just ends" so it drives a specific action.

Common mistakes

• Trailing off with no resolution. • No explicit lesson. • No ask, so nothing happens.

What good looks like: your stories end by making the next step feel obvious and urgent.

Module 3 · Telling It

Module 3 · Lesson 9↑

Storytelling in Pitches

A pitch is a story with a deal at the end. The winning pattern frames a problem the audience feels, shows a better world, and positions your idea as the bridge — wrapped in a narrative that makes the logic feel inevitable.

Principle: pitch the transformation, not the features. People buy the better world, then justify with specifics.

How
  • Open with the problem as a story the audience recognises.
  • Paint the "after" — the better world your idea creates.
  • Position your solution as the bridge, then back it with proof.
  • End on a clear ask (Persuasion).
Scenario
Pitching a new tool to leadership. Problem story (the team drowning) → after (effortless, automated) → bridge (the tool) → proof (pilot data) → ask (approve rollout). The narrative carries the decision.
Drills

Starter: structure your next pitch as problem-story → after → bridge → ask.

Stretch: rewrite a feature-led pitch as a transformation story.

Common mistakes

• Leading with features, not the problem. • No vision of the "after." • Pitch with no clear ask.

What good looks like: your pitches feel inevitable — the audience wants the after and sees your idea as the way there.

Module 3 · Lesson 10↑

Stories in Updates & Meetings

You don't need a stage to use storytelling — a 30-second story in a status update or meeting makes your point land where a dry summary wouldn't. The skill is the micro-story: tight, relevant, and pointed.

Principle: a 20-second story beats a paragraph of summary. Keep a few ready.

How
  • Use micro-stories — one customer, one moment — to illustrate a status or risk.
  • Keep them tight (under 30 seconds) and tied to the meeting's point.
  • Lead a recurring update with one concrete example rather than only aggregates.
Scenario
Weekly update. Instead of "support load is up": "A customer waited 40 minutes for a chat reply yesterday and tweeted about it. Support volume's up 30% — that's the story behind the number." The micro-story makes the metric real.
Drills

Starter: add one 20-second concrete story to your next update.

Stretch: keep a running note of 3 short work stories ready to deploy.

Common mistakes

• Only aggregates, never examples. • Stories too long for the setting. • Stories not tied to the point.

What good looks like: your updates and points land because you illustrate them with quick, relevant stories.

Module 3 · Lesson 11↑

Personal & Origin Stories

"Tell me about yourself," "why this company," "what drives you" — these moments (interviews, intros, networking) call for a crafted personal story, not a résumé recital. A good origin story is memorable, authentic, and points at where you're going.

Principle: don't list what you've done — narrate why and where you're headed.

How
  • Find the through-line — the thread connecting your path, even if it wasn't linear.
  • Use a turning point — the moment something clicked or changed direction.
  • Connect past → present → future so it points forward, not just back.
  • Keep it authentic (Conversation Day 37) — real beats polished-but-hollow.
Scenario
"Tell me about yourself" in an interview. Not a CV read-out. Instead: "I started in X, but a project where I [turning point] made me realise I loved Y — which is exactly what this role is, and where I want to go deeper." A story with a direction.
Drills

Starter: draft your 60-second origin story with a through-line and a turning point.

Stretch: tailor it to point at a specific role or goal.

Common mistakes

• Reciting a résumé. • No through-line or turning point. • Polished but inauthentic.

What good looks like: people remember your story and understand not just what you've done but who you are and where you're going.

Module 3 · Lesson 12 · Finish↑

Build a Story Library

Great storytellers don't improvise from scratch every time — they have a mental library of refined stories ready for the moment. Collecting, shaping, and practising your stories turns storytelling from a scramble into a reliable tool.

Principle: collect and rehearse your stories in advance. The "spontaneous" perfect story was prepared.

How
  • Collect raw material — note moments worth telling as they happen (wins, failures, customer stories).
  • Shape each into a tight arc with a clear point (Modules 1–2).
  • Tag them by point — "resilience," "why cut scope," "customer pain" — so you can pull the right one fast.
  • Rehearse aloud and refine with each telling (Conversation Day 7).
Drills

Starter: write down three work stories worth keeping, each with its point.

Stretch: build a tagged library of 5–7 ready stories and rehearse them.

Common mistakes

• Improvising every time. • Letting good stories slip by uncaptured. • Never refining them.

What good looks like: you always have the right story ready — because you collected and practised it ahead of time.
